Week 1 · Mechanisms

How many ways can it move, and how many motors does it need?

A mechanism is a set of rigid links joined by joints. Its degrees of freedom (DoF, also called mobility) say how many numbers fix its configuration, and so the minimum number of actuators needed to control it. The Grübler–Kutzbach (G–K) equation counts the DoF from the number of links and joints alone. Master that count, and know when it lies.

Intuition first

DoF = how many knobs

A coin slid on a table needs three knobs: left–right, forward–back, spin. Pin a stick on a nail and one knob is enough: its angle. DoF counts the independent knobs, nothing else.

Joints take freedom away

Two loose coins have 3 + 3 = 6 knobs. Push a drawing pin through both: the second coin can only spin about the pin, so 3 + 1 = 4 knobs remain. The pin removed 2.

G–K is an allowance sheet

Every moving link starts with D freedoms. Every joint confiscates D, then hands back the F it allows. Ground never spends. What is left is the mobility M.

The third rope that adds nothing

Hang a plank from two equal parallel ropes: it swings, one knob. A third identical rope between the same bars restricts nothing new, but the formula charges for it and says 0. The count assumes every constraint is a fresh one.

Reach vs dexterity

You can touch a spot on a wall (reachable). You can touch it with your palm facing up, down, left and right (dexterous). Everything dexterous is reachable; not the reverse.

1 · Kinematics, mechanisms and kinematic chains pp. 18–26

Picture

Kinematics is the geometry of motion with no forces (p.18). Dynamics is the forces and torques that cause motion, split into statics (no acceleration) and kinetics (acceleration) (p.19). The notebook warns that these words are used loosely in other languages and that the course sticks to this split.

A mechanism is “a mechanical system that transfers/modifies motion and/or forces from one or more sources to one or more outputs” (p.21). A kinematic chain is “a system of rigid bodies connected by joints” (p.22). Parts: links (rigid), joints (allow relative motion), an end effector (touches the world), a base (rigidly fixed to the ground) (p.23).

Classifying chains

Closed chainforms a closed loop (p.25)gripper linkage, delta robot (p.24)
Open chainnot closedan industrial arm
Serial chainopen chain in which every link except the first and last connects to two other linksan industrial arm (p.24)
Parallel mechanism addedthe end effector is joined to the base through several chains at once, so there are closed loops. The slides show pictures only, no definitionABB FlexPicker delta; Stewart platform

Why use closed loops (p.26): the actuators can sit on the base, so the moving parts are lighter; the load capacity is larger (several legs share it); and loops give interesting motion paths (Strandbeest, Cyber Tiger).

Worked example

A 6-joint industrial arm: open and serial. A delta robot: closed loops, parallel. A four-bar linkage: closed, and no link is a “first” or “last”, so it is not serial. Motor placement is the usual exam justification: a delta’s three motors sit on the base, so the light rods are all that move quickly.

Definition questions here are easy marks lost by a swapped word: kinematics (no forces) against kinetics (forces with acceleration); statics against kinetics. A scenario question asking “why a parallel robot for a fast pick-and-place line” wants: base-mounted actuators, low moving mass, larger load.

2 · Degrees of freedom and joints pp. 27–36

Picture

DoF = “number of independent parameters that define the configuration of a mechanism” (p.27). Link lengths are taken as known; the DoF do not depend on the lengths (notebook). Examples (pp. 28–33): a point in 2D has 2 DoF; a pendulum in 2D has 1 (x and y are tied together by the fixed length, so one angle is enough); a rigid body in 3D has 6 (three translations, three rotations); a rigid body in 2D has 3 (two translations, one rotation; notebook Q1).

Joint types (p.34). The last two columns are added: the constraints a joint removes are \(D - F\).

JointSymbolDoF \(F\)Removes in 3D (\(6-F\))Removes in 2D (\(3-F\))

Helical (screw)H15spatial only

The slides list no planar joint, so planar mechanisms in this unit use only R and P.

Derivation

A free rigid body has \(D\) DoF (\(D = 3\) planar, \(D = 6\) spatial). Two free bodies have \(2D\). A joint with \(F\) DoF between them leaves \(F\) relative freedoms, so it removes \(D - F\) of the \(D\) relative freedoms. For the planar revolute pair: \(6 - (3-1) = 4\) (p.36). checked

Typical short question: “how many DoF does a rigid body have in 2D?” (3, not 2: a point has 2, but a body also has orientation), “a rigid body in 3D?” (6), “the DoF of a spherical joint?” (3), “a helical joint?” (1: rotation and translation are locked together by the pitch). Do not confuse the DoF of a joint with the mobility of the mechanism.

3 · Controllable versus total DoF pp. 37–38

Picture

A DoF is controllable if an actuator drives it (a motor on a joint). Unactuated DoF are uncontrollable (p.37). Compare the number of actuators with the total DoF:

Controllable vs totalName on the slide
equalfully actuated
lessunder-actuated: fewer actuators than joints; low cost, simpler design, lower weight (p.38)
more“redundant”
The “redundant” wording

Slide 37 reads, word for word: “Controllable DOF > Total DOF, the robot is called redundant”. Notebook cell 8 adds “An example of this is a 7DOF robot arm in 3D space”. Taken literally these disagree. A serial arm with 7 revolute joints has mobility 7 by G–K, and with 7 motors its controllable DoF equal its total DoF, so by the wording it would be fully actuated.

The standard meaning added is kinematic redundancy: the arm has more joint DoF than the task needs. A 3D end-effector pose has 6 numbers, so a 7-joint arm is redundant (7 > 6), and so is a planar 3R arm used only to position a point (3 > 2). Redundancy gives a family of joint configurations for the same tool pose (the usual use: avoiding obstacles and joint limits).

What to write in the exam: for “fully / under / redundant” compare actuators with the DoF of the mechanism as the slide does; if the question names a 7-DoF arm as redundant, answer “redundant: more joint DoF than the 6-DoF task” and say that you are using this meaning. checked

Worked example

A 6R arm with 6 motors: fully actuated. The same arm with 4 motors: under-actuated (two joints free-swinging). A Stewart platform (M = 6, section 5) with 6 linear actuators: fully actuated. A delta robot with 3 motors and true M = 3: fully actuated, whereas the plain G–K value 9 would wrongly suggest under-actuated. checked

Slides vs notebook

The definition on p.37 and the “7DOF arm” example (notebook cell 8) do not match unless “total DOF” means the task’s pose DoF (6). Use the standard meaning above for redundancy; for under-actuated and fully actuated the slide’s comparison with the mechanism’s DoF is the one to quote.

A scenario question may ask “why build an under-actuated robot?” Answer from p.38: fewer actuators means lower cost, simpler design and lower weight, at the price that not every DoF can be commanded.

4 · Mobility and the Grübler–Kutzbach equation pp. 39–47

Picture

Mobility is the DoF that remain after all joints have applied their constraints. It is the commonly used word for a mechanism, and the “minimum number of actuators you need” (p.39). The equation does bookkeeping: start with every moving link free, subtract what each joint takes, add back what each joint allows. It uses only the topology (how many links, which joints), never the lengths.

Derivation

Form 2 (p.41), the one used in the W13 sample questions:

\[ M = D\,(N-1) - D\,J + \sum_{i=1}^{J}F_i \;=\; D\,(N-J-1)+\sum_{i=1}^{J}F_i \]

\(M\) mobility; \(D = 3\) (planar) or \(6\) (spatial); \(N\) number of links including the grounded link; \(J\) number of joints; \(F_i\) DoF of joint \(i\). The \(N-1\) moving links have \(D(N-1)\) DoF if free; each joint removes \(D\) and returns \(F_i\).

Form 1 (p.40) collects the joints by their DoF, \(P_i\) = number of joints with \(i\) DoF. A joint with \(i\) DoF removes \(D-i\) (section 2):

\[ M_{3D} = 6(N-1) - 5P_1 - 4P_2 - 3P_3 - 2P_4 - P_5, \qquad M_{2D} = 3(N-1) - 2P_1 - P_2 \]

The two forms are the same sum grouped differently: \(\sum_i (D-F_i) = \sum_i (D-i)\,P_i\). checked Both forms agree on every count on this page.

Counting links. Ground is link 0 and counts. A prismatic joint joins two parts that slide on each other (cylinder and rod); these are two links. Several ground pivots are still one link (the ground).

Exam recipe
  1. Decide D: planar drawing with joint axes all perpendicular to the page gives D = 3. Anything with S, U, C, or a note that it is “still 3D” gives D = 6.
  2. Number the links, ground = 0. Split every prismatic joint into its two links.
  3. List every joint with its F (R, P = 1; C, U = 2; S = 3). Count J and \(\sum F_i\).
  4. Substitute into \(M = D(N-J-1)+\sum F_i\) and show the line.
  5. Cross-check with form 1 if time allows.
  6. Sanity checks: a serial chain has \(N = J+1\), so \(M = \sum F_i\); M should be at least 1 for a moving machine; if M is 0 or negative, ask whether constraints are dependent (section 6).
Worked examples from the slides (all planar, D = 3)

Planar serial RPR (pp. 42–43): links 0–3, so \(N = 4\); three 1-DoF joints, \(P_1 = 3\), \(P_2 = 0\).
Form 1: \(M = 3(4-1) - 2\times3 - 1\times0 = 3\). Form 2: \(M = 3(4-3-1) + (1+1+1) = 3\). checked (A serial chain: M = sum of joint DoF.)

Planar four-bar (pp. 44–45, notebook Q2): ground plus three moving bars, \(N = 4\); four revolutes, \(P_1 = 4\).
Form 1: \(M = 3(4-1) - 2\times4 - 0 = 1\). Form 2: \(M = 3(4-4-1) + 4 = 1\). checked One motor on the crank drives the whole linkage.

Planar parallel manipulator (pp. 46–47, notebook Q3; the same figure is W13 Q6): a triangular platform on three R–P–R legs. Links: ground, platform, and \(3\times2\) leg parts (each prismatic joint has a cylinder and a rod), so \(N = 8\). Joints: \(3\times3 = 9\), all 1 DoF.

Two extra planar counts added that exam questions like: five-bar (N = 5, five R): \(3(5-5-1)+5 = 2\); slider-crank (N = 4, three R and one P): \(3(4-4-1)+4 = 1\). checked Both are presets in the widget above.

Slides vs notebook

Notebook cell 9 introduces the equation for “simple serial” kinematic chains, yet the slides and notebook then apply it to the closed four-bar and the parallel manipulator, and the answers (1 and 3) are the standard ones. Treat G–K as valid for any rigid-body mechanism whose constraints are independent (the real condition, section 6).

The classic traps are all in the bookkeeping: N without ground (3-RPR gives 0 instead of 3), \(D=6\) on a planar drawing (four-bar gives −2), swapping “constraints removed” with “DoF allowed” (four-bar gives 5), and counting each prismatic joint as one link. 5 · Spatial mechanisms: Stewart platform and the delta p. 48 · W13 pp. 14–17

Picture

The Stewart platform (p.48) is a base and a platform joined by six identical legs. Each leg is ground–U–P–S–platform: a universal joint at the base, a prismatic actuator, a spherical joint at the platform. The slide shows one leg and asks “What is the mobility of this system?” as homework. It gives no answer, so the count below is added.

The delta (W13 Q7, p.14) has a base, a platform and three legs. Each leg is a revolute joint at the base driving an upper arm, then a parallelogram of two rods with a spherical joint at each of its four ends.

Derivation

Stewart platform, D = 6. Links: ground, platform, and \(6\times2\) leg parts (lower cylinder, upper rod), so \(N = 14\). Joints: six U (2 DoF), six P (1), six S (3), so \(J = 18\) and \(\sum F_i = 6(2+1+3) = 36\):

\[ M = 6\,(14-18-1) + 36 = -30 + 36 = 6 \]

Six DoF, driven by the six prismatic actuators: fully actuated, and the platform can take any 3D pose. checked The one leg drawn on p.48 has \(N=4\), \(J=3\): \(M = 6(4-3-1) + 6 = 6\), the same number because a serial chain has \(M=\sum F_i\) (here \(2+1+3\)). checked

Why the U matters added: with an S at both ends of each leg (six SPS), \(\sum F_i = 42\) and \(M = 12\). The extra 6 are the legs spinning about their own axes, which move nothing. The U at the base blocks that spin, so the UPS count of 6 is the real one. checked

Delta, D = 6 (W13 p.14: “still 3D despite this depiction”). Links: base, platform, and per leg one upper arm plus two rods: \(N = 2 + 3\times3 = 11\). Joints: per leg 1 R and 4 S, so \(J = 3\times5 = 15\), \(\sum F_i = (1 + 4\times3)\times3 = 39\) (W13 p.15):

\[ M = 6\,(11-15-1) + 39 = 9 \quad\text{(plain G–K)} \]

The delta’s platform really has 3 DoF (it translates in x, y, z and stays parallel to the base). The six rods each spin freely about their own axis, since a rod has a ball joint at both ends. These six spins are local mobility \(R_M = 6\) and have no effect on the platform. The extended equation (W13 p.16) subtracts them, with \(R_C = 0\) over-constraints (p.17):

\[ M = D(N-J-1) + \sum F_i + R_C - R_M = 6(11-15-1) + 39 + 0 - 6 = 3 \]

checked Week 4 derives \(R_C\) and \(R_M\) in full. For now: plain G–K is a first estimate; local spins add fake DoF (subtract \(R_M\)) and dependent constraints hide real ones (add \(R_C\)).

Exam recipe (W13 Q7 style)
  1. Write D = 6 (state why: spherical joints, “still 3D”).
  2. List the links by name (base, platform, each leg’s parts) and sum to N.
  3. List the joints by leg and sum J and \(\sum F_i\). Each S is 3.
  4. Give the plain answer (9). Then say: six rods spin about their own axes, so \(R_M=6\); with \(R_C = 0\) the extended equation gives 3.
  5. Final check: a delta robot has 3 motors and moves a point in x, y, z, consistent with 3.
A short-answer version expects you to say whether 9 or 3 is wanted. The trap is stopping at the plain-G–K value. If the question only quotes the plain equation, 9 is the answer it wants; if it quotes the extended equation or asks for the platform’s real DoF, it is 3. Read which equation is given.

6 · Where G–K fails pp. 49–51

Picture

Slide 49 shows a body hung from the ground by three legs, each with an R at the ground and an R at the body (the notebook’s Q4 uses the same figure). The count gives 0, “but the system can move: it has 1 degree of freedom!”. The slide’s reason: the equation “assumes that the constraints on motion are independent”; one of the middle links can be removed without changing what the mechanism does, so it is redundant “and should not be counted”.

Counting it, and why it miscounts

Links: ground, three legs, the body: \(N = 5\). Joints: six R, \(P_1 = 6\), \(P_2 = 0\) (p.49):

\[ M = 3(5-1) - 2\times6 - 1\times0 = 0, \qquad\text{form 2: } 3(5-6-1)+6 = 0 \]

Counted 0, real 1. Why, in terms of the geometry added: the unknowns are the three leg angles and the body’s \(x, y, \theta\) (6 numbers); each leg gives two closure equations (6 equations). G–K treats the 6 equations as independent, hence \(6-6 = 0\). But when the three legs are equal in length and parallel, and the body’s pivots are spaced exactly like the ground pivots, the third leg’s two equations are implied by the first two (they say the same thing again). Only 5 of the 6 are independent, leaving 1 free motion. The remedy on the slide, deleting one middle leg, gives \(N=4,\ J=4\): \(M = 3(4-1) - 2\times4 = 1\), the parallelogram four-bar, which is correct. checked The constraint Jacobian has rank 5 at the parallel geometry (so 1 DoF) and the whole family closes exactly; with unequal legs, 20 random assemblies all give rank 6 (0 DoF).

So the miscount is a property of the special geometry, not of the topology: change one leg length slightly and the structure locks, and then G–K’s 0 is right. G–K only sees N, J and F, never the lengths, so it cannot tell the two cases apart. W13 p.16 names the correction: over-constraints \(R_C\) are added back (Week 4).

Hoberman mechanism (p.51): the slide gives only “24 links and 36 revolute joints”, no calculation. Putting those into G–K (my arithmetic, taking one link as the frame, added) gives \(3(24-36-1)+36 = -3\) for D = 3 and \(6(24-36-1)+36 = -42\) for D = 6. Both are negative, yet the object expands and contracts: the same lesson, G–K is not a proof that nothing moves. checked

Exam recipe: “Did you notice anything odd?”
  1. Do the count and show it (M = 0).
  2. State the observation: the mechanism visibly moves (1 DoF).
  3. Give the reason in the slide’s words: G–K assumes the constraints are independent; here one leg is redundant.
  4. Show the fix: remove one leg, recount, N = 4, J = 4, M = 1.
  5. Add the geometry caveat: it works because the legs are equal and parallel.
Absolute-word traps live here: “M = 0 means the mechanism cannot move” (false, p.49) and “G–K always gives the true mobility” (false). The true statement: M from G–K is exact only if the constraints are independent; otherwise it can undercount (p.49) or, with local motions, overcount (the delta).

7 · Workspace: reachable and dexterous pp. 52–57

Picture

The workspace is the set of points the end effector can reach. Its size and shape come from (1) the geometry (link lengths) and (2) the constraints on the joints (limits on angles or strokes) (p.52).

Reachable workspace: points the end effector can reach in one orientation. Dexterous workspace: points it can reach in all possible orientations. So \(W_{Dexterous}\subseteq W_{Reachable}\) (p.57; notebook Q7 answer \(W_R \supseteq W_D\)). The inclusion is non-strict: the sets can be equal in a degenerate case, so “\(\subset\)” is a trap.

The four arm types on the slides, with the shapes shown in the pictures (pp. 53–56; figures by A. Becker et al., “Robot Manipulator Workspaces”). Joint-limit cut-outs are visible in the slide images; the shape descriptions are added from reading those images.

ArmJointsWorkspace shape (as drawn)
PUMA (p.53)RRRthick spherical shell around the shoulder, with a wedge cut out by joint limits
SCARA (p.54)RRPa flat ring (annulus) swept by the two vertical-axis revolutes, made thick by the vertical stroke; a gap where the limits stop it
Cylindrical, PlateCrane EX (p.55)RPPa thick-walled cylinder sector: angle from the R, height and radius ranges from the two P
Cartesian, Yamaha (p.56)PPPa rectangular box with edges equal to the three strokes
Derivation (planar examples) added

2R arm, links \(L_1, L_2\): the tip distance \(r\) from the base satisfies \(|L_1-L_2| \le r \le L_1+L_2\) (elbow fully folded to fully stretched). For \(L = 0.5, 0.3\) m this is \(0.2 \le r \le 0.8\) m. checked

3R arm, links \(L_1, L_2, L_3\), tool at \(\mathbf p\) with direction angle \(\phi\): the wrist is at \(\mathbf w = \mathbf p - L_3(\cos\phi, \sin\phi)\), and \(\mathbf p\) is reachable at that \(\phi\) iff \(|L_1-L_2| \le |\mathbf w| \le L_1+L_2\). Reachable: some \(\phi\) works. Dexterous: every \(\phi\) works, i.e. the whole circle of radius \(L_3\) about \(\mathbf p\) lies inside the 2R annulus. For equal links \(L\): the annulus is \(0 \le |\mathbf w| \le 2L\), so the circle fits iff \(r + L \le 2L\), that is \(r \le L\). With \(L = 0.3\) m: \(W_R\) is the disc \(r \le 0.9\) m and \(W_D\) the disc \(r \le 0.3\) m. checked by sampling points and tool angles.

A mechanism with fewer DoF than the pose has (3 planar, 6 spatial) cannot reach all orientations at any point, so its dexterous workspace is empty.

Expect: “which is true, \(W_R \subseteq W_D\), \(W_R \subset W_D\), \(W_R \supseteq W_D\), \(W_R \supset W_D\)?” Answer \(W_R \supseteq W_D\) (dexterous is the harder condition, so the smaller set). The strict options are traps. Also expect to name the arm from its joint string (RRR PUMA, RRP SCARA, RPP cylindrical, PPP Cartesian) and to say what limits a workspace (link geometry, joint limits).

8 · The manipulation problems this unit solves pp. 58–62

Picture

Slide 59 previews the unit. Forward kinematics: joint coordinates to end-effector pose (W2). Inverse kinematics: tip pose to joint coordinates (W3). Velocity kinematics: how fast the end effector moves for given joint rates, or the joint rates needed (W6). Dynamics: the torque to accelerate the tip, or the force it exerts (W7–W9). Trajectory generation: a path in joint or tool space (W5). A pose is position plus orientation (p.60). Relating two frames takes 6 numbers, 3 translation and 3 rotation, written as a 4×4 homogeneous matrix (p.62). Frames named: {B} base, {W} wrist, {T} tool, {S} station, {G} goal, {C} camera (p.61).

Low weight alone, but the 6-number pose is why a 6-DoF arm is the minimum for arbitrary tool poses, and why 7 joints are “redundant”.

P1.1 · Planar serial RPR (slides pp. 42–43)calculate

A planar chain has a revolute, a prismatic and a revolute joint in series from the ground. Find M with both forms of the equation.

Hint 1: D and N

Planar, so D = 3. Links: ground (0) and three moving links.

Hint 2: the joints

All three joints have F = 1, so \(P_1 = 3\), \(P_2 = 0\).

Full solution

Form 1: \(M = 3(N-1) - 2P_1 - P_2 = 3(4-1) - 2\times3 - 0 = 9 - 6 = 3\).

Form 2: \(M = D(N-J-1) + \sum F_i = 3(4-3-1) + 3 = 0 + 3 = 3\). Both agree. For any serial chain \(N = J+1\), so \(M\) is simply the sum of the joint DoF: here 3. Three actuators (one per joint) are needed, and they are enough to set the tip’s x, y and angle. checked

Common wrong answer: N = 3 (forgetting ground) gives \(3(3-1)-6 = 0\).

Full solution

(a) \(M = 3(5-1) - 2\times6 - 0 = 0\). (b) The body moves: 1 DoF (slide: “it has 1 degree of freedom”). Check: remove one leg, \(N=4\), \(J=4\), \(M = 1\).

(c) G–K assumes the constraints are independent. Because the legs are equal and parallel, the third leg repeats a restriction already imposed by the other two (a redundant link), so only 5 of the 6 closure equations are independent. With unequal legs the structure would lock and 0 would be right. checked (rank 5 versus rank 6)

P1.5 · Stewart platform (slide p.48 homework)calculate

Six identical legs, each ground–U–P–S–platform. Find the mobility of the whole platform.

Hint 1

D = 6. Links: ground, platform, and two parts per leg.

Hint 2

\(J = 18\); the sum of joint DoF is \(6(2+1+3)\).

Full solution

\(N = 14\), \(J = 18\), \(\sum F_i = 36\): \(M = 6(14-18-1) + 36 = 6\). Six DoF, six actuators: fully actuated. checked

Caution: counting only the single drawn leg also gives 6 (a serial chain’s M is the sum of its joint DoF), but that is the leg’s DoF, not the platform’s count. Common wrong answers: 0 (no ground), 21 (D = 3).

P1.6 · Delta mechanism (W13 Q7)calculateexplain

A delta has a base, a platform and three legs. Each leg is one R at the base driving an arm, then two rods, each rod with a spherical joint at both ends. Find M by plain G–K, then with the extended equation using \(R_C = 0\) and \(R_M = 6\).

Hint 1

\(N = 2 + 3\times3 = 11\); per leg 1 R and 4 S, so \(J = 15\).

Hint 2

\(\sum F_i = 3\times(1 + 4\times3)\). Then subtract \(R_M\).

Full solution

Plain: \(6(11-15-1) + 39 = -30 + 39 = 9\). Extended: \(9 + 0 - 6 = 3\). The six rods can each spin about their own axis (ball joints at both ends); these local spins do not move the platform, so they are subtracted. The delta moves its platform in 3 DoF (x, y, z) with 3 motors. checked

Explain in the exam: “plain G–K counts local rod spins as DoF; \(R_M\) removes them.” Week 4 shows how to find \(R_C\) and \(R_M\) yourself.

P1.8 · Classify the actuationexplain

Name each as fully actuated, under-actuated or redundant, and say which meaning you use: (a) a 6R arm with 6 motors; (b) the same arm with 4 motors; (c) a 7R arm with 7 motors used to place a tool in a 3D pose; (d) a Stewart platform with 6 actuators.

P1.9 · Workspace shapes of four arms (notebook Q6)explainsketch

Describe and sketch the workspace of a PUMA 560 (RRR), a SCARA (RRP), a cylindrical (RPP) and a Cartesian (PPP) arm, and say what limits each.

Model answer

PUMA (RRR): a thick spherical shell about the shoulder, the outer radius set by the sum of the upper-arm and forearm lengths, with a cut-out wedge where the waist angle is limited. SCARA (RRP): a flat ring (annulus) from the two vertical-axis revolutes, extruded by the vertical prismatic stroke, with a gap from joint limits. Cylindrical (RPP): a thick-walled cylinder sector: the R gives the angular span, one P the height, the other P the radial range (the inner hole is the minimum radial extension). Cartesian (PPP): a box whose edges are the three strokes. Every shape is limited by the link geometry and the joint limits (p.52). The shapes are read from the slide figures (pp. 53–56).

P1.10 · Spot the errorsspot the error

A student writes: “Four-bar: D = 6, N = 4, J = 4, so \(M = 6(4-4-1)+4 = -2\); a negative mobility means the mechanism cannot be built.” And: “Delta with three motors: M = 9, so it is under-actuated.” Find all the errors.

Q1trap

Which is true? (a) \(W_R\subset W_D\) (b) \(W_R\supseteq W_D\) (c) \(W_R=W_D\) always (d) \(W_R\subseteq W_D\)

Answer

(b). Dexterous (all orientations) is the stronger condition. (a) and (d) are backwards; (c) is “always”.

Q2calculate

A serial 6R arm in 3D: N = ?, J = ?, M = ? With 4 motors, what is it called?

Answer

N = 7 (ground plus 6 links), J = 6, \(M = 6(7-6-1)+6 = 6\). With 4 motors it is under-actuated.

Q3concept

G–K gives M = 0 for a mechanism you can see moving. Give the reason and the usual fix.

Answer

G–K assumes the constraints are independent; here some are redundant (an extra link repeating a constraint, as in the three equal parallel legs). Remove the redundant link (or add the over-constraint term \(R_C\), Week 4) and recount.
