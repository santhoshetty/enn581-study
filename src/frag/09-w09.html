Week 9 · Control, part 2

How do we control a robot whose dynamics are non-linear and coupled?

Week 8 gave a servo law that works for a plain mass-spring-damper. A real arm adds gravity, friction and coupled inertia. The fix is computed-torque (partitioned) control: use the model to cancel everything predictable, then let a simple PD law steer what is left. This week extends that idea to many joints, to Cartesian space, to force control and to mixed force/position tasks, and asks what goes wrong when the model is wrong.

Intuition first

Cancel, then steer

Carrying a bag of known weight, you first add the force that holds it up, then walk wherever you like. Computed torque adds the torque that cancels gravity, friction and inertia in advance. What remains behaves like a weightless unit mass: \(\ddot q = \) whatever the servo asks for.

The servo is a tuned spring

Once the plant is a unit mass, the PD part is just a spring-damper pulling the error to zero: \(\ddot e + k_v\dot e + k_p e = 0\). Pick \(k_v = 2\sqrt{k_p}\) and the error settles with no overshoot, like a well-set door closer.

A wrong scale leaves a push

If you think a box is 7% lighter than it is, you under-lift every time. The leftover is a steady push on the error equation, proportional to your mismatch. A stiffer spring (bigger \(k_p\)) shrinks the sag but never removes it.In the practical: part (h) uses 2.8 kg against the true 3 kg.

Slow planner, fast reflex

A pianist plans the piece slowly, but the fingers correct in milliseconds. The dynamics model is expensive, so run it slowly along the planned path. The cheap error-times-gain servo runs fast.

Pushing on a wall

Press a hand on a stiff wall: a tiny motion makes a big force change, so the force controller must act on tiny motions (hence the factor \(k_e^{-1}\)). Wiping a window needs both: press with force normal to the glass, glide with position along it.In the practical: none; the humanoid on p. 46 is the lecture’s example.

1 · Partitioned control of a non-linear single-input system pp. 5–11

Picture

The controller is cut into two parts (p.10). The model-based part (\(\alpha\), \(\beta\)) knows the physics and cancels it. The servo part (\(f'\)) sees only a perfect unit mass and steers it with the usual PD law. The slide’s summary (p.49): the model-based part cancels the non-linearity, the servo part ensures stability.

Derivation: cubic spring

Slide 8: a mass-spring-damper is linear, \(m\ddot x + b\dot x + kx = f\) (m kg, b N·s/m, k N/m, f N, x m). Slide 9 makes the spring cubic, so the plant is non-linear:

\[ m\ddot x + b\dot x + kx^{3} = f \qquad (k\ \text{in N/m}^3) \]

Step 1: isolate the acceleration term, \(m\,\ddot x + (b\dot x + kx^3) = f\). Step 2: partition \(f = \alpha f' + \beta\) with \(\alpha = m\) and \(\beta = b\dot x + kx^3\) (p.10). Step 3: substitute,

\[ m\ddot x + \beta = m f' + \beta \;\Rightarrow\; \ddot x = f'. \]

The plant now looks like a pure double integrator, whatever the spring does. \(f'\) (“f-prime”) is a commanded acceleration in m/s². Step 4: the servo law is the Week 8 law,

\[ f' = \ddot x_d + k_v\dot e + k_p e,\qquad e = x_d - x \;\Rightarrow\; \ddot e + k_v\dot e + k_p e = 0 . \]

Critical damping needs \(k_v = 2\sqrt{k_p}\) (Week 8; slide 8 only says “like being critically damped”). Units: \(k_p\) in s⁻², \(k_v\) in s⁻¹. Check: \(\alpha f'\) is kg·m/s² = N. ✓

Limitation (p.15): you must know the structure (cubic spring, Coulomb friction) and the parameters (\(k\), \(b_c\), …). In practice they are not always known. Section 4 shows what that costs.

Exam recipe
  1. Write the plant with the acceleration term isolated: (coefficient)·\(\ddot x\) + (everything else) = input.
  2. \(\alpha\) = the coefficient of \(\ddot x\). \(\beta\) = everything else, including every non-linear term and every sign.
  3. Servo: \(f' = \ddot x_d + k_v\dot e + k_p e\) with \(e = x_d - x\) (desired minus actual).
  4. Law: \(f = \alpha f' + \beta\). Write \(\beta\) in terms of the measured \(x,\dot x\), not the desired ones.
  5. Show the closed loop: \(\ddot x = f'\), hence \(\ddot e + k_v\dot e + k_p e = 0\).
  6. Sanity check units: \(\alpha f'\) must have the units of \(f\).
Worked example (numbers are mine; the slides are symbolic)

m = 2 kg, b = 4 N·s/m, k = 50 N/m³, \(k_p = 100\) s⁻², \(k_v = 20\) s⁻¹. Now: x = 0.10 m, ẋ = 0.30 m/s; desired \(x_d = 0.12\) m, \(\dot x_d = 0.40\) m/s, \(\ddot x_d = 0.50\) m/s².

\(e = 0.02\) m, \(\dot e = 0.10\) m/s. \(f' = 0.50 + 20(0.10) + 100(0.02) = 4.5\) m/s². \(\beta = 4(0.30) + 50(0.10)^3 = 1.25\) N. \(f = 2(4.5) + 1.25 = 10.25\) N.

Plant: \(\ddot x = (f - b\dot x - kx^3)/m = (10.25 - 1.2 - 0.05)/2 = 4.5\) m/s² \(= f'\). ✓ Then \(\ddot e = 0.5 - 4.5 = -4.0\), and \(\ddot e + 20(0.10) + 100(0.02) = -4 + 2 + 2 = 0\). ✓ checked

Typical question: “Given \(m\ddot x + b\dot x + kx^3 = f\) (or another EoM), write the partitioned law.” Marks are for \(\alpha = m\), \(\beta\) = the rest, and the servo with \(\ddot x_d\). Trap: the law uses \(f'\), an acceleration, inside \(\alpha f'\). Do not add \(k_p e\) to \(f\) directly without multiplying by \(\alpha\).

2 · A single link with gravity and friction pp. 12–15

Picture

One rigid link of length L, pivoted at the origin, with a point mass m at its tip. The angle q is measured counter-clockwise from the horizontal (p.12). Gravity pulls the mass down with force mg. Its lever arm about the pivot is the horizontal distance \(L\cos q\), so the gravity torque is \(mgL\cos q\): largest when the link is horizontal (q = 0), zero when vertical (q = 90°).

Derivation: equation of motion (Lagrangian, as on the handwritten slide 13)

Tip position \(x = L\cos q,\ y = L\sin q\), so \(\dot x = -L\sin q\,\dot q\) and \(\dot y = L\cos q\,\dot q\) (m, m/s). Kinetic and potential energy:

\[ K = \tfrac12 m(\dot x^2+\dot y^2) = \tfrac12 mL^2\dot q^2,\qquad P = mgy = mgL\sin q,\qquad \mathcal L = K - P . \]

The generalised torque is \(Q = \dfrac{d}{dt}\dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot q} - \dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial q}\). Here \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q = mL^2\dot q\), so its derivative is \(mL^2\ddot q\), and \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial q = -mgL\cos q\). Hence \(Q = mL^2\ddot q + mgL\cos q\). Friction opposes motion, so the actuator torque must also overcome it (p.12): viscous \(\tau_v = b_v\dot q\) and Coulomb \(\tau_c = b_c\,\mathrm{sgn}(\dot q)\):

\[ \tau = mL^2\ddot q + b_v\dot q + b_c\,\mathrm{sgn}(\dot q) + mgL\cos q \qquad\text{(N·m)} \]

checked (Lagrangian result, symbolically) Units: m kg, L m, \(b_v\) N·m·s/rad, \(b_c\) N·m, g = 9.81 m/s². added If the link also has its own mass or inertia, \(mL^2\) becomes the total inertia about the pivot.

Partition (p.14): \(\tau = \alpha\tau' + \beta\) with

\[ \alpha = mL^2,\qquad \beta = b_v\dot q + b_c\,\mathrm{sgn}(\dot q) + mgL\cos q,\qquad \tau' = \ddot q_d + k_v\dot e + k_p e,\quad e = q_d - q . \]

Substituting into the EoM gives \(mL^2\ddot q = mL^2\tau'\), so \(\ddot q = \tau'\) and \(\ddot e + k_v\dot e + k_p e = 0\) again.

added \(\mathrm{sgn}(\dot q)\) jumps at \(\dot q = 0\). With a noisy velocity measurement the term chatters between \(\pm b_c\). Practical implementations smooth it (the widget above uses \(\tanh(\dot q/0.01)\) in plant and controller alike).

Exam recipe
  1. Get the EoM (Lagrangian if not given): \(K\), \(P\), then \(\tfrac{d}{dt}\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q - \partial\mathcal L/\partial q\).
  2. Add friction torques on the side that the actuator must overcome.
  3. \(\alpha\) = coefficient of \(\ddot q\); \(\beta\) = the rest (velocity terms, gravity, friction).
  4. \(\tau' = \ddot q_d + k_v\dot e + k_p e\).
  5. \(\tau = \alpha\tau' + \beta\). Quote it with the measured \(q,\dot q\) inside \(\beta\).
  6. Check: at rest on the path (\(e = 0\), \(\ddot q_d = 0\), \(\dot q = 0\)) the torque must equal the gravity torque \(mgL\cos q\).
Worked example

m = 2 kg, L = 0.5 m, \(b_v = 0.3\) N·m·s/rad, \(b_c = 0.2\) N·m, g = 9.81 m/s². State: q = 30°, \(\dot q\) = 1 rad/s. Desired \(\ddot q_d\) = 2 rad/s², e = 0.05 rad, \(\dot e\) = −0.1 rad/s; \(k_p\) = 64 s⁻², \(k_v\) = 16 s⁻¹.

\(\alpha = 2(0.5)^2 = 0.5\) kg·m². \(\tau' = 2 + 16(-0.1) + 64(0.05) = 3.6\) rad/s². \(\beta = 0.3(1) + 0.2(+1) + 2(9.81)(0.5)\cos30^\circ = 0.5 + 8.4957 = 8.9957\) N·m. \(\tau = 0.5(3.6) + 8.9957 = 10.7957\) N·m.

Check with the plant: \(\ddot q = (\tau - \beta)/\alpha = 1.8/0.5 = 3.6 = \tau'\). ✓ checked

Slides vs slides

Friction is written \(\mathbf f(\dot{\mathbf q})\) on p. 19 and on the “true” side of p. 27, but \(\hat{\mathbf f}(\mathbf q,\dot{\mathbf q})\) in \(\hat\beta\) (p. 27), in the error equation (p. 28) and on the practical sheet. The friction in this deck (viscous, Coulomb) depends on velocity only, so both mean the same. The extra \(\mathbf q\) is generality (a friction that varies with position). Write \(\mathbf f(\mathbf q,\dot{\mathbf q})\) if the question does, otherwise \(\mathbf f(\dot{\mathbf q})\). Slide 18 also writes the generalised force as F where p. 19 writes Q; same object.

Calculation: given the EoM and a state, compute \(\tau\). Common slips: using degrees in \(\cos q\) on a radian calculator, forgetting that \(\mathrm{sgn}(\dot q)\) takes the sign of the velocity (not of q or e), and using \(\ddot q_d\) in \(\beta\). Explain: “why does the law need the parameters?” (p. 15).

3 · The n-joint manipulator: computed torque pp. 16–21

Picture

Every line in the block diagram is now an \(n\times1\) vector (p.17). The model-based part has two jobs: multiply the commanded accelerations by the inertia matrix \(\mathbf M(\mathbf q)\), and add the velocity, gravity and friction torques. The first job undoes the coupling: pushing joint 1 also needs torque on joints 2 and 3 (the off-diagonal terms of \(\mathbf M\)), and \(\mathbf M\) supplies exactly that.

Derivation

The manipulator dynamics from Week 8 (p.19), all \(n\times1\) except \(\mathbf M\) (\(n\times n\)):

\[ \mathbf Q = \mathbf M(\mathbf q)\ddot{\mathbf q} + \mathbf C(\mathbf q,\dot{\mathbf q}) + \mathbf g(\mathbf q) + \mathbf f(\dot{\mathbf q}) \]

Partition \(\mathbf Q = \boldsymbol\alpha\mathbf Q' + \boldsymbol\beta\) with \(\boldsymbol\alpha = \mathbf M(\mathbf q)\), \(\boldsymbol\beta = \mathbf C + \mathbf g + \mathbf f\), and servo \(\mathbf Q' = \ddot{\mathbf q}_d + \mathbf K_v\dot{\mathbf E} + \mathbf K_p\mathbf E\) (pp. 18–19). Substituting: \(\mathbf M\ddot{\mathbf q} + \boldsymbol\beta = \mathbf M\mathbf Q' + \boldsymbol\beta\). \(\mathbf M\) is symmetric positive definite, hence invertible, so \(\ddot{\mathbf q} = \mathbf Q'\) and

\[ \ddot{\mathbf E} + \mathbf K_v\dot{\mathbf E} + \mathbf K_p\mathbf E = \mathbf 0 \quad(\text{slide }21). \]

With diagonal gains each row is independent: \(\ddot e_i + k_{v,i}\dot e_i + k_{p,i}e_i = 0\). A coupled, non-linear robot becomes \(n\) separate linear second-order systems. checked (20 random states on the 3-link arm)

Three facts the slides stress (p.18, p.20): \(\boldsymbol\alpha\) is chosen to decouple the n equations and is not diagonal; \(\mathbf K_v,\mathbf K_p\) are usually diagonal; the model-based box “linearises the system”, the servo box “stabilises the closed-loop system”.

Exam recipe
  1. Write the EoM as \(\mathbf M\ddot{\mathbf q} + \mathbf C + \mathbf g + \mathbf f = \mathbf Q\) and identify the matrices.
  2. \(\boldsymbol\alpha = \mathbf M(\mathbf q)\) (full matrix), \(\boldsymbol\beta = \mathbf C + \mathbf g + \mathbf f\).
  3. \(\mathbf Q' = \ddot{\mathbf q}_d + \mathbf K_v\dot{\mathbf E} + \mathbf K_p\mathbf E\); choose \(\mathbf K\) diagonal, e.g. \(k_p=\omega_n^2\), \(k_v = 2\omega_n\).
  4. \(\mathbf Q = \mathbf M(\mathbf q)\,\mathbf Q' + \boldsymbol\beta\).
  5. State the closed loop \(\ddot{\mathbf E} + \mathbf K_v\dot{\mathbf E} + \mathbf K_p\mathbf E = \mathbf 0\) and say “decoupled because the gains are diagonal”.
  6. Qualify: “only if the model is exact and the law runs continuously”.
Worked example: why \(\boldsymbol\alpha\) is not diagonal (practical arm, initial pose)

For the 3-link arm of the practical sheet at \(\mathbf q = (0, 0, 90^\circ)\) (derived in problem P9.2) \(\mathbf M = \begin{bmatrix}103&31&1\\31&13&1\\1&1&1\end{bmatrix}\) kg·m². Ask for a unit acceleration of joint 1 only, \(\mathbf Q' = (1,0,0)\) rad/s². Then \(\mathbf Q - \boldsymbol\beta = \mathbf M\mathbf Q' = (103, 31, 1)\) N·m. To accelerate joint 1 by 1 rad/s² and keep joints 2 and 3 still, the controller must also apply 31 N·m at joint 2 and 1 N·m at joint 3. A \(\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf M)\) would skip those. checked

Explain / spot-the-error: “the closed loop is linear and decoupled” is true only for a perfect model (slide 21). “\(\alpha\) is diagonal because \(K_p,K_v\) are” is false. If asked “what does the red box do and the green box do?”: red (model-based) linearises, green (servo) stabilises.

4 · When the ideal fails: imprecise model and discretisation pp. 22–28

Picture

Two things break the clean result \(\ddot E + K_v\dot E + K_pE = 0\) (p.22). (1) The model is inexact: friction is hard to model, the robot wears, a grasped object shifts the mass (p.26). (2) The law runs on a computer: sensors are sampled, torques updated at discrete times, and the dynamics computation takes finite time (p.23). The dynamics part is the slow one; error × gain is quick (p.24).

The slide’s fix for (2) (p.25): move the model-based block to the outer loop. Its input is the desired trajectory \(\ddot{\mathbf q}_d,\dot{\mathbf q}_d,\mathbf q_d\), so it can be evaluated ahead of time or at a slower rate, and the fast servo loop (\(K_p,K_v\) on the measured error) runs inside. reading of the diagram The cancellation is then only approximate (the model is evaluated along the plan, not the actual state), so the servo has to mop up the difference.

Derivation: error equation with hats (slide 28)

Hats mean estimates. The law uses \(\boldsymbol\alpha = \hat{\mathbf M}(\mathbf q)\), \(\boldsymbol\beta = \hat{\mathbf C} + \hat{\mathbf g} + \hat{\mathbf f}\) (p.27) with the same servo \(\mathbf Q'\). The true plant is \(\mathbf M\ddot{\mathbf q} + \mathbf C + \mathbf g + \mathbf f = \mathbf Q\). Equate the two:

\[ \mathbf M\ddot{\mathbf q} + \mathbf C + \mathbf g + \mathbf f = \hat{\mathbf M}\big(\ddot{\mathbf q}_d + \mathbf K_v\dot{\mathbf E} + \mathbf K_p\mathbf E\big) + \hat{\mathbf C} + \hat{\mathbf g} + \hat{\mathbf f}. \]

Add and subtract \(\hat{\mathbf M}\ddot{\mathbf q}\) on the left, move the hatted torques across, multiply by \(\hat{\mathbf M}^{-1}\) and use \(\ddot{\mathbf E} = \ddot{\mathbf q}_d - \ddot{\mathbf q}\):

\[ \ddot{\mathbf E} + \mathbf K_v\dot{\mathbf E} + \mathbf K_p\mathbf E = \hat{\mathbf M}^{-1}\Big[(\mathbf M-\hat{\mathbf M})\ddot{\mathbf q} + (\mathbf C-\hat{\mathbf C}) + (\mathbf g-\hat{\mathbf g}) + (\mathbf f-\hat{\mathbf f})\Big] \quad(\text{slide }28). \]

checked (20 random states, 2-link arm with mismatched masses) Read it as a servo loop driven by a forcing term: each model mismatch pushes on the error. If every hat is exact the right side is zero. The error no longer decays to zero; for a bounded forcing it settles at roughly (forcing)/\(k_p\), so a bigger \(k_p\) shrinks it (at the price of noise sensitivity and torque limits). Note the \(\ddot{\mathbf q}\) on the right is the actual acceleration.

Single link with only the mass wrong, \(\hat m = km\) (added: the special case of the slide’s equation). Here \(\alpha = mL^2\), \(\hat\alpha = kmL^2\), and \(\beta-\hat\beta = (m-\hat m)gL\cos q\). Dividing the bracket by \(\hat\alpha\):

\[ \ddot e + k_v\dot e + k_p e = \frac{m-\hat m}{\hat m}\Big[\ddot q + \frac gL\cos q\Big] . \]

checked (20 random states) The coefficient \((m-\hat m)/\hat m\) is the relative error in mass; the bracket is the acceleration the mass actually has plus the gravity acceleration at the pivot. If \(\hat m\lt m\) gravity is under-compensated, the link sags, and \(e = q_d - q\) becomes positive.

Exam recipe
  1. Write the law with hats: \(\boldsymbol\alpha = \hat{\mathbf M}\), \(\boldsymbol\beta = \hat{\mathbf C}+\hat{\mathbf g}+\hat{\mathbf f}\).
  2. Equate to the true dynamics and isolate \(\ddot{\mathbf E} + \mathbf K_v\dot{\mathbf E} + \mathbf K_p\mathbf E\).
  3. Quote the slide-28 right side, term by term (mass, Coriolis, gravity, friction mismatch).
  4. For a numeric case, plug in the mismatch: \((m-\hat m)/\hat m\) and the bracket.
  5. Static error: set \(\ddot e = \dot e = 0\), \(\ddot q = 0\): \(e_{ss} \approx \dfrac{m-\hat m}{\hat m}\dfrac{g}{L}\dfrac{\cos q}{k_p}\).
  6. Discretisation: say the model part is slow, so run it in the outer loop; the servo loop stays fast.
Worked example: the practical’s 2.8 kg (single link: m = 3 kg, L = 3 m, \(k_p=100\) s⁻², \(k_v=20\) s⁻¹)

\(\hat m = 2.8\) kg, so \((m-\hat m)/\hat m = 0.2/2.8 = 1/14 = 0.0714\). \(g/L = 9.81/3 = 3.27\) rad/s². Holding the link horizontal (q = 0, \(\ddot q = 0\)): forcing \(= 0.0714(3.27) = 0.2336\) rad/s², so \(e_{ss} = 0.2336/100 = 2.34\) mrad \(= 0.134^\circ\). Holding it vertical (q = 90°): \(\cos q = 0\), no lever arm, so the static error from mass mismatch is essentially zero. checked, including a simulation of the hold

Typical question: “the mass is estimated 5% too small; write the closed-loop error equation and say what happens”. State: forcing term non-zero, error does not decay to zero, steady-state error proportional to the mismatch and inversely to \(k_p\), larger when the link is horizontal. Trap: “the servo law fixes any model error” (it reduces it, it does not remove it).

5 · Control in Cartesian space pp. 29–33

Picture

Tasks are given as paths of the tool, \(\mathbf x(t)\). The joint-space controller of Section 3 needs the whole plan in joint space. Slide 30 does that with a trajectory generator that converts \((\mathbf x_d,\dot{\mathbf x}_d,\ddot{\mathbf x}_d)\) into \((\mathbf q_d,\dot{\mathbf q}_d,\ddot{\mathbf q}_d)\). Slide 31 lists the three conversions and says all are computationally intensive:

\[ \mathbf q = \mathcal K^{-1}(\mathbf x),\qquad \dot{\mathbf q} = \mathbf J^{-1}(\mathbf q)\dot{\mathbf x},\qquad \ddot{\mathbf q} = \tfrac{d}{dt}\big(\mathbf J^{-1}\big)\dot{\mathbf x} + \mathbf J^{-1}\ddot{\mathbf x}. \]

The alternative (p.32): write the dynamics in Cartesian space, \(\mathcal F = \mathbf M_x(\mathbf q)\ddot{\mathbf x} + \mathbf C_x(\mathbf q,\dot{\mathbf q}) + \mathbf g_x(\mathbf q)\), solve for the control force/wrench \(\mathcal F\) from the Cartesian error, then convert to joint torques with \(\mathbf Q = \mathbf J^\top(\mathbf q)\,\mathcal F\). Feedback is cheap and forward: \(\mathbf x = \mathcal K(\mathbf q)\) (forward kinematics) and \(\dot{\mathbf x} = \mathbf J(\mathbf q)\dot{\mathbf q}\) (p.33).

Derivation

The Cartesian law mirrors the joint law (p.33): servo on \(\mathbf e_x = \mathbf x_d - \mathbf x\), then \(\mathbf M_x\), then add the model terms:

\[ \mathcal F = \mathbf M_x(\mathbf q)\big(\ddot{\mathbf x}_d + \mathbf K_v\dot{\mathbf e}_x + \mathbf K_p\mathbf e_x\big) + \mathbf c_x(\mathbf q,\dot{\mathbf q}) + \mathbf g_x(\mathbf q),\qquad \mathbf Q = \mathbf J^\top(\mathbf q)\,\mathcal F, \]

so \(\ddot{\mathbf e}_x + \mathbf K_v\dot{\mathbf e}_x + \mathbf K_p\mathbf e_x = \mathbf 0\) in Cartesian coordinates (exact model).

added Why \(\mathbf Q = \mathbf J^\top\mathcal F\): virtual work. A small joint motion \(\delta\mathbf q\) moves the tool by \(\delta\mathbf x = \mathbf J\delta\mathbf q\). Equal work gives \(\mathbf Q^\top\delta\mathbf q = \mathcal F^\top\delta\mathbf x = \mathcal F^\top\mathbf J\delta\mathbf q\), so \(\mathbf Q = \mathbf J^\top\mathcal F\) for any \(\delta\mathbf q\). This is the statics result from W6.

added Where the Cartesian terms come from (standard result, not on the slides): from \(\ddot{\mathbf x} = \mathbf J\ddot{\mathbf q} + \dot{\mathbf J}\dot{\mathbf q}\) get \(\ddot{\mathbf q} = \mathbf J^{-1}(\ddot{\mathbf x} - \dot{\mathbf J}\dot{\mathbf q})\). Substitute in the joint dynamics and premultiply by \(\mathbf J^{-\top}\): \(\mathbf M_x = \mathbf J^{-\top}\mathbf M\mathbf J^{-1}\), \(\mathbf c_x = \mathbf J^{-\top}(\mathbf C - \mathbf M\mathbf J^{-1}\dot{\mathbf J}\dot{\mathbf q})\), \(\mathbf g_x = \mathbf J^{-\top}\mathbf g\). checked (numerically, 2-link arm) So these still need \(\mathbf J\) to be invertible: near a singularity \(\mathbf M_x\) blows up (some tool direction cannot be accelerated), even though \(\mathbf J^\top\mathcal F\) itself is always fine.

Slides vs textbook

Slide 31 prints the third line as \(\dot J^{-1}(\mathbf q)\dot{\mathbf x} + J^{-1}(\mathbf q)\ddot{\mathbf x}\), with the dot over J. The correct term is the time derivative of the inverse: \(\tfrac{d}{dt}(\mathbf J^{-1}) = -\mathbf J^{-1}\dot{\mathbf J}\mathbf J^{-1}\). Derivation: \(\dot{\mathbf x} = \mathbf J\dot{\mathbf q}\Rightarrow\ddot{\mathbf q} = \mathbf J^{-1}(\ddot{\mathbf x} - \dot{\mathbf J}\dot{\mathbf q}) = \mathbf J^{-1}\ddot{\mathbf x} - \mathbf J^{-1}\dot{\mathbf J}\mathbf J^{-1}\dot{\mathbf x}\). “Inverse of \(\dot{\mathbf J}\)” would be wrong (and \(\dot{\mathbf J}\) is usually not even invertible). checked by finite differences

Exam recipe
  1. State why: joint-space needs \(\mathcal K^{-1}\), \(\mathbf J^{-1}\) and \(\tfrac d{dt}\mathbf J^{-1}\) along the whole path (slide 31).

6 · Force control pp. 34–44

Picture

Goal (p.35): make the end effector push on the environment with a desired force. Model (p.36): a mass m pushed by the control force f against a wall, which behaves as a spring \(f_e = k_e x\). An unknown disturbance \(f_{dist}\) also acts on the mass. The handwritten balance is \(f - f_e - f_{dist} = m\ddot x\).

Derivation: the ideal law, and why it fails

Plant (p.37): \(f = m\ddot x + f_{dist} + f_e\). Because \(f_e = k_ex\), \(x = k_e^{-1}f_e\) and \(\ddot x = k_e^{-1}\ddot f_e\), so \(f = mk_e^{-1}\ddot f_e + f_{dist} + f_e\). Now the controlled quantity is the force, and the same partition applies with \(\alpha = mk_e^{-1}\) (units kg·m/N = s²) and \(\beta = f_{dist} + f_e\):

\[ f = mk_e^{-1}\big[\ddot f_d + k_v\dot E + k_pE\big] + f_{dist} + f_e,\qquad E = f_d - f_e \]

with \(f_d\) the desired interaction force. Substituting gives \(\ddot f_e = f'\), i.e. \(\ddot E + k_v\dot E + k_pE = 0\) (p.38). But \(\beta\) contains \(f_{dist}\), which we do not know, so the law is infeasible. Two ways out (p.39). Write \(\alpha = mk_e^{-1}\).

Option 1: drop \(f_{dist}\), keep \(f_e\): \(f = \alpha[\ddot f_d + k_v\dot E + k_pE] + f_e\). Equate to the plant \(f = \alpha\ddot f_e + f_{dist} + f_e\): \(\alpha(\ddot E + k_v\dot E + k_pE) = f_{dist}\), so

\[ \ddot E + k_v\dot E + k_pE = \frac{f_{dist}}{mk_e^{-1}} \;\Rightarrow\; E_{ss} = \frac{f_{dist}}{mk_e^{-1}k_p}\quad(\ddot E = \dot E = 0). \]

Option 2: replace \(f_{dist} + f_e\) by \(f_d\): \(f = \alpha[\ddot f_d + k_v\dot E + k_pE] + f_d\). Equating: \(\alpha(\ddot E + k_v\dot E + k_pE) = f_{dist} + f_e - f_d = f_{dist} - E\), so

\[ \ddot E + k_v\dot E + \Big(\frac{1}{mk_e^{-1}} + k_p\Big)E = \frac{f_{dist}}{mk_e^{-1}} \;\Rightarrow\; E_{ss} = \frac{f_{dist}}{1 + mk_e^{-1}k_p}. \]

checked (both closed loops and steady states, symbolically and by simulation) Let \(x = mk_e^{-1}k_p\) (dimensionless: kg·(m/N)·s⁻² = 1). Then \(E_1 = f_{dist}/x\), \(E_2 = f_{dist}/(1+x)\), and \(E_2/E_1 = x/(1+x) < 1\): Option 2 is always better, and much better when \(x\) is small, which is exactly a stiff environment (\(k_e\) large \(\Rightarrow x\) small) (p.40). Physically, as \(k_p\to0\) Option 2 still pushes with \(f_d\), so the contact force is \(f_d - f_{dist}\) and \(E\to f_{dist}\); Option 1 gets \(E\to\infty\).

Practical implementation (pp. 41–43). The full law needs \(\dot f_e\). Differentiating a noisy force measurement is “not a good idea” (p. 42), and \(\ddot f_d,\dot f_d\) are zero for a constant \(f_d\). Since \(\dot E = \dot f_d - k_e\dot x = -k_e\dot x\), replace the derivative of the force by the measured velocity: with Option 2,

\[ f = m\big[k_e^{-1}k_pE - k_v\dot x\big] + f_d . \]

checked (equal to Option 2 for constant \(f_d\)) This is the form on p. 43: outer loop \(k_p/k_e\) on the force error, inner velocity loop \(k_v\), and \(f_d\) fed forward. Stiffness is usually unknown; \(k_e\) is “usually chosen to be a high value” and the controller is designed to be robust to small changes (p.44). likely reason, inferred \(k_e\) enters only through the gain \(k_p/k_e\), so a high assumed value gives a lower, safer loop gain.

Slides vs slides

Slide 38 writes the closed loop with bold \(\mathbf K_v,\mathbf K_p\) and slide 39 (Option 2) writes capital \(K_v\) where Option 1 has \(k_v\). The system is scalar, so these are the plain gains \(k_v,k_p\). The steady-state formulas on slides 39–40 are consistent with the closed-loop equations printed beside them.

Exam recipe

  2. \(\alpha = mk_e^{-1}\), \(\beta = f_{dist} + f_e\); \(E = f_d - f_e\). Note \(\beta\) is infeasible (\(f_{dist}\) unknown).
  3. Option 1: delete \(f_{dist}\). Option 2: replace \(f_{dist}+f_e\) by \(f_d\).

7 · Hybrid force and position control pp. 45–47

Picture

Many tasks need both (p.46: a humanoid pressing and wiping a wall). Along the wall normal the hand must push with a force; along the wall it must follow a path. The hybrid controller runs both laws and uses a diagonal 0/1 selection matrix for each to pass, axis by axis, only the output that applies (p.47).

Derivation

The commanded Cartesian force is the sum of the two filtered outputs:

\[ \mathcal F = \mathbf S\,\mathcal F_{pos} + \mathbf S'\,\mathcal F_{force}, \]

where \(\mathcal F_{pos}\) comes from the Cartesian position law of Section 5 and \(\mathcal F_{force}\) from a force law as in Section 6. \(\mathbf S\) and \(\mathbf S'\) are diagonal with 0 or 1 entries, “complementary to each other”, saying for each degree of freedom whether it is in position or force mode (slide 47). inferred Complementary means \(\mathbf S' = \mathbf I - \mathbf S\), so each axis is controlled by exactly one law. The slide does not write this relation. It also says “joint” in the last bullet although the diagram is in Cartesian space.

For the wall in the slide with the wall normal along \(x\) (axis labels are my choice): position control in \(y,z\), force control in \(x\):

\[ \mathbf S = \mathrm{diag}(0,1,1),\qquad \mathbf S' = \mathrm{diag}(1,0,0). \]

A full pose has six Cartesian degrees of freedom (three forces, three moments), and \(\mathbf S\) is then 6×6. The surface constraint decides: translation along the normal and rotations that tilt the hand off the surface are blocked (so they can be force/moment-controlled); sliding along the surface and the rotation about the normal are free (position-controlled).

Exam recipe
  1. Name the constraint surface and its normal direction.
  2. Directions the surface blocks: force control, \(\mathbf S'\) entry 1 (and \(\mathbf S\) entry 0).
  3. Free directions: position control, \(\mathbf S\) entry 1 (and \(\mathbf S'\) entry 0).
  4. Write \(\mathbf S\) and \(\mathbf S' = \mathbf I - \mathbf S\) as diagonal matrices in the stated order of axes.
  5. Write \(\mathcal F = \mathbf S\mathcal F_{pos} + \mathbf S'\mathcal F_{force}\) and \(\mathbf Q = \mathbf J^\top\mathcal F\).
  6. Feedback: position law uses \(\mathbf x\); force law uses the measured \(\mathcal F_E\).
Worked example

With the wall normal along \(x\): position law outputs \(\mathcal F_{pos} = (5, 3, -2)\) N, force law outputs \(\mathcal F_{force} = (8, 40, 7)\) N. Then

\[ \mathcal F = \mathrm{diag}(0,1,1)\begin{bmatrix}5\\3\\-2\end{bmatrix} + \mathrm{diag}(1,0,0)\begin{bmatrix}8\\40\\7\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0\\3\\-2\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}8\\0\\0\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}8\\3\\-2\end{bmatrix}\ \text{N}. \]

The 5 N of the position law along \(x\) and the 40 N and 7 N of the force law along \(y,z\) are discarded: each axis obeys one law only. checked

Scenario: “a robot polishes a flat table / presses a stamp / turns a crank; choose S and S′”. Name the normal, put the normal in force mode, everything else in position mode. Draw the p. 47 diagram if asked to sketch. Trap: swapping S and S′ (S goes with the position law in the diagram).

Stack \(\mathbf x = [\mathbf q;\dot{\mathbf q}]\), \(\dot{\mathbf x} = [\dot{\mathbf q};\ \ddot{\mathbf q}]\) and integrate.

Full solution

Algorithm (state \(\mathbf x_k = [\mathbf q_k;\dot{\mathbf q}_k]\), step \(h\)):

for k = 0, 1, 2, ...
    Q = controller(t_k, q_k, qdot_k)               # partitioned law, from the *model*
    qddot = solve(M_true(q_k), Q - C_true - g_true - f_true)   # forward dynamics (solve, do not invert)
    (q_{k+1}, qdot_{k+1}) = integrate(q_k, qdot_k, qddot, h)   # RK4, or semi-implicit Euler for small h
    log q_{k+1}, qdot_{k+1}, e = q_d(t_{k+1}) - q_{k+1}

Why. The plant maps torque to motion, so simulating it is forward dynamics: input \(\mathbf Q\), output \(\ddot{\mathbf q}\). The controller needs the torque that produces a desired acceleration, which is inverse dynamics: input \(\ddot{\mathbf q}\), output \(\mathbf Q\). Computed torque is inverse dynamics inside the controller; the simulator is forward dynamics standing in for the real robot.

Numbers. \(\ddot q(0) = (\tau - \beta)/\alpha = (151.91 - 88.29)/27 = 2.3562\) rad/s², exactly \(\ddot q_d(0)\) as the closed-loop theory says. checked

Keep the controller’s model and the simulator’s “true” model separate in the code: part (h) works only because the two differ.

P9.8 · Spot the error: three claims about computed torquespot the error

A study note says: (1) “Computed torque guarantees \(\ddot E + K_v\dot E + K_pE = 0\) for any robot.” (2) “Since \(K_p\) and \(K_v\) are diagonal, \(\alpha\) is diagonal.” (3) “\(f' = \ddot x_d + k_v\dot e + k_pe\) has units of force, so \(f = f' + \beta\).” Find the error in each and correct it.

Model answer

(1) It holds only if the model is exact (and the law runs continuously). (2) \(\boldsymbol\alpha = \mathbf M(\mathbf q)\) is a full matrix (slide 18); diagonal gains plus full \(\mathbf M\) are what give decoupled errors. (3) \(f'\) is an acceleration (m/s²); the law is \(f = \alpha f' + \beta\) with \(\alpha = m\) (kg), so \(\alpha f'\) is in newtons.

Blocks, left to right: inputs \(\ddot{\mathbf q}_d,\dot{\mathbf q}_d,\mathbf q_d\); servo = sum of \(\ddot{\mathbf q}_d\), \(\mathbf K_v\dot{\mathbf E}\), \(\mathbf K_p\mathbf E\) giving \(\mathbf Q'\); multiply by \(\mathbf M(\mathbf q)\); add \(\mathbf C(\mathbf q,\dot{\mathbf q}) + \mathbf g(\mathbf q) + \mathbf f(\dot{\mathbf q})\); output \(\mathbf Q\) into the manipulator, giving \(\mathbf q,\dot{\mathbf q}\). Mini-quiz

Q1concept

In computed-torque control, what does the model-based part do and what does the servo part do?

Answer

The model-based part (\(\alpha\), \(\beta\)) cancels the non-linear dynamics so the system looks like \(\ddot q = q'\); the servo part (\(\ddot q_d + k_v\dot e + k_pe\)) makes that linear system stable and tracking (slide 49). The first linearises, the second stabilises.

Q3spot the error

A student writes the Option 2 steady-state force error as \(f_{dist}/(mk_e^{-1}k_p)\) and concludes that Option 2 gets better as the wall gets stiffer. What is wrong?

Answer

That is Option 1’s formula. Option 2 is \(f_{dist}/(1 + mk_e^{-1}k_p)\). For a stiffer wall (\(k_e\) larger) \(x = mk_e^{-1}k_p\) shrinks, so both errors grow; but Option 2 saturates at \(f_{dist}\) while Option 1 grows without bound. So Option 2 is the better option on a stiff wall, but its error does not improve as the wall stiffens.
