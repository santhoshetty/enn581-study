Week 8 · Control part 1

How do the joints actually follow the planned motion?

Dynamics (W7) tells us the torque a trajectory needs. A real joint has friction, errors in the model and outside pushes, so we measure the error and correct it. This week builds that feedback law from one idea: cancel the known physics so the joint looks like a unit mass, then control the leftover with one spring-and-damper recipe.

Intuition first

Open loop is driving with your eyes shut

Inverse dynamics is a perfect recipe for the torques. But if the joint starts slightly off, or a gust pushes it, the recipe never notices. Closed loop is opening your eyes: measure the error, steer against it.In the practical: the torques of question 3 are exactly the open-loop recipe.

PD is a virtual spring and shock absorber

\(k_p\) pulls the joint toward the target like a spring. \(k_v\) resists speed like a shock absorber. Too little damper and the joint bounces. Too much and it oozes like treacle. Critical damping is the fastest return without a bounce.

Partitioning cancels the physics

Weigh the load, then add exactly the push that cancels friction and the spring. Now the joint behaves like a bare 1 kg mass whatever it really is. One tuning recipe (\(k_p,k_v\)) then works for every joint.

The sagging trolley and the patient friend

Push a trolley uphill with a spring: it sags back until the spring force balances the slope. That sag is the steady-state error. An integral term is a friend who keeps pushing harder for as long as you are still behind.

Gears make the motor feel heavy

A low bicycle gear spins the pedals fast and moves the wheel slowly. The rider’s own inertia is felt at the wheel multiplied by the ratio squared, \(\eta^2\). So a tiny rotor can weigh as much as the whole link.

1 · Open loop, closed loop, servo error, independent joints pp. 8–14

Picture

Open loop (p.11): Trajectory generator \(\to\) \((\mathbf q,\dot{\mathbf q},\ddot{\mathbf q})\) \(\to\) inverse-dynamics algorithm \(\to\) torques \(\mathbf Q\) \(\to\) robot. There is no arrow coming back.

Closed loop (p.12): the generator now sends \((\mathbf q_D,\dot{\mathbf q}_D,\ddot{\mathbf q}_D)\) to a control system that also receives the measured \(\mathbf q,\dot{\mathbf q}\) from the robot. The slide asks “what happens inside this block?”. The rest of the week answers that.

Derivation

The recap slide (p.8) gives the inverse dynamics the open-loop block evaluates along the planned path:

\[ \mathbf Q=\mathbf M(\mathbf q)\ddot{\mathbf q}+\mathbf C(\mathbf q,\dot{\mathbf q})+\mathbf g(\mathbf q) \]

\(\mathbf M\) is the inertia matrix (kg·m²), \(\mathbf C\) the Coriolis and centripetal torques, \(\mathbf g\) gravity (N·m). If the real joint starts at the planned state and the model is perfect, \(\ddot{\mathbf q}=\ddot{\mathbf q}_d\) and the plan is followed. Any mismatch is never seen, so it is never corrected.

Servo error (p.13) is desired minus actual, for position and velocity:

\[ E=\mathbf q_d-\mathbf q,\qquad \dot E=\dot{\mathbf q}_d-\dot{\mathbf q} \]

The aim is for \(E\to 0\) “quickly”, or to a small value. The sign matters: every later formula assumes desired minus actual.

Independent joint control (p.14): the \(n\times1\) vector loop is split into \(n\) separate single-joint loops. Each joint sees only its own \(q_{iD}(t)\), \(q_i\), \(\dot q_i\) and produces its own \(Q_i\). The coupling between joints (off-diagonal terms of \(\mathbf M\), Coriolis terms) is ignored by this assumption; the later disturbance analysis is what absorbs the resulting error. added (the last clause is our reading, not on the slide)

Exam recipe
  1. Draw the loop: trajectory generator, summing junction (desired \(-\) measured), controller, robot, feedback of \(q,\dot q\).
  2. Label open loop as “no measurement of the output”.
  3. State the error with its sign: \(E=q_d-q\).
  4. For “why does open loop fail?”: model error, disturbance and initial offset are never corrected.
  5. For “what does independent joint control assume?”: each joint is a single-input single-output problem, coupling ignored.
Worked example

Open loop on a unit-mass joint: the torque equals \(\ddot x_d\), so \(\ddot E=0\) and \(E(t)=E(0)+\dot E(0)\,t\). A joint that starts 0.1 rad off the plan with zero relative speed stays exactly 0.1 rad off for ever. With feedback (section 4) the same offset decays. checked

Typical question: “Explain, with block diagrams, why the inverse-dynamics torque alone is not enough.” Draw both diagrams from p.11 and p.12 and name three causes: wrong model, outside disturbance, wrong starting state. One sentence per cause earns the marks.

2 · The spring–mass–damper and its three cases pp. 16–26

Picture

A block of mass \(m\) on a spring \(k\) with a damper \(b\) (p.16). Pull it to \(x(0)=-1\), let go, and it returns to 0. How it returns depends on \(b\) against \(2\sqrt{mk}\): too much damping creeps, too little rings, exactly enough is the fastest return with no overshoot. This is the model of one joint for the rest of the week.

Derivation

The block obeys (p.16), a second-order, linear, constant-coefficient, homogeneous ODE:

\[ m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx=0 \]

Try \(x=e^{st}\). Every term carries \(e^{st}\), so the characteristic equation is (p.17)

\[ ms^2+bs+k=0,\qquad s_{1,2}=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4mk}}{2m} \]

The roots are the poles. The sign of \(b^2-4mk\) picks the case:

Case\(b^2-4mk\)RootsSolutionSlide
Over-damped (real, unequal)\(\gt 0\)\(s_1\ne s_2\), both real and negative\(A_1e^{s_1t}+A_2e^{s_2t}\)p.18
Under-damped (complex)\(\lt 0\)\(\lambda\pm i\mu\)\(e^{\lambda t}(A_1\cos\mu t+A_2\sin\mu t)\)p.21
Critically damped (real, equal)\(=0\)\(s_1=s_2=-\tfrac{b}{2m}\)\((A_1+A_2t)e^{-bt/2m}\)p.24

For the complex case, \(\lambda=-\dfrac{b}{2m}\) and \(\mu=\dfrac{\sqrt{4mk-b^2}}{2m}\) added (the slide leaves \(\lambda,\mu\) undefined). The constants \(A_1,A_2\) come from \(x(0)\) and \(\dot x(0)\):

\[ \text{real unequal: } x(0)=A_1+A_2,\ \dot x(0)=s_1A_1+s_2A_2;\quad \text{complex: } x(0)=A_1,\ \dot x(0)=\lambda A_1+\mu A_2;\quad \text{equal: } x(0)=A_1,\ \dot x(0)=s A_1+A_2 \]

Damping ratio and natural frequency of the plant added: \(\omega_n=\sqrt{k/m}\) rad/s and \(\zeta=\dfrac{b}{2\sqrt{mk}}\). Then \(\zeta\gt1\), \(=1\), \(\lt1\) match over-, critical and under-damped. The slides never define \(\zeta\) or \(\omega_n\); they are the standard shorthand and are used in section 5.

Exam recipe
  1. Write \(ms^2+bs+k=0\) and compute \(b^2-4mk\). Name the case, using strict inequalities (\(=0\) is critical, not “overdamped”).
  2. Compute the roots (\(\lambda\pm i\mu\) if complex).
  3. Write the matching general solution.
  4. Write the two initial-condition equations for \(x(0)\) and \(\dot x(0)\) and solve the 2×2 for \(A_1,A_2\).
  5. Sanity check: \(x(0)\) and \(\dot x(0)\) from your final formula; and \(x\to0\) as \(t\to\infty\).
Worked example: the three deck examples

All three use \(x(0)=-1,\ \dot x(0)=0\) and \(t\gt0\).

Over-damped, \(m=1,\ b=3,\ k=2\) (p.19). \(b^2-4mk=9-8=1\gt0\). Roots \(s=\dfrac{-3\pm1}{2}=-1,\,-2\). Conditions: \(A_1+A_2=-1\) and \(-A_1-2A_2=0\), so \(A_1=-2,\ A_2=1\):

\[ x(t)=-2e^{-t}+e^{-2t} \]

It rises monotonically from −1 to 0 with no overshoot. For instance \(x(1)=-2e^{-1}+e^{-2}=-0.6004\). checked

Under-damped, \(m=b=k=1\) (p.22). \(b^2-4mk=1-4=-3\lt0\). Roots \(s=-\tfrac12\pm\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i\), so \(\lambda=-\tfrac12,\ \mu=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\). Conditions: \(A_1=-1\) and \(-\tfrac{A_1}{2}+\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}A_2=0\), so \(A_2=-\tfrac1{\sqrt3}=-0.5774\):

\[ x(t)=-e^{-t/2}\Big[\cos\tfrac{\sqrt3\,t}{2}+\tfrac1{\sqrt3}\sin\tfrac{\sqrt3\,t}{2}\Big] \]

It overshoots: the first peak is \(+0.1630\) at \(t=2\pi/\sqrt3=3.628\) s, i.e. 16.3 % of the initial offset, then it undershoots to \(-0.027\) at 7.26 s. Here \(\zeta=0.5\). checked

Critically damped, \(m=1,\ b=4,\ k=4\) (p.25). \(b^2-4mk=0\), root \(s=-2\) (twice). Conditions: \(A_1=-1\) and \(-2A_1+A_2=0\), so \(A_2=-2\):

\[ x(t)=(-1-2t)\,e^{-2t} \]

checked All three solutions were also integrated numerically as ODEs and agree to \(10^{-8}\).

Which is fastest? Time to stay within 2 % of the starting offset: critical 2.92 s, over-damped 4.60 s, under-damped 8.08 s. Critical damping is the quickest return without overshoot. checked

Calculation question: “Given \(m,b,k\), classify and find \(x(t)\).” The classic slips are \(\lambda\) taken as \(-b/m\) (it is \(-b/2m\)), and solving the initial conditions with \(\dot x(0)=0\) as \(A_2=0\) (only true when \(\lambda=0\)). In a short answer, the roots’ real parts decide decay speed and their imaginary parts decide ringing.
Slide p.26 versus the three examples

The red hand-drawn curves on p.26 label the real-unequal curve as the slowest, with the complex curve overshooting widely. The exact solutions of pp. 19, 22 and 25 do not match that sketch: the over-damped example reaches 2 % at 4.60 s, faster than the under-damped one at 8.08 s. Trust the equations. The only message the sketch can be relied on for is: critical is fastest without overshoot; under-damped overshoots. checked

3 · Actuating the system: PD control pp. 27–32

Picture

Add an actuator that pushes the block with force \(f\) (p.28). Measure position \(x\) and velocity \(\dot x\), and let the actuator imitate an extra spring and damper wired to the target (p.30). Adding them to the real \(b\) and \(k\) moves the poles to wherever you want.

Derivation \[ m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx=f,\qquad f=-k_px-k_v\dot x \quad(\text{PD law, }\textbf{p.29}) \]

\(k_p\) is the position gain (N/m) and \(k_v\) the velocity gain (N·s/m). Substituting (p.32):

\[ m\ddot x+(b+k_v)\dot x+(k+k_p)x=0\ \Rightarrow\ m\ddot x+b'\dot x+k'x=0,\quad b'=b+k_v,\ k'=k+k_p \]

It is the same equation as before with new coefficients, so every result of section 2 applies with \(b',k'\). Critically damped when \(b'^2=4mk'\) (p.32).

Exam recipe
  1. Write the plant with \(f\) on the right: \(m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx=f\).
  2. Substitute the control law and collect \(\dot x\) and \(x\) terms.
  3. Read off \(b'\) and \(k'\). Never forget the plant’s own \(b\) and \(k\).
  4. Apply the section 2 test with \(b',k'\): critical when \(b'^2=4mk'\).
  5. Solve for the gain you were asked for.
Worked example

Plant \(m=2\) kg, \(b=3\) N·s/m, \(k=8\) N/m. Alone it is under-damped (\(9-64=-55\lt0\)). Choose \(k_p=10\) N/m. Then \(k'=18\). Critical needs \(b'=\sqrt{4mk'}=\sqrt{144}=12\), so \(k_v=b'-b=9\) N·s/m. The closed-loop poles are a double root at \(-b'/2m=-3\). checked

Trap: using the unit-mass shortcut \(k_v=2\sqrt{k_p}=6.32\) here. It is wrong, because this loop still contains \(m,b,k\); with 6.32 the system stays under-damped. The shortcut belongs to the partitioned controller of section 4. checked Also, PD alone gains depend on the plant; partitioning removes that dependence.

4 · Control-law partitioning: model-based plus servo pp. 33–37

Picture

Split the force into two parts (p.33): a model-based part that uses \(m,b,k\) to cancel the plant, and a servo part that does not. After cancelling, the joint looks like a unit mass, so the servo part is the same for any joint.

Derivation

Write the force as \(f=\alpha f'+\beta\) (p.33) with \(\alpha,\beta\) chosen so \(f'\) acts like the input of a unit mass. Put it into the plant (p.34):

\[ m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx=\alpha f'+\beta \quad\text{with}\quad \alpha=m,\ \ \beta=b\dot x+kx \ \Rightarrow\ \ddot x=f' \]

So \(f=m\,f'+b\dot x+kx\): \(m\) and \(b\dot x+kx\) are the model-based portion, \(f'\) is the servo portion (p.37). Now choose the servo law (p.36):

\[ f'=-k_v\dot x-k_px\ \Rightarrow\ \ddot x+k_v\dot x+k_px=0,\qquad s^2+k_vs+k_p=0 \]

These two terms are independent of \(m,b,k\), as the slide says. Compare with section 2 using \(m=1,\ b=k_v,\ k=k_p\): the discriminant is \(k_v^2-4k_p\), so

\[ \text{critical damping: } k_v^2=4k_p\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad k_v=2\sqrt{k_p} \]

Units added: after partitioning, \(f'\) is an acceleration, so \(k_p\) is in s\(^{-2}\) and \(k_v\) in s\(^{-1}\). (In the plain PD law of section 3 they are N/m and N·s/m.)

Pole placement added: for wanted poles \(p_1,p_2\), match \(s^2+k_vs+k_p=(s-p_1)(s-p_2)\), so \(k_v=-(p_1+p_2)\) and \(k_p=p_1p_2\).

If the model is wrong added (not on the slides). Suppose the controller uses estimates \(\hat m,\hat b,\hat k\). Then \(m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx=\hat m f'+\hat b\dot x+\hat kx\). With \(\hat m=m\) and \(\hat k=k\) but \(\hat b\ne b\), the loop becomes \(\ddot x+\big(k_v+\tfrac{b-\hat b}{m}\big)\dot x+k_px=0\): the damping gain is shifted by \((b-\hat b)/m\). With \(\hat b=b,\hat k=k\) and \(\hat m\ne m\), both gains are scaled by \(\hat m/m\). So the servo gains do not stay “independent of \(m,b,k\)” when the model is imperfect.

Slides vs textbook

Slide p.36 says “For a critically damped system, we can directly write \(k_p^2=4k_v\).” That has the subscripts swapped. For \(\ddot x+k_v\dot x+k_p x=0\) (and the handwritten \(s^2+k_vs+k_p=0\) on the same slide) the roots are equal exactly when \(k_v^2-4k_p=0\). The slide’s own p.32 condition \(b'^2=4mk'\) agrees with the correct form. Use \(k_v^2=4k_p\), i.e. \(k_v=2\sqrt{k_p}\). Example: \(k_p=25\) needs \(k_v=10\), not \(k_v=625/4\). With the printed formula, \(k_p=16\) would give \(k_v=64\): the roots are −63.75 and −0.25, a very slow over-damped response. checked An exam that quotes the slide formula in a statement to judge is testing this exact swap.

Exam recipe
  1. Write the plant \(m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx=f\) and set \(f=\alpha f'+\beta\).
  2. Match: \(\alpha=m\), \(\beta=b\dot x+kx\). Say “so \(\ddot x=f'\), a unit mass”.
  3. Servo law \(f'=-k_v\dot x-k_px\) (regulation) or the error form of section 6 (tracking).
  4. Closed loop \(\ddot x+k_v\dot x+k_px=0\). Critical when \(k_v^2=4k_p\).
  5. Final force: \(f=m f'+b\dot x+kx\). Label the two portions on your block diagram.
  6. Sanity check: \(\ddot x\) computed from the plant must equal \(f'\).
Worked example

Plant \(m=2,\ b=3,\ k=8\). Servo gains \(k_p=25,\ k_v=10\) (critical, since \(100=4\cdot25\)): double pole at \(-5\). At state \(x=0.5\) m, \(\dot x=1\) m/s:

\(f'=-10(1)-25(0.5)=-22.5\) m/s², then \(f=2(-22.5)+3(1)+8(0.5)=-45+3+4=-38\) N. Check: the plant gives \(\ddot x=(f-b\dot x-kx)/m=(-38-3-4)/2=-22.5=f'\) ✓. checked

Model error: if the controller uses \(\hat b=1\) while the true \(b=3\), the effective velocity gain is \(10+(3-1)/2=11\). If it uses \(\hat m=1.5\) while \(m=2\), the loop is \(s^2+7.5s+18.75\): \(56.25-75\lt0\), now under-damped. checked

The most likely question here is a spot-the-error or short question on \(k_v^2=4k_p\), plus “identify the model-based and servo portions on this block diagram”. A calculation question will give \(m,b,k\) and ask for \(f\) at a state, as above.

5 · Choosing the gains: \(\omega_n\) and \(\zeta\) added not on the slides; builds on p.36

Picture

The slides only say “choose \(k_p,k_v\) to tune the system”. Exam questions about choosing gains need two numbers: how fast (\(\omega_n\)) and how bouncy (\(\zeta\)). Think of \(k_p\) as the stiffness of a virtual spring on a unit mass, and \(k_v\) as the damper.

Derivation

Compare \(\ddot E+k_v\dot E+k_pE=0\) with the standard form \(\ddot E+2\zeta\omega_n\dot E+\omega_n^2E=0\):

\[ \omega_n=\sqrt{k_p}\ \text{rad/s},\qquad \zeta=\frac{k_v}{2\sqrt{k_p}},\qquad \text{poles } s=-\zeta\omega_n\pm\omega_n\sqrt{\zeta^2-1} \]

\(\zeta=1\) gives \(k_v=2\sqrt{k_p}\), matching section 4. \(\zeta\gt1\): over-damped (real poles). \(0\lt\zeta\lt1\): under-damped, poles \(-\zeta\omega_n\pm i\,\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\). \(\zeta=0\) (\(k_v=0\)): never decays.

For \(0\lt\zeta\lt1\) the first overshoot of a step-sized initial error is \(\exp\!\big(-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\big)\) of that error. This standard result is also not on the slides.

Exam recipe
  1. State what is wanted (critical, or a given \(\zeta\) and \(\omega_n\), or given poles).
  2. Critical with speed \(\omega_n\): \(k_p=\omega_n^2,\ k_v=2\omega_n\).
  3. Given \(\zeta\), \(\omega_n\): \(k_p=\omega_n^2,\ k_v=2\zeta\omega_n\).
  4. Given poles \(p_1,p_2\): \(k_p=p_1p_2,\ k_v=-(p_1+p_2)\).
  5. Check the roots of \(s^2+k_vs+k_p\) numerically.
Worked example

(a) Critical, \(\omega_n=10\) rad/s: \(k_p=100,\ k_v=20\). checked (b) \(\zeta=0.7,\ \omega_n=8\) rad/s: \(k_p=64,\ k_v=2(0.7)(8)=11.2\). checked (c) \(k_p=25,\ k_v=6\): \(\omega_n=5,\ \zeta=0.6\), poles \(-3\pm4i\), overshoot \(e^{-\pi(0.6)/0.8}=9.48\,\%\). checked (d) Poles at \(-2,-8\): \(k_p=16,\ k_v=10\), \(\zeta=1.25\), over-damped. checked

Possible short question: “doubling \(k_p\) with \(k_v\) fixed does what to \(\zeta\)?” It divides \(\zeta\) by \(\sqrt2\) (more bounce) and raises \(\omega_n\) by \(\sqrt2\). To keep \(\zeta\) fixed, \(k_v\) must grow with \(\sqrt{k_p}\).

6 · Trajectory following with servo error pp. 38–40

Picture

Holding a fixed position is only half the job. To follow a moving target \(x_d(t)\) the controller needs its speed and acceleration too, so the trajectory must be smooth and twice differentiable (p.38). The planned acceleration \(\ddot x_d\) is fed straight in (feed-forward); the spring and damper only fix the leftover error (p.40).

Derivation

With \(E=x_d-x\) and \(\dot E=\dot x_d-\dot x\), the servo law becomes (p.39)

\[ f'=\ddot x_d+k_v\dot E+k_pE \]

Since \(\ddot x=f'\) after partitioning, \(\ddot x=\ddot x_d+k_v\dot E+k_pE\). Move \(\ddot x_d-\ddot x=\ddot E\) to one side:

\[ \ddot E+k_v\dot E+k_pE=0 \]

“Second order in error space”: the same equation as section 4, but for the error, so any smooth trajectory is tracked with the same gains. The full force is \(f=m f'+b\dot x+kx\), as in the block diagram (p.40).

Without feed-forward added: dropping \(\ddot x_d\) gives \(\ddot E+k_v\dot E+k_pE=\ddot x_d\). For constant \(\ddot x_d=a\), the error settles at \(a/k_p\) instead of 0.

Exam recipe
  1. Define \(E=x_d-x\), \(\dot E=\dot x_d-\dot x\).
  2. Servo law: \(f'=\ddot x_d+k_v\dot E+k_pE\).
  3. Use \(\ddot x=f'\) and subtract: \(\ddot E+k_v\dot E+k_pE=0\).
  4. Critical: \(k_v^2=4k_p\). Then \(f=mf'+b\dot x+kx\).
  5. Name the three inputs you need from the trajectory: \(x_d,\dot x_d,\ddot x_d\).
Worked example

\(k_p=100,\ k_v=20\) (critical, \(\omega_n=10\)). An initial error \(E(0)=0.5\) m with \(\dot E(0)=0\) decays as \(E(t)=E_0(1+10t)e^{-10t}\); at \(t=0.5\) s, \(E=0.5\cdot6\cdot e^{-5}=0.0202\) m. checked With zero initial error, feed-forward keeps \(E\equiv0\) for any \(x_d\).

Without feed-forward on \(x_d=t^2\) m (\(a=2\) m/s²): \(E_{ss}=a/k_p=2/100=0.02\) m. checked

Scenario: “Why is \(\ddot x_d\) in the control law?” It cancels the planned acceleration so the error equation is homogeneous. Also expect “which quantities must the planner supply?”: position, velocity and acceleration, hence “smooth and twice differentiable”.

7 · Disturbance rejection and steady-state error pp. 41–43

Picture

An outside force \(f_{disturb}\) (a payload, a push, friction not in the model) enters at the force junction (p.41, red arrow, with a minus sign). A spring-like controller lets the joint sag until its restoring force balances the push. That leftover sag is the steady-state error.

Derivation

The plant now sees \(f-f_{disturb}\). After partitioning, \(m\ddot x=mf'-f_{disturb}\), so \(\ddot x=f'-f_{disturb}/m\). With the servo law of section 6 and \(E=x_d-x\) (p.42):

\[ \ddot E+k_v\dot E+k_pE=\frac{f_{disturb}}{m} \]

The sign: the disturbance subtracts from \(\ddot x\), and \(E=x_d-x\) flips it, giving a plus sign on the right. If \(f_{disturb}\) is bounded, \(E\) stays bounded (the bounded-input bounded-output principle on the slide).

For a constant \(f_{disturb}\), steady state means the derivatives vanish (p.43):

\[ k_pE=\frac{f_{disturb}}{m}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad E_{ss}=\frac{f_{disturb}}{k_p\,m} \]

The slide writes \(E=f_{disturb}/k_pm\), the same thing. Note what is absent: \(k_v,b,k\) do not matter. What matters: the disturbance size, the mass, and \(k_p\).

Exam recipe
  1. Write the error equation with \(f_{disturb}/m\) on the right.
  2. Set \(\ddot E=\dot E=0\).
  3. Solve: \(E_{ss}=f_{disturb}/(k_pm)\).
  4. To meet a tolerance: \(k_p\ge f_{disturb}/(m\,E_{max})\), then set \(k_v=2\sqrt{k_p}\) for critical damping.
  5. Name the limit: a PD law cannot make \(E_{ss}\) zero unless \(f_{disturb}=0\) or \(k_p\to\infty\).
Worked example

\(f_{disturb}=10\) N, \(m=2\) kg, \(k_p=25\): \(E_{ss}=10/(25\cdot2)=0.2\) m. With \(m=5\) kg: \(10/(25\cdot5)=0.08\) m. A full simulation of the real plant (\(m=2,b=3,k=8\)) with the partitioned law gives 0.2 m, and the same 0.2 m after changing \(b\) and \(k\). checked

Requirement \(E_{ss}\le5\) mm with \(m=2\) kg: \(k_p\ge10/(2\cdot0.005)=1000\) N/m, then \(k_v=2\sqrt{1000}=63.25\). checked Very high gains are not free in practice (noise, actuator limits, unmodelled dynamics), so integral action is preferred. added

Typical traps: “\(E_{ss}\) is independent of \(m\)” (false), “\(E_{ss}=f_{disturb}/k_v\)” (false), “increasing \(k_v\) reduces the steady error” (false: it only changes how fast, not where it ends). Calculation: the formula and a number as above.

8 · Integral action: PID and the third-order error equation pp. 44–45

Picture

An integral term adds up the error over time. While any error remains, the sum keeps growing and pushes harder. It only stops when the error is exactly zero. So against a constant push it can win completely, where a pure spring only compromises.

Derivation

Add the integral to the servo law (p.44), where \(k_i\) is the integral gain (s\(^{-3}\) once the plant is partitioned):

\[ f'=\ddot x_d+k_v\dot E+k_pE+k_i\!\int\!E\,dt\ \Rightarrow\ \ddot E+k_v\dot E+k_pE+k_i\!\int\!E\,dt=\frac{f_{disturb}}{m} \]

Differentiate once to remove the integral. The slide’s version (p.45), assuming \(E(t)=0\) for \(t\lt0\), is third order:

\[ \dddot E+k_v\ddot E+k_p\dot E+k_iE=\frac{\dot f_{disturb}}{m} \]

“Verify that at steady state \(E=0\)”: for a constant disturbance \(\dot f_{disturb}=0\) and all derivatives of \(E\) vanish, leaving \(k_iE=0\), so \(E=0\) (if \(k_i\ne0\)). In the integral form the integral itself settles to \(f_{disturb}/(m\,k_i)\): it has learned the disturbance.

Stability limit added: the characteristic polynomial is \(s^3+k_vs^2+k_ps+k_i\). With positive gains the roots are stable only if \(k_vk_p\gt k_i\) (Routh–Hurwitz). At \(k_i=k_vk_p\) it factors as \((s+k_v)(s^2+k_p)\): undamped oscillation. Too much integral gain destabilises the loop. The slide does not say this.

Exam recipe
  1. Write the PID law \(f'=\ddot x_d+k_v\dot E+k_pE+k_i\int E\,dt\).
  2. Error equation: add \(k_i\int E\,dt\) to the left; disturbance \(/m\) on the right.
  3. Differentiate: third order; characteristic polynomial \(s^3+k_vs^2+k_ps+k_i\).
  4. Constant disturbance, steady state: \(k_iE=0\Rightarrow E=0\).
  5. State the caveat: needs a stable loop, \(k_v k_p\gt k_i\).
Worked example

\(k_p=25,\ k_v=10,\ k_i=40\): \(k_vk_p=250\gt40\), stable. Roots of \(s^3+10s^2+25s+40\): \(-7.335\) and \(-1.332\pm1.918i\). On the real plant with \(f_{disturb}=10\) N, \(m=2\): \(E\to0\) and \(\int E\,dt\to10/(2\cdot40)=0.125\) m·s. With \(k_i=250\) the roots are \(-10,\ \pm5i\); with \(k_i=300\) the loop is unstable. checked

Spot-the-error favourite: “PD removes steady-state error” (false, see section 7) and “adding integral action never hurts” (false: \(k_i\lt k_vk_p\)). “Why third order?” because differentiating the integral raises the order by one. Do not claim anything about \(k_i\) the slides do not say beyond the added stability limit above.

9 · A joint with a DC motor and gearbox pp. 46–50

Picture

The torque the controller asks for is produced by an actuator, usually a DC motor (p.46). The motor has an electrical side (armature circuit \(V_a,R_a,L_a,i_a\), back-emf \(V_b\); field circuit \(V_f,R_f,L_f,i_f\) that generates \(B\)) and a mechanical side (rotor inertia \(I_m\), friction) (p.48). A gearbox then trades speed for torque, and from the link’s view the motor’s own inertia and friction look bigger.

Derivation

Armature current makes torque (p.49); friction takes some of it:

\[ Q_m=k_1Bi_a=k_4i_a,\qquad Q_{net}=Q_m-f_m,\qquad I_m\ddot q_m=k_4i_a-c_m\dot q_m \]

\(Q_m\) is the motor torque (N·m), \(k_4\) the torque constant (N·m/A), \(c_m\) the motor viscous-friction coefficient (N·m·s/rad). With a gear ratio \(\eta\) (p.50):

\[ Q=\eta Q_m,\qquad \dot q_{axle}=\frac1\eta\dot q_m,\qquad Q=(I+\eta^2I_m)\ddot q_{axle}+(c+\eta^2c_m)\dot q_{axle} \]

\(\eta\gt1\) means the motor turns \(\eta\) times faster than the axle. \(I,c\) are the link-side inertia and friction. Why \(\eta^2\)? The motor shaft moves \(\eta\) times faster, so its inertia torque is \(I_m\eta\ddot q_{axle}\), and a torque on the motor shaft counts \(\eta\) times at the axle: \(\eta\cdot\eta=\eta^2\). Derivation, using \(q_m=\eta q_{axle}\): motor torque needed \(=I_m\eta\ddot q+c_m\eta\dot q+(I\ddot q+c\dot q)/\eta\); multiply by \(\eta\). checked symbolically

Reading the symbols: on p.50 the left-hand \(Q\) is the motor’s torque as felt at the axle, \(\eta Q_m\) (our reading; the slide uses \(Q\) and \(\tau\) loosely and does not say so). The required motor torque is therefore \(Q_m=Q/\eta\).

Consequence added: with a large \(\eta\), the reflected term \(\eta^2 I_m\) can be as large as or larger than the link’s own \(I\), so the joint’s effective inertia changes little when the arm configuration changes \(I\).

Exam recipe
  1. Write the gear ratio convention: \(\dot q_m=\eta\dot q_{axle}\).
  2. Reflect: \(I_{eff}=I+\eta^2I_m\), \(c_{eff}=c+\eta^2c_m\).
  3. Axle torque needed: \(Q=I_{eff}\ddot q+c_{eff}\dot q\) at the planned \(\ddot q,\dot q\).
  4. Motor torque: \(Q_m=Q/\eta\). Current: \(i_a=Q_m/k_4\).
  5. Check units and that the motor speed is \(\eta\) times the axle speed.
Worked example

\(\eta=50,\ I_m=2\times10^{-5}\) kg·m², \(I=0.04\) kg·m², \(c=0.1,\ c_m=10^{-4}\) N·m·s/rad, \(k_4=0.04\) N·m/A. Plan: \(\ddot q=10\) rad/s², \(\dot q=2\) rad/s.

\(I_{eff}=0.04+2500(2\times10^{-5})=0.04+0.05=0.09\) kg·m². \(c_{eff}=0.1+2500(10^{-4})=0.35\) N·m·s/rad. \(Q=0.09(10)+0.35(2)=1.6\) N·m at the axle. \(Q_m=1.6/50=0.032\) N·m. \(i_a=0.032/0.04=0.8\) A. The motor turns at \(50\times2=100\) rad/s. checked

Note that the motor’s own inertia (0.05) exceeds the link’s (0.04) here.

Slide notation

On pp. 48–50 the motor torque appears as \(\tau_m\) and \(Q_m\), friction as \(f_m\) (a torque) and \(c_m\) (a coefficient). Use \(c_m\dot q_m\) in equations. p.51 “Bringing it all together” is blank: no slide combines the PID law with the motor model, so do not invent a combined formula.

Calculation: “Find the effective inertia at the axle for gear ratio \(\eta\).” The traps are \(\eta\) instead of \(\eta^2\), and dividing the wrong way. Conceptual: “why does a high gear ratio make the motor inertia matter?” because it is multiplied by \(\eta^2\).

Q3spot the error

“Adding an integral term always removes the steady-state error, so make \(k_i\) as large as possible.” What is wrong?

Answer

It removes the error for a constant disturbance only while the loop is stable. The polynomial \(s^3+k_vs^2+k_ps+k_i\) is stable only if \(k_vk_p\gt k_i\) (added result). A huge \(k_i\) makes the loop oscillate or diverge.
