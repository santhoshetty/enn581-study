Week 11 · Wheeled robot dynamics

What force and torque on the wheels produce which motion of the chassis?

Week 10 told you which chassis velocity a given wheel motion produces. Dynamics asks the reverse of cause and effect: which forces and torques accelerate the chassis. The deck applies Newton–Euler to one rigid planar body, packs the result into \(\mathbf M\dot{\boldsymbol\zeta}+c(\boldsymbol\zeta)={}^C\mathbf Q\), and shows how wheel forces build \({}^C\mathbf Q\). This tab rebuilds every line, fixes two slips in the slides (pp. 24–25 and p. 34), and ends with a simulator you drive with wheel torques.

Intuition first

Dynamics is the cause, kinematics the effect

W10 answered “if the chassis moves like this, how fast must each wheel turn?” W11 answers “how hard must the wheels push to make it move like this?” Like a shopping trolley: speed tells you where it goes, but only a push changes the speed.

One push, one twist

A rigid body obeys two laws: net force \(=m\times\)acceleration of the centre of mass, net torque \(=I\times\)angular acceleration. These are the same Newton and Euler equations as for an arm link in W7 (W7 p.34), now for one body in a plane.

Turning at constant speed still accelerates you

In a car on a bend you are pushed outward even at a steady 60 km/h. Your speed along the car’s nose is constant, but the nose direction keeps rotating, so the velocity vector changes. That is the \(u\dot\theta\) term: a sideways acceleration with no sideways speed.

Add up pushes with lever arms

Each wheel pushes forward at a distance from the axle midpoint. Sum the pushes for the net force. Sum push \(\times\) lever arm for the net twist. That sum is a matrix, and it is the transpose of the W10 wheel-speed matrix, because the power must be the same either way.

Wheels grip sideways like skate blades

A skate glides forward but resists sideways motion. That side grip is what bends the path into a circle. The slide-37 matrix lists only the forward pushes; the sideways force is a reaction that the no-slip rule supplies.

1 · The dynamics problem: forward and inverse pp. 9–11

Picture

Kinematics (W10) relates motions. Dynamics relates a motion to the forces and torques that cause it. For a planar robot the pose is \(\boldsymbol\xi=(x,y,\theta)^\top\) in the inertial frame, and the net force and torque on it, also in the inertial frame, are \(\mathbf Q=(f_x,f_y,\tau_z)^\top\) (p.9).

Derivation \[ \mathbf Q = f(\boldsymbol\xi,\dot{\boldsymbol\xi},\ddot{\boldsymbol\xi}) \]

Forward dynamics (p.10): given \(\mathbf Q\), find the trajectory \((\boldsymbol\xi,\dot{\boldsymbol\xi},\ddot{\boldsymbol\xi})\). Use: simulation and analysis. Inverse dynamics (p.11): given a desired trajectory, find the \(\mathbf Q\) needed. Use: control.

Both use the same equation. Forward solves for the acceleration and integrates; inverse evaluates the right-hand side along a known path.

Exam recipe
  1. Read which quantities are given. Forces or torques given and motion wanted: forward (integrate).
  2. Motion given and forces wanted: inverse (substitute, no integration).
  3. Name the frame of every vector: \(\mathbf Q\) in {O} is \({}^O\mathbf Q\); in the chassis it is \({}^C\mathbf Q\).
  4. Sanity check with a straight line: \(f=m\ddot x\).
Worked example

A 10 kg robot moves along x. Forward: \(f_x=10\) N gives \(\ddot x=f_x/m=1\) m/s². Inverse: a required path \(x=\tfrac12t^2\) has \(\ddot x=1\) m/s², so \(f_x=m\ddot x=10\) N.

Typical one-line question: “Is simulating the robot from known wheel torques forward or inverse dynamics?” (forward). “Which does a controller use to compute feed-forward torque?” (inverse). Beware the trap “inverse dynamics gives the trajectory”.

2 · Frames, symbols, and the W10 recap pp. 4–6, p. 12

Picture

Three frames (p.12): {O} fixed to the world (inertial); {C} attached to the chassis at a convenient reference point; {G} at the centre of mass, with the same orientation as {C} but a different origin. The chassis has mass \(m\) and a moment of inertia \({}^G I\) about the vertical axis through G. In {C}, G sits at \(({}^Cx_G,{}^Cy_G)\); in {C} the chassis velocity is \(\boldsymbol\zeta=(u,v,\dot\theta)^\top\), with \(u\) along \(\hat x_C\) and \(v\) along \(\hat y_C\).

Derivation: the W10 wheel model, recapped

The generalised wheel model (p.6) gives the angular velocity \(\dot\varphi_i\) of wheel \(i\) from the chassis velocity:

\[ \dot\varphi_i=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac1{r_i}&\tfrac{\tan\gamma_i}{r_i}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\cos\beta_i&\sin\beta_i\\-\sin\beta_i&\cos\beta_i\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&0&-{}^Cy_{W_i}\\0&1&{}^Cx_{W_i}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta&0\\-\sin\theta&\cos\theta&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\dot x\\\dot y\\\dot\theta\end{bmatrix} \]

\(r_i\): wheel radius (m). \(\gamma_i\): angle between the free-roll velocity and the normal to the wheel plane. \(\beta_i\): angle between the drive velocity and the chassis. \(({}^Cx_{W_i},{}^Cy_{W_i})\): wheel position in {C} (m). \(\theta\): chassis orientation (rad). The last 3×3 matrix is \(\mathbf J^{-1}=\mathbf J^\top\): it turns the global \((\dot x,\dot y,\dot\theta)\) into the chassis-frame \(\boldsymbol\zeta=(u,v,\dot\theta)\). The dynamics below works with \(\boldsymbol\zeta\).

For a standard fixed wheel at \((0,y_W)\) pointing forward (\(\gamma=0,\beta=0\)) this reduces to \(\dot\varphi=(u-y_W\dot\theta)/r\). added

Exam recipe
  1. Sketch {O}, {C}, {G}; mark \(\theta\) from \(\hat x_O\) to \(\hat x_C\), anticlockwise positive.
  2. Write \(\boldsymbol\xi\) (global pose) and \(\boldsymbol\zeta\) (chassis velocity) separately and say which frame each is in.
  3. State the link \(\dot{\boldsymbol\xi}=\mathbf J(\boldsymbol\xi)\boldsymbol\zeta\) with \(\mathbf J\) the rotation about z by \(\theta\) (p.30).
  4. Check \(\mathbf J^{-1}=\mathbf J^\top\) for a rotation.
Worked example

A differential drive: \(r=0.1\) m, wheels at \(y_W=\pm a=\pm0.2\) m (left is \(+y\)). At \(u=1\) m/s, \(\dot\theta=1.6\) rad/s: left \(\dot\varphi_L=(1-0.2\cdot1.6)/0.1=6.8\) rad/s, right \(\dot\varphi_R=(1+0.2\cdot1.6)/0.1=13.2\) rad/s. checked

The recap is the bridge: expect a question that first asks for the wheel speeds (W10 formula) and then for the wheel forces (this week). Keep \(\boldsymbol\xi\) (global) and \(\boldsymbol\zeta\) (chassis) separate: the single most common error is mixing them.

3 · Newton–Euler balance on the chassis pp. 14–17

Picture

Two sets of loads act on the chassis. The applied loads from the wheels, drawn at C: a force \(({}^Cf_x,{}^Cf_y)\) and a torque \({}^Cn_z\). The inertial loads at G: a force \(({}^CF_x,{}^CF_y)=m\,{}^C\ddot{\mathbf P}_G\) and a torque \({}^CN_z\). Newton–Euler says they balance. The twist: the applied force acts at C but the inertial force acts at G, so moving one to the other creates a moment.

Derivation

All terms are in {C} (p.15). Force balance:

\[ {}^C\mathbf F_G-{}^C\mathbf f_C=0\;\Rightarrow\;{}^CF_x={}^Cf_x,\quad {}^CF_y={}^Cf_y \]

Moment balance about G. The applied force acts at C, which is the vector \(-{}^C\mathbf P_{CG}\) from G, so it contributes the moment \((-{}^C\mathbf P_{CG})\times{}^C\mathbf f_C\):

\[ {}^CN_G-{}^Cn_C-(-{}^C\mathbf P_{CG})\times{}^C\mathbf f_C=0\;\Rightarrow\;{}^CN_z={}^Cn_z+\big({}^Cy_{CG}\,{}^Cf_x-{}^Cx_{CG}\,{}^Cf_y\big) \]

The z-component of \((-x,-y,0)\times(f_x,f_y,0)\) is \(-xf_y+yf_x\), which is the bracket. checked The slides write \({}^C\mathbf P_{CG}\) on p.15 and \({}^C\mathbf P_G\) later; they are the same vector (C to G, in {C}).

The inertial loads (p.17) come from Newton and Euler (the same laws as W7 p.34):

\[ {}^C\mathbf F_G=m\,{}^C\dot{\boldsymbol\upsilon}_G\;(F_x=m\,{}^C\ddot x_G,\;F_y=m\,{}^C\ddot y_G),\qquad {}^C\mathbf N_G={}^G\mathbf I\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}+\boldsymbol\omega\times{}^G\mathbf I\boldsymbol\omega\;\Rightarrow\;{}^CN_z={}^GI\,\ddot\theta \]

In planar motion \(\boldsymbol\omega\) is along z and so is \({}^G\mathbf I\boldsymbol\omega\), so the \(\boldsymbol\omega\times{}^G\mathbf I\boldsymbol\omega\) term vanishes. The slide’s handwritten note: \(m\) and \({}^GI\) are known; \(\ddot x_G,\ddot y_G,\ddot\theta\) are the unknowns. The one missing piece is the acceleration of G.

Exam recipe
  1. Draw applied loads at C and inertial loads at G, all in {C}.
  2. Write \(F_x=f_x\), \(F_y=f_y\).
  3. Write the moment about G, remembering the lever arm \(-\mathbf P_{CG}\) for the applied force.
  4. Replace the inertial loads by \(m\,\ddot{\mathbf P}_G\) and \({}^GI\ddot\theta\).
  5. Sanity check: if C = G the lever arm is zero and \({}^Cn_z={}^GI\ddot\theta\).
Worked example

G is 0.05 m ahead of C (\(x_G=0.05\), \(y_G=0\) m). Applied \({}^Cf=(10,4)\) N and \({}^Cn_z=0.4\) N·m. Moment about G: \({}^CN_z=0.4+(0\cdot10-0.05\cdot4)=0.2\) N·m. The sideways push 0.05 m ahead of G produces a clockwise moment about G that reduces the net twist. checked

Calculation or sketch. Marks go to the free-body figure and to the moment-transfer sign. A common slip is to treat the applied torque as acting about G without the \(y f_x-x f_y\) term.

4 · The acceleration of the centre of mass in a rotating frame pp. 19–24

Picture

The chassis frame turns with the robot, so the unit vectors \(\hat x_C,\hat y_C\) rotate at \(\dot\theta\): \(\dot{\hat x}=\dot\theta\,\hat y\) and \(\dot{\hat y}=-\dot\theta\,\hat x\). A velocity written as \(u\hat x+v\hat y\) therefore changes even when \(u\) and \(v\) do not. The \(u\hat x\) part swings towards \(+\hat y\); the \(v\hat y\) part swings towards \(-\hat x\).

Derivation (the slides, then an independent check)

Position of G in {O} (p.19): \({}^O\mathbf P_G={}^O\mathbf P_C+{}^O\mathbf R_C\,{}^C\mathbf P_G\). G is fixed in the chassis, so \({}^C\dot{\mathbf P}_G=0\) and \({}^O\dot{\mathbf R}_C\,\mathbf p={}^O\boldsymbol\omega_C\times{}^O\mathbf R_C\,\mathbf p\). Rotating the result into {C} (p.20):

\[ {}^O\dot{\mathbf P}_G={}^O\mathbf R_C\big[{}^C\dot{\mathbf P}_C+{}^C\boldsymbol\omega_C\times{}^C\mathbf P_G\big] \]

Differentiate once more with the product rule (p.22). The term \({}^C\boldsymbol\omega_C\times{}^C\dot{\mathbf P}_G\) is zero, and the derivative of \({}^O\mathbf R_C\) again produces \(\boldsymbol\omega\times\). Rotating into {C}:

\[ {}^C\ddot{\mathbf P}_G={}^C\ddot{\mathbf P}_C+{}^C\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}_C\times{}^C\mathbf P_{CG}+{}^C\boldsymbol\omega_C\times{}^C\dot{\mathbf P}_C+{}^C\boldsymbol\omega_C\times{}^C\boldsymbol\omega_C\times{}^C\mathbf P_G \]

Here \({}^C\dot{\mathbf P}_C=(u,v,0)\) and \({}^C\ddot{\mathbf P}_C=(\dot u,\dot v,0)\) are just the rates of change of the components. The turning of the frame is carried by the \(\boldsymbol\omega\times\) terms. Insert \(\boldsymbol\omega=(0,0,\dot\theta)\), \(\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}=(0,0,\ddot\theta)\), \({}^C\mathbf P_G=(x_G,y_G,0)\) (p.24), using \((0,0,k)\times(a,b,0)=(-kb,\,ka,\,0)\):

\[ \dot{\boldsymbol\omega}\times\mathbf P_G=\ddot\theta\begin{bmatrix}-y_G\\x_G\\0\end{bmatrix},\quad \boldsymbol\omega\times\dot{\mathbf P}_C=\dot\theta\begin{bmatrix}-v\\u\\0\end{bmatrix},\quad \boldsymbol\omega\times\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf P_G=\dot\theta^2\begin{bmatrix}-x_G\\-y_G\\0\end{bmatrix} \] \[ {}^C\ddot x_G=\dot u-{}^Cy_G\ddot\theta-v\dot\theta-{}^Cx_G\dot\theta^2,\qquad {}^C\ddot y_G=\dot v+{}^Cx_G\ddot\theta+\boxed{u}\,\dot\theta-{}^Cy_G\dot\theta^2 \]

Cross-check with unit vectors alone: \(\mathbf v_C=u\hat x+v\hat y\), so \(\mathbf a_C=\dot u\hat x+\dot v\hat y+u\dot\theta\hat y-v\dot\theta\hat x=(\dot u-v\dot\theta)\hat x+(\dot v+u\dot\theta)\hat y\). checked symbolically (checks/w11.py derives the full G acceleration from \(\mathbf R(\theta(t))\))

Slides vs derivation

Pages 24 and 25 print \(+v\dot\theta\) in the y-acceleration (\({}^C\ddot y_G=\dot v+{}^Cx_G\ddot\theta+v\dot\theta-{}^Cy_G\dot\theta^2\), and the matching \({}^CF_y\), \({}^Cf_y\)). The correct term is \(+u\dot\theta\). Three independent reasons: (1) the cross product on p.24 itself, \((0,0,\dot\theta)\times(u,v,0)=(-v\dot\theta,\,u\dot\theta,\,0)\), has \(u\dot\theta\) in y; (2) the p.26 matrix already uses \(u\) (row 2 \(m\dot\theta(u-{}^Cy_G\dot\theta)\), row 3 \(m\dot\theta({}^Cx_Gu+{}^Cy_Gv)\)), and the printed p.25 form does not reproduce it (checked symbolically); (3) physics: constant \(u\) on a circle with \(v=0\) needs the centripetal acceleration \(u\dot\theta\) in y, and the printed form gives zero. The x-equation (\(-v\dot\theta\)) is right. Write \(u\dot\theta\) in the exam. checked

Exam recipe
  1. Write \({}^C\ddot{\mathbf P}_G\) as the four-term sum (acceleration of C components, \(\dot\omega\times P\), \(\omega\times\dot P_C\), \(\omega\times\omega\times P\)).
  2. Substitute the vectors; evaluate each cross product with \((0,0,k)\times(a,b,0)=(-kb,ka,0)\).
  3. Collect x and y. Memorise the pattern: x gets \(-v\dot\theta\), y gets \(+u\dot\theta\) (the velocity components swap and the sign flips on the first).
  4. Check: if C = G and \(\ddot\theta=0\), \(\dot u=\dot v=0\), the acceleration is \(\dot\theta\,(-v,u)\), perpendicular to the velocity.
Worked example

C = G, \(u=2\) m/s, \(v=0\), \(\dot\theta=0.5\) rad/s, everything else zero. Then \({}^C\ddot x_G=0\), \({}^C\ddot y_G=u\dot\theta=1.0\) m/s², so a 10 kg chassis needs \({}^Cf_y=10\) N sideways. With G 0.05 m ahead of C the x-equation adds \(-x_G\dot\theta^2=-0.0125\) m/s². checked

A calculation or derivation question, and a spot-the-error one: this is where the printed slide is wrong, so a marker comparing against the slide may expect \(v\dot\theta\). Show the cross-product line so your \(u\dot\theta\) is justified.

5 · Equations of motion in matrix form: \(\mathbf M\dot{\boldsymbol\zeta}+c(\boldsymbol\zeta)={}^C\mathbf Q\) pp. 25–27

Picture

Rearranged as “mass times acceleration, plus velocity terms, equals the applied load”: the same shape as an arm’s \(\mathbf M(\mathbf q)\ddot{\mathbf q}+\dots=\boldsymbol\tau\) from W7. \(\mathbf M\) carries mass and geometry. \(c(\boldsymbol\zeta)\) carries the velocity-dependent (centripetal and Coriolis) terms that exist because the frame rotates. \({}^C\mathbf Q\) is what the wheels apply.

Derivation

Equate applied to inertial loads (p.25, with the corrected \(u\dot\theta\)):

\[ {}^Cf_x=m\big(\dot u-{}^Cy_G\ddot\theta-v\dot\theta-{}^Cx_G\dot\theta^2\big),\qquad {}^Cf_y=m\big(\dot v+{}^Cx_G\ddot\theta+u\dot\theta-{}^Cy_G\dot\theta^2\big) \] \[ {}^Cn_z={}^CN_z-\big({}^Cy_G\,{}^Cf_x-{}^Cx_G\,{}^Cf_y\big)={}^GI\ddot\theta-\big({}^Cy_G\,{}^Cf_x-{}^Cx_G\,{}^Cf_y\big) \]

Substitute \(f_x,f_y\) into the moment equation. The coefficient of \(\ddot\theta\) is \({}^GI+m({}^Cx_G^2+{}^Cy_G^2)\). Collect the terms in \(\dot u,\dot v,\ddot\theta\) (p.26):

\[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}m&0&-m\,{}^Cy_G\\0&m&m\,{}^Cx_G\\-m\,{}^Cy_G&m\,{}^Cx_G&{}^GI+m({}^Cx_G^2+{}^Cy_G^2)\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf M}\begin{bmatrix}\dot u\\\dot v\\\ddot\theta\end{bmatrix}+\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}-m\dot\theta\,(v+{}^Cx_G\dot\theta)\\m\dot\theta\,(u-{}^Cy_G\dot\theta)\\m\dot\theta\,({}^Cx_G\,u+{}^Cy_G\,v)\end{bmatrix}}_{c(\boldsymbol\zeta)}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}{}^Cf_x\\{}^Cf_y\\{}^Cn_z\end{bmatrix}}_{{}^C\mathbf Q} \]

checked symbolically Reading the pieces. \(\mathbf M\) is constant and symmetric; its lower-right entry is the inertia about C by the parallel-axis theorem. \(c\) is zero when \(\boldsymbol\zeta=0\), so at rest \(\dot{\boldsymbol\zeta}=\mathbf M^{-1}\,{}^C\mathbf Q\). Everything is written in {C} (the boxed note on p.26). Homework on p.27: derive the same equations with Lagrange’s method. The result must match, since both describe the same body. added (the Lagrange route is not worked in the deck)

Special case C = G (\({}^Cx_G={}^Cy_G=0\)): \(\mathbf M=\mathrm{diag}(m,m,{}^GI)\) and \(c=(-mv\dot\theta,\;mu\dot\theta,\;0)^\top\). The diagonal \(\mathbf M\) means no coupling between force and torque. added

Exam recipe
  1. Start from the two component equations and the moment equation.
  2. Substitute \(f_x,f_y\) into \(n_z\) before collecting terms.
  3. Read \(\mathbf M\) from the coefficients of \(\dot u,\dot v,\ddot\theta\), and \(c\) from everything left (velocity terms).
  4. Check: \(\mathbf M\) symmetric; \(\mathbf M_{33}=I_G+m(x_G^2+y_G^2)\); \(c=0\) at rest.
  5. To find accelerations: \(\dot{\boldsymbol\zeta}=\mathbf M^{-1}({}^C\mathbf Q-c)\).
Worked example: off-centre COM

\(m=10\) kg, \({}^GI=0.25\) kg·m², G is 0.05 m ahead of C (\(x_G=0.05\), \(y_G=0\) m). At rest (\(c=0\)) the wheels apply \({}^Cf=(10,0)\) N and \({}^Cn_z=0.4\) N·m. Inertia about C: \(0.25+10(0.05)^2=0.275\) kg·m². The equations:

\[ \begin{bmatrix}10&0&0\\0&10&0.5\\0&0.5&0.275\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\dot u\\\dot v\\\ddot\theta\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}10\\0\\0.4\end{bmatrix} \]

Row 1: \(\dot u=1.0\) m/s². Row 2: \(\dot v=-0.05\,\ddot\theta\). Row 3: \(0.5(-0.05\ddot\theta)+0.275\ddot\theta=0.25\ddot\theta=0.4\), so \(\ddot\theta=1.6\) rad/s² and \(\dot v=-0.08\) m/s². C accelerates sideways because it swings about G (\(a_{C,y}=-x_G\ddot\theta\)). The naive \({}^Cn_z/0.275=1.4545\) rad/s² ignores the coupling and is wrong. checked

Calculation (solve a 3×3 at rest) or explanation (“what does each term mean, and what vanishes when C = G?”). Frequent trap: using \({}^GI\) as the third diagonal entry when C ≠ G.

6 · From the chassis frame to the inertial frame pp. 28–34

Picture

The chassis form is convenient for simulation, but a planner or a world-frame controller works with \((\boldsymbol\xi,\dot{\boldsymbol\xi},\ddot{\boldsymbol\xi})\) and a force \({}^O\mathbf Q\) in world axes. Two ingredients convert it (p.28): a way to write \(\boldsymbol\zeta,\dot{\boldsymbol\zeta}\) in terms of \(\boldsymbol\xi\) and its derivatives, and a relation between \({}^C\mathbf Q\) and \({}^O\mathbf Q\).

Derivation

Velocities (p.30): from W10, \(\dot{\boldsymbol\xi}=\mathbf J(\boldsymbol\xi)\boldsymbol\zeta\) with \(\mathbf J\) the rotation about z by \(\theta\) (the slide’s “\(\mathbf J(\eta)\)” is a typo for \(\mathbf J(\boldsymbol\xi)\)). Differentiate:

\[ \ddot{\boldsymbol\xi}=\dot{\mathbf J}\boldsymbol\zeta+\mathbf J\dot{\boldsymbol\zeta}\;\Rightarrow\;\dot{\boldsymbol\zeta}=\mathbf J^{-1}\big[\ddot{\boldsymbol\xi}-\dot{\mathbf J}\boldsymbol\zeta\big]=\mathbf J^{-1}\big[\ddot{\boldsymbol\xi}-\dot{\mathbf J}\mathbf J^{-1}\dot{\boldsymbol\xi}\big] \]

Loads (p.32): power is the same in any frame, \({}^O\mathbf Q\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol\xi}={}^C\mathbf Q\cdot\boldsymbol\zeta\). Insert \(\dot{\boldsymbol\xi}=\mathbf J\boldsymbol\zeta\): \({}^O\mathbf Q^\top\mathbf J\boldsymbol\zeta={}^C\mathbf Q^\top\boldsymbol\zeta\) for every \(\boldsymbol\zeta\), hence

\[ \mathbf J(\boldsymbol\xi)^\top\,{}^O\mathbf Q={}^C\mathbf Q \]

This is the same “transpose of the velocity map maps loads back” rule as \(\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf J^\top\mathbf F\) for arms (statics, W6 p.58). For a rotation \(\mathbf J^\top=\mathbf J^{-1}\), so \({}^O\mathbf Q=\mathbf J\,{}^C\mathbf Q\): rotate the load by \(\theta\). added

Plug in (p.34). Substitute \(\dot{\boldsymbol\zeta}\) and \({}^C\mathbf Q\) into \(\mathbf M\dot{\boldsymbol\zeta}+c=\,{}^C\mathbf Q\) and multiply by \((\mathbf J^\top)^{-1}\):

\[ {}^O\mathbf M\,\ddot{\boldsymbol\xi}+{}^Oc(\boldsymbol\xi,\dot{\boldsymbol\xi})={}^O\mathbf Q,\qquad {}^O\mathbf M=(\mathbf J^\top)^{-1}\mathbf M\,\mathbf J^{-1},\quad {}^Oc=(\mathbf J^\top)^{-1}\Big[c-\mathbf M\,\mathbf J^{-1}\dot{\mathbf J}\,\mathbf J^{-1}\dot{\boldsymbol\xi}\Big] \]

Entirely in terms of \(\boldsymbol\xi,\dot{\boldsymbol\xi},\ddot{\boldsymbol\xi}\), as the slide says. checked numerically on random poses, velocities and COM offsets

Special case C = G: \((\mathbf J^\top)^{-1}=\mathbf J\), so \({}^O\mathbf M=\mathbf J\,\mathrm{diag}(m,m,{}^GI)\,\mathbf J^\top=\mathrm{diag}(m,m,{}^GI)\), and \({}^Oc=0\). A free rigid body obeys \(m\ddot x=f_x\), \(m\ddot y=f_y\), \({}^GI\ddot\theta=\tau_z\) in world axes, with no velocity terms. The Coriolis-like terms of the chassis form exist only because the chassis axes rotate. checked added

Slides vs derivation

Page 34, third line, drops a \(\mathbf J^{-1}\). The slide prints \({}^Oc=(\mathbf J^\top)^{-1}[c-\mathbf M\,\dot{\mathbf J}\,\mathbf J^{-1}\dot{\boldsymbol\xi}]\). Expanding line 2 of the same slide, \(\mathbf M\mathbf J^{-1}(\ddot{\boldsymbol\xi}-\dot{\mathbf J}\mathbf J^{-1}\dot{\boldsymbol\xi})\), gives the subtracted term \(\mathbf M\,\mathbf J^{-1}\dot{\mathbf J}\,\mathbf J^{-1}\dot{\boldsymbol\xi}\), with an extra \(\mathbf J^{-1}\) in front of \(\dot{\mathbf J}\). The two agree only at \(\theta=0\) (where \(\mathbf J=\mathbf I\)). Test: C = G, \(m=10\), \(\theta=90^\circ\), \(u=1\), \(v=0\), \(\dot\theta=2\): the correct \({}^Oc=0\) (free body), the printed formula gives \((-20,\,20,\,0)\). The p.30 equations are correct. checked

Exam recipe

7 · Using the model: forward simulation p. 35

Picture

Wheel-ground forces are most natural in the chassis frame, so simulate there (p.35). Start from a known pose and velocity, with the wheel forces given. Each step: compute \(\dot{\boldsymbol\zeta}\), integrate to get \(\boldsymbol\zeta\), rotate to \(\dot{\boldsymbol\xi}\), integrate to get \(\boldsymbol\xi\). The simulator above does exactly this.

Derivation \[ \dot{\boldsymbol\zeta}=\mathbf M^{-1}\big[{}^C\mathbf Q-c(\boldsymbol\zeta)\big],\qquad \boldsymbol\zeta\leftarrow\int\dot{\boldsymbol\zeta}\,\mathrm dt,\qquad \dot{\boldsymbol\xi}=\mathbf J(\boldsymbol\xi)\boldsymbol\zeta,\qquad \boldsymbol\xi\leftarrow\int\dot{\boldsymbol\xi}\,\mathrm dt \]

The slide says “integrate”; the simulator uses fourth-order Runge–Kutta (RK4) with a 5 ms step, which for this smooth model is far more accurate than explicit Euler. One explicit Euler step is \(\boldsymbol\zeta_{k+1}=\boldsymbol\zeta_k+\Delta t\,\dot{\boldsymbol\zeta}_k\). added

Exam recipe
  1. Compute \(c(\boldsymbol\zeta)\) from the current velocity; at rest it is 0.
  2. Solve \(\mathbf M\dot{\boldsymbol\zeta}={}^C\mathbf Q-c\) (diagonal \(\mathbf M\): divide).
  3. Update \(\boldsymbol\zeta\), then rotate by \(\mathbf J(\theta)\) for the world velocity.
  4. Update \(\boldsymbol\xi\). Check \(\theta\) is in radians for the rotation.
Worked example

From rest with \(\dot{\boldsymbol\zeta}=(1,0,1.6)\) (section 8), one Euler step of 0.1 s gives \(\boldsymbol\zeta_1=(0.1,0,0.16)\). The pose is still the origin after step 1, because \(\boldsymbol\zeta_0=0\) means \(\dot{\boldsymbol\xi}_0=0\). checked

8 · From wheel forces to chassis loads: the differential drive pp. 36–37

Picture

“Where does the torque on the chassis come from?” (p.36): from the wheel forces at their lever arms. A differential drive has two driven wheels a distance \(2a\) apart, with C and G both at the midpoint of the axle (p.37). \(F_1\) acts on the left wheel (\(+y\)) and \(F_2\) on the right (\(-y\)), both along \(+x\). The figure also shows a small passive wheel at the front (a castor): it carries load but is not driven and does not appear in the equations.

Derivation

Each force along \(+x\) contributes \(f_x\). The moment about the origin is \(n_z=x\,f_y-y\,f_x\): \(F_1\) at \(y=+a\) gives \(-aF_1\), \(F_2\) at \(y=-a\) gives \(+aF_2\). Hence (p.37, the handwritten matrix):

\[ \begin{bmatrix}{}^Cf_x\\{}^Cf_y\\{}^Cn_z\end{bmatrix}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&0\\-a&a\end{bmatrix}}_{3\times2}\begin{bmatrix}F_1\\F_2\end{bmatrix} \]

The slide stops here. Connect it to the rest. A wheel torque \(\tau_i\) gives a force \(F_i=\tau_i/r\) at the ground (wheel inertia neglected). The W10 wheel-speed map (section 2) for the two wheels is \(\dot{\boldsymbol\varphi}=\mathbf J_w\boldsymbol\zeta\), \(\mathbf J_w=\tfrac1r\begin{bmatrix}1&0&-a\\1&0&a\end{bmatrix}\) (left, right rows). Then \({}^C\mathbf Q=\mathbf J_w^\top\boldsymbol\tau\): the p.37 matrix is \(r\mathbf J_w^\top\) applied to \(F=\tau/r\). This follows because power is conserved: \(\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol\varphi}=\boldsymbol\tau^\top\mathbf J_w\boldsymbol\zeta={}^C\mathbf Q\cdot\boldsymbol\zeta\). checked added

With C = G the equations of motion (section 5) become

\[ m(\dot u-v\dot\theta)=F_1+F_2,\qquad m(\dot v+u\dot\theta)=f_y,\qquad {}^GI\,\ddot\theta=a(F_2-F_1) \]

The p.37 matrix has \(f_y=0\): the two driven wheels push only forward. The no-slip rule \(v=0\) from W10 (a wheel cannot slide along its axle) then gives \(\dot v=0\) and so requires a sideways reaction \(f_y=mu\dot\theta\) from the tyres. The model reduces to

\[ \dot u=\frac{F_1+F_2}{m},\qquad \ddot\theta=\frac{a\,(F_2-F_1)}{{}^GI},\qquad f_y^{\text{required}}=m\,u\,\dot\theta \]

If you literally set \(f_y=0\) and keep \(v\) free (the simulator’s “lateral grip” unticked), you get \(\dot v=-u\dot\theta\): the body drifts sideways like a puck. Real wheels do not, so the grip version describes a real differential drive.

Slide gap (not an error)

The deck never states the no-slip constraint inside the dynamics. The p.37 matrix lists only the forward drive forces; the lateral force that keeps \(v=0\) is a constraint reaction, absent from \({}^C\mathbf Q\). If a question gives wheel forces and asks for \(\dot u,\dot v,\ddot\theta\), read whether the robot is allowed to slide: with no-slip, \(\dot v=0\); free-body, use \(\mathbf M\dot{\boldsymbol\zeta}+c=\mathbf Q\) with \(f_y=0\). For ordinary wheels use no-slip and say so.

Exam recipe
  1. Draw the top view; mark C, G, \(\pm a\), forces \(F_1\) (left, \(+y\)) and \(F_2\) (right, \(-y\)).
  2. Write \(f_x=F_1+F_2\), \(f_y=0\), \(n_z=a(F_2-F_1)\) (the matrix).
  3. If given torques, convert \(F=\tau/r\).
  4. With C = G and no slip: \(\dot u=f_x/m\), \(\ddot\theta=n_z/{}^GI\), \(v=0\).
  5. Check the sign: the right wheel pushing harder turns the robot left (positive \(\dot\theta\)). Check power: \(\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol\varphi}=f_xu+n_z\dot\theta\).
Worked example: full numbers (no numbers in the deck; mine)

Parameters: \(m=10\) kg, \({}^GI=0.25\) kg·m², \(r=0.1\) m, \(a=0.2\) m (track \(2a=0.4\) m), C = G, no wheel inertia. Wheel torques \(\tau_L=0.4\) and \(\tau_R=0.6\) N·m, from rest.

Forces: \(F_1=\tau_L/r=4\) N, \(F_2=\tau_R/r=6\) N. Wrench: \(f_x=4+6=10\) N, \(f_y=0\), \(n_z=0.2(6-4)=0.4\) N·m.

At rest \(c=0\): \(\dot u=10/10=1.0\) m/s², \(\dot v=0\) (no slip), \(\ddot\theta=0.4/0.25=1.6\) rad/s². checked

Because both accelerations are constant, \(u=t\) and \(\dot\theta=1.6t\), so the path radius \(R=u/\dot\theta=0.625\) m never changes: the robot drives a circle of radius 0.625 m (centre 0.625 m to its left), speeding up. Generally \(R=\dfrac{{}^GI\,f_x}{m\,n_z}\). checked (also against the exact solution \(x=R\sin\theta\), \(y=R(1-\cos\theta)\))

At \(t=1\) s: \(u=1\) m/s, \(\dot\theta=1.6\) rad/s. Wheel speeds \(\dot\varphi_L=(1-0.32)/0.1=6.8\) and \(\dot\varphi_R=(1+0.32)/0.1=13.2\) rad/s. Sideways force to hold \(v=0\): \(mu\dot\theta=16\) N; if free, \(\dot v=-u\dot\theta=-1.6\) m/s². Power: \(0.4(6.8)+0.6(13.2)=10.64\) W \(=f_xu+n_z\dot\theta=10(1)+0.4(1.6)\). checked

At \(t=2\) s the simulator gives \(u=2.0\) m/s, \(\dot\theta=3.2\) rad/s, \(\theta=3.2\) rad, \((x,y)=(-0.036,\,1.249)\) m, matching the exact circle. checked (widget test vectors: checks/w11_vectors.json)

Calculation: “given \(\tau_L,\tau_R\), \(r\), \(a\), \(m\), \(I\), find \(\dot u\) and \(\ddot\theta\)”. Marks for \(F=\tau/r\), the matrix, and the lever arm \(a\). Trap: using \(2a\) as the lever arm (doubles \(\ddot\theta\)) or swapping the sign so a harder right push turns the robot right.

9 · Swedish-wheel robot, castors, and the summary pp. 38–39

Picture

Page 38 is a figure only: a chassis with four Swedish (mecanum-type) wheels, forces \(F_1\)–\(F_4\) drawn along the roller axes at about \(\pm45^\circ\) to the chassis, offsets labelled \(a\) and \(b\), and {C, G} at the centre. No equation is on the slide; the matrix is left as the exercise “write \({}^C\mathbf Q\) as a function of the wheel forces”, like the differential drive. A castor (p.37) is a free-swivelling wheel: it transmits no drive force, so it adds nothing to \({}^C\mathbf Q\).

Method (mine; the slide gives none)

Each wheel \(i\) pushes with force \(F_i\) along a unit vector \(\mathbf d_i=(d_x,d_y)\) at position \((x_i,y_i)\) in {C}. Its column in the 3×4 matrix is

\[ \begin{bmatrix}d_x\\d_y\\x_i d_y-y_i d_x\end{bmatrix} \]

Illustration under my own assumptions (the slide’s \(a,b\) are not dimensioned precisely): wheels at \((\pm b,\pm a)\), drive directions \((1,1)/\sqrt2\) for \(F_1,F_3\) and \((1,-1)/\sqrt2\) for \(F_2,F_4\), as drawn. With \(s=1/\sqrt2\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix}{}^Cf_x\\{}^Cf_y\\{}^Cn_z\end{bmatrix}=s\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1&1\\1&-1&1&-1\\b-a&b-a&a-b&a-b\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}F_1\\F_2\\F_3\\F_4\end{bmatrix} \]

Four wheel forces, three chassis loads: a 3×4 matrix of rank 3 (for \(a\ne b\)) has a one-dimensional null space, so one combination of wheel forces (internal forces that fight each other) produces zero net load. Equal forces on all four give a pure forward force \(4sF\). checked

Summary (p.39): the dynamics equation in the body frame, \(\mathbf M\dot{\boldsymbol\zeta}+c=\,{}^C\mathbf Q\); and in the inertial frame, \({}^O\mathbf M\ddot{\boldsymbol\xi}+{}^Oc={}^O\mathbf Q\), linked by \({}^C\mathbf Q=\mathbf J^\top\,{}^O\mathbf Q\).

Exam recipe (any wheeled robot)
  1. For each driven wheel, write position \((x_i,y_i)\) in {C} and the unit direction of its force.
  2. Column \(=(d_x,\,d_y,\,x_id_y-y_id_x)^\top\); stack to get \({}^C\mathbf Q=\mathbf B\mathbf F\).
  3. Count wheels versus the three chassis degrees of freedom: more wheels than 3 means redundancy and a null space.
  4. Check with equal forces (net torque zero for a symmetric layout).
Likely as an explain or sketch question: build \(\mathbf B\) for a given layout, say what its rank and shape mean. The matrix \(\mathbf B\) is the transpose of the W10 wheel-speed matrix (up to the factor \(r\)).

P11.5 · Forward versus inverse, and why the chassis frameexplain

(a) State forward and inverse dynamics and give one use of each. (b) Why does the deck simulate in the chassis frame, and what does it cost? (c) What does \(c(\boldsymbol\zeta)\) represent, and when is it zero?

Model answer

(a) Forward: given forces and torques, find the motion (simulation, analysis). Inverse: given a desired motion, find the forces and torques (control, feed-forward). (b) Wheel–ground forces are naturally expressed in the chassis frame, so \({}^C\mathbf Q\) is easy to write, and \(\mathbf M\) is constant. The cost: velocity terms \(c(\boldsymbol\zeta)\) appear and you must rotate with \(\mathbf J(\theta)\) to get world coordinates. (c) \(c\) holds centripetal/Coriolis effects due to the rotating frame; it is zero when \(\boldsymbol\zeta=0\) (and, for C = G, its third entry is always zero).

P11.9 · Swedish-wheel matrix: redundancyexplain

For the four-Swedish-wheel robot of p.38 the map from wheel forces to chassis loads is 3×4. What does that shape imply, and how would you find a wheel-force set that exerts no net load?

Model answer

Three chassis loads, four wheel forces: there are more actuators than degrees of freedom, so the map \(\mathbf B\) (3×4, rank 3 for a sensible layout) has a one-dimensional null space. Solve \(\mathbf B\mathbf F=0\) for \(\mathbf F\neq0\): forces that cancel and only squeeze the wheels against the floor (internal forces). Any chassis load is reachable with infinitely many force sets, so a controller has a free choice, for example the minimum-norm solution \(\mathbf F=\mathbf B^\top(\mathbf B\mathbf B^\top)^{-1}\,{}^C\mathbf Q\). added (the minimum-norm choice is not in the deck)
