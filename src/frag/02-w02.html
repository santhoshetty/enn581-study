Week 2 · Forward kinematics

Where is the tool, given the joints?

Forward kinematics (FK) turns joint coordinates \(\mathbf q\) into the pose of the end effector. Every method in this week builds one 4×4 matrix by multiplying small, simple matrices in the right order. Get the order and the signs right and the rest is arithmetic.

Intuition first

A transform is a set of directions

\({}^A\mathbf T_B\) says “stand at A’s origin, walk along vector \(\mathbf t\), then turn so you face like B”. Like telling a friend: “from the station, go 200 m north, then face the café door”.

Walk along the arm

An ETS is a walk from the base to the tool, writing down every step (translation) and every twist (rotation) in the order you meet them. The product of that list is FK.

Each step is relative to where you now face

“Turn left, then go two blocks” only makes sense from where you stand after turning. Multiplying on the right means: the next move is measured in the frame you just arrived in.

DH is an ETS with a dress code

Denavit–Hartenberg allows only four moves per link, in a fixed order: twist, slide, rotate, slide. That strict code puts every frame in a standard place, so any arm fits in one table.In the practical: the PUMA 560 script is exactly this four-move pattern, repeated six times.

1 · FK by trigonometry: the planar RRR arm pp. 6–9

Picture

In a planar arm every link lies in the same plane and every joint axis points out of it. Each link is a vector of fixed length whose direction is the sum of all joint angles before it. The tip is just the sum of those vectors.

Derivation

Link \(k\) points at the absolute angle \(\phi_k = q_1+\dots+q_k\). Adding the three link vectors (p.7):

\[ x = L_1\cos q_1 + L_2\cos(q_1+q_2) + L_3\cos(q_1+q_2+q_3) \] \[ y = L_1\sin q_1 + L_2\sin(q_1+q_2) + L_3\sin(q_1+q_2+q_3) \]

Orientation in the plane is one angle, the sum of all joint angles (p.9): \( q_E = q_1+q_2+q_3 \).

The same answer as a chain of transforms: \( {}^0\mathbf T_E = R_z(q_1)\,t_x(L_1)\,R_z(q_2)\,t_x(L_2)\,R_z(q_3)\,t_x(L_3) \). Its position column is the pair above, and \(\operatorname{atan2}(T_{21},T_{11}) = q_E\). checked in checks/w02.py

Exam recipe
  1. Write each link’s absolute angle as a running sum.
  2. Sum \(L_k\cos\phi_k\) for x and \(L_k\sin\phi_k\) for y.
  3. Orientation = sum of revolute angles.
  4. Check at q = 0: the tip must be at \((L_1+L_2+L_3, 0)\).
Worked example

L = (0.5, 0.4, 0.2) m, q = (30°, 45°, −60°). Absolute angles: 30°, 75°, 15°.

\(x = 0.5\cos30^\circ + 0.4\cos75^\circ + 0.2\cos15^\circ = 0.4330 + 0.1035 + 0.1932 = 0.7297\) m
\(y = 0.5\sin30^\circ + 0.4\sin75^\circ + 0.2\sin15^\circ = 0.2500 + 0.3864 + 0.0518 = 0.6881\) m,   \(q_E = 15^\circ\). checked

A trap answer uses the joint angles directly (cos 45°, cos −60°) instead of the running sums. At q = 0 every method gives the same answer, so test your formula at a non-zero pose too.

2 · The homogeneous transformation matrix p. 12

Picture

\({}^A\mathbf T_B\) is the pose of frame {B} as seen from frame {A}. The top-left 3×3 block is a rotation whose columns are B’s axes written in A’s coordinates. The last column is where B’s origin sits in A. The bottom row \([0\;0\;0\;1]\) only makes the 4×4 maths work.

Derivation \[ {}^A\mathbf T_B = \begin{bmatrix} {}^A\mathbf R_B & {}^A\mathbf t_{B} \\ \mathbf 0^{\top} & 1\end{bmatrix} \in SE(3),\qquad {}^A\mathbf R_B\in SO(3),\qquad {}^A\mathbf t_B\in\mathbb R^3 \]

A point known in B maps to A by one multiplication, using homogeneous coordinates \([x\;y\;z\;1]^\top\): \( {}^A\mathbf P = {}^A\mathbf T_B\,{}^B\mathbf P \) (p.12). This is just \( {}^A\mathbf P = {}^A\mathbf R_B\,{}^B\mathbf P + {}^A\mathbf t_B \) written as one matrix.

The elementary rotations (right-hand rule about each axis) are the building blocks for everything else:

\[ R_x(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&c\theta&-s\theta\\0&s\theta&c\theta\end{bmatrix},\; R_y(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}c\theta&0&s\theta\\0&1&0\\-s\theta&0&c\theta\end{bmatrix},\; R_z(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}c\theta&-s\theta&0\\s\theta&c\theta&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix} \]

Note where the minus sign sits in \(R_y\): bottom-left, the opposite place from \(R_x\) and \(R_z\). It follows from the cyclic order z→x (positive rotation about y turns z towards x). added

The inverse is cheap because \(\mathbf R^{-1}=\mathbf R^\top\): \( {}^B\mathbf T_A = ({}^A\mathbf T_B)^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf R^\top & -\mathbf R^\top\mathbf t\\ \mathbf 0^\top & 1\end{bmatrix} \). added checked

Worked example

\( {}^A\mathbf T_B = R_z(30^\circ)\,t(1,2,0) \). B’s origin in A is \(R_z(30^\circ)[1,2,0]^\top = (0.8660-1.0000,\;0.5000+1.7321,\;0) = (-0.1340,\;2.2321,\;0)\). The point \({}^B\mathbf P=(1,0,0)\) lands at \((0.7321,\;2.7321,\;0)\) in A, which is the origin plus B’s x-axis \((0.8660, 0.5, 0)\). checked

“Find \({}^B\mathbf T_A\)” is a favourite one-liner. The rotation transposes, and the translation becomes \(-\mathbf R^\top\mathbf t\), not \(-\mathbf t\). Also expect “what does column 2 of R mean?”: it is B’s y-axis expressed in A.

3 · Compound transformations and the order of multiplication p. 13

Picture

Chaining frames chains matrices in the same order: \( {}^A\mathbf T_C = {}^A\mathbf T_B\,{}^B\mathbf T_C \). The inner subscripts cancel like fractions. Each new factor on the right moves along the axes of the frame you have just reached (the moving frame).

Derivation

Slide 13: {B} is {A} rotated by θ about xA, then {C} is {B} translated by d along zB. So \( {}^A\mathbf T_C = R_x(\theta)\,t_z(d) \). The translation is along the rotated z-axis, so C’s origin in A is \( R_x(\theta)[0,0,d]^\top = (0,\,-d\sin\theta,\,d\cos\theta) \).

Swap the order and you get \( t_z(d)\,R_x(\theta) \): slide along the old z first, then rotate in place. The origin is at \((0,0,d)\). Different matrix, different pose.

Rule added: right-multiplication = “then move in the current frame”; left-multiplication = “then move in the fixed/original frame”.

Worked example

θ = 30°, d = 2: \(R_x(30^\circ)t_z(2)\) puts C at \((0, -1.000, 1.732)\); \(t_z(2)R_x(30^\circ)\) puts it at \((0,0,2)\). checked

If the question says “rotated about its own / the new axis”, multiply on the right. If it says “about the fixed / original axis”, multiply on the left. Write a one-line sentence saying which you used; it earns method marks even if the arithmetic slips.

4 · Elementary Transform Sequence (ETS) pp. 15–19

Picture

Only six moves exist: \(t_x, t_y, t_z, R_x, R_y, R_z\) (p.16). Each takes a parameter η that is either a constant (a link length, a fixed twist) or a joint variable \(q_j(t)\). Walk from the base to the tool and write each move as you meet it. A joint becomes one move about (or along) its own axis.

The lecturer’s tip (p.18): redraw the arm with every joint displaced from rest. Then you can see which way each angle is measured, and therefore its sign.

Derivation: Furuta pendulum

Base {B} at the floor, z up. Up the post by L0, rotate by θ1 about the vertical, along the arm by L1, rotate by θ2 about the arm’s axis (x), down the pendulum by L2, then flip so that zE points down the link (p.19):

\[ {}^B\mathbf T_E = t_z(L_0)\,R_z(\theta_1)\,t_x(L_1)\,R_x(\theta_2)\,t_z(-L_2)\,R_y(\pi) \]

Multiplying out, the position column is checked symbolically:

\[ x = L_1\cos\theta_1 - L_2\sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2,\quad y = L_1\sin\theta_1 + L_2\cos\theta_1\sin\theta_2,\quad z = L_0 - L_2\cos\theta_2 \]

Sanity check: at θ2 = 0 the pendulum hangs straight down, so \(z = L_0-L_2\) and the tip sits at the end of the arm. ✓

Intermediate (link) frames are just partial products (notebook): \({}^B\mathbf T_1 = t_z(L_0)R_z(\theta_1)\), \({}^1\mathbf T_2 = t_x(L_1)R_x(\theta_2)\), \({}^2\mathbf T_E = t_z(-L_2)R_y(\pi)\).

Exam recipe (W13 Q1 style)
  1. Draw {S} on the figure. Mark each joint axis and the positive sense of each angle (from the drawn arrow).
  2. Walk to the first joint: write the translations (\(t_z(\cdot)\) etc.).
  3. At each joint write \(R_{axis}(\pm\theta_i)\). Use the minus sign if the drawn positive rotation is opposite to the right-hand rule about your chosen axis.
  4. Continue to the tool. Finish with any constant rotation that makes your last frame match the drawn {T}.
  5. Draw and label an intermediate frame after each joint. Write \({}^{i-1}\mathbf T_i\) for each group.
  6. Multiply, or at least give the position column. Check at all joints = 0 against the picture.
Worked example

L = (0.5, 0.4, 0.3) m, θ = (60°, 45°). Then \(x = 0.4(0.5) - 0.3(0.8660)(0.7071) = 0.0163\), \(y = 0.4(0.8660) + 0.3(0.5)(0.7071) = 0.4525\), \(z = 0.5 - 0.3(0.7071) = 0.2879\) m.

\[ {}^B\mathbf T_E=\begin{bmatrix}-0.5000&-0.6124&-0.6124&0.0163\\-0.8660&0.3536&0.3536&0.4525\\0&0.7071&-0.7071&0.2879\\0&0&0&1\end{bmatrix} \]

checked with sympy and roboticstoolbox

5 · Proximal (modified, Craig) Denavit–Hartenberg pp. 20–36

Picture

Links are numbered 0 (fixed base) to n. Joint i joins link i−1 to link i (p.23). In the proximal convention, frame {i} sits on joint axis i, at the near end of link i, with zi along that axis. Between two neighbouring axes there is exactly one shortest line, the common normal. The four parameters describe how to get from one axis to the next along it.

Definitions and the link transform
ParameterMeasuredSlide
\(a_{i-1}\) link lengthdistance from \(z_{i-1}\) to \(z_i\) along \(x_{i-1}\) (common normal); never negativep.24, p.30
\(\alpha_{i-1}\) link twistangle from \(z_{i-1}\) to \(z_i\) about \(x_{i-1}\) (right-hand sense)p.25
\(d_i\) link offsetsigned distance from \(x_{i-1}\) to \(x_i\) along \(z_i\)p.26
\(\theta_i\) joint angleangle from \(x_{i-1}\) to \(x_i\) about \(z_i\)p.27

A revolute joint’s variable is \(\theta_i\); a prismatic joint’s variable is \(d_i\). The link transform is the ETS twist → slide → rotate → slide (p.28):

\[ {}^{i-1}\mathbf T_i = R_x(\alpha_{i-1})\,t_x(a_{i-1})\,R_z(\theta_i)\,t_z(d_i) = \begin{bmatrix} c\theta_i & -s\theta_i & 0 & a_{i-1}\\ s\theta_i c\alpha_{i-1} & c\theta_i c\alpha_{i-1} & -s\alpha_{i-1} & -s\alpha_{i-1} d_i\\ s\theta_i s\alpha_{i-1} & c\theta_i s\alpha_{i-1} & c\alpha_{i-1} & c\alpha_{i-1} d_i\\ 0&0&0&1\end{bmatrix} \]

Then \({}^0\mathbf T_n = {}^0\mathbf T_1\,{}^1\mathbf T_2\cdots{}^{n-1}\mathbf T_n\) (p.35).

Frame-assignment recipe (slide 29)
  1. Identify all joint axes. Work on axes i and i+1.
  2. Find their common normal, or their intersection point. The origin of {i} is where the common normal meets axis i (or the intersection point).
  3. zi along joint axis i.
  4. xi along the common normal, pointing towards axis i+1. If the axes intersect, xi is normal to the plane containing both.
  5. yi completes a right-handed frame.
  6. Choose {0} to coincide with {1} when q1 = 0. Choose {N}’s origin and xN freely, making as many parameters zero as possible.
  7. Fill the table row by row: \(a_{i-1}, \alpha_{i-1}\) look backwards (from axis i−1 to axis i); \(d_i, \theta_i\) look along axis i.

7 · Why four parameters are enough, and what a good model needs pp. 42–44

Argument

A general rigid-body pose needs 6 numbers (3 rotation, 3 translation). DH spends 2 of them on rules about where frames go: in proximal DH, xi must be perpendicular to zi+1 and must intersect it. Each rule removes one freedom, so 6 − 2 = 4 (p.43). The price: a link frame need not lie on the physical link.

A kinematic model should be (p.44): complete (it can describe any geometry of successive joints), continuous (small geometry change gives a small parameter change; DH fails this when two axes become nearly parallel, because the common normal jumps), and minimal (no redundant parameters).

A 2–4 mark “explain” question: state 6 − 2 = 4 and name the two constraints. Then add one limitation: DH is not continuous for nearly parallel axes, and frames may sit off the link.

8 · URDF pp. 45–47

Picture

URDF (Unified Robot Description Format) is XML that lists <link>s and <joint>s. Each joint names a parent and child link, an origin (a fixed transform, i.e. constant ETS steps) and an axis (the joint variable’s axis). It can also carry visual geometry. The slide’s planar robot (p.46) is the ETS \(R_z(q_1)\,t_x(1)\,R_z(q_2)\,t_x(1)\): joint q2’s origin is xyz = “1 0 0” and the fixed joint to “end” adds another \(t_x(1)\).

Low exam weight. Be able to read a URDF snippet as an ETS: origin → constant translations and rotations, axis → which \(R\) the joint is.

P2.5 · Why are 4 DH parameters enough?explain

A pose between two rigid bodies needs six numbers. Explain in 3 sentences why DH uses four, and give one weakness.

Model answer

DH restricts where frames go: in proximal DH, xi is perpendicular to zi+1 and intersects it. These two constraints remove two of the six freedoms, leaving a, α, d, θ. Weakness: for nearly parallel axes the common normal is ill-defined, so tiny geometry changes cause large parameter jumps (not continuous). Frames may also lie off the physical link.

P2.6 · Workspace by sampling (notebook Question 6)explainsketch

Describe how to plot a manipulator’s workspace from its FK, and sketch the Furuta pendulum’s workspace (no joint limits).

Model answer

Sample joint vectors uniformly within the joint limits, push each through FK, and plot the tool positions as a point cloud. For the Furuta pendulum, the tip lies on a circle of radius L2 centred at the arm’s end. That circle lies in the plane perpendicular to the arm, not in the vertical plane through the post. From the FK, \(x^2+y^2 = L_1^2 + L_2^2\sin^2\theta_2\) and \(z - L_0 = -L_2\cos\theta_2\), so \(\rho^2 + (z-L_0)^2 = L_1^2 + L_2^2\) where ρ is the horizontal distance from the post. Every tip position is the same distance \(\sqrt{L_1^2+L_2^2}\) from the top of the post. The workspace is a band of a sphere of that radius, centred at (0, 0, L0), between heights \(L_0 \pm L_2\). It is a surface, not a solid, because the arm has only two joints. For the example lengths: radius 0.5 m, heights 0.2 m to 0.8 m. checked

Common wrong answer: a torus. A torus would need the pendulum to swing in the radial plane.

Mini-quiz

Q1concept

What is the third column of \({}^A\mathbf R_B\), physically?

Answer

The unit z-axis of {B} expressed in {A}’s coordinates.

Q2calculate

\({}^A\mathbf T_B = R_x(90^\circ)\,t_z(1)\). Where is B’s origin in A?

Answer

\(R_x(90^\circ)[0,0,1]^\top = (0,-1,0)\). The translation is along the rotated z, which now points along −yA.

Q3spot the error

A student writes the proximal link transform as \(R_z(\theta_i)\,t_z(d_i)\,t_x(a_{i-1})\,R_x(\alpha_{i-1})\). What is wrong?

Answer

That is the distal order with proximal subscripts. Proximal is \(R_x(\alpha_{i-1})\,t_x(a_{i-1})\,R_z(\theta_i)\,t_z(d_i)\): twist and slide along the old x first, then rotate and slide along the new z.
