Week 5 · Trajectories

How does the arm get there, and when?

A path says where the robot goes. A trajectory also says when: position, velocity and acceleration of every joint against time. This week builds trajectories from simple pieces: cubics, quintics, and straight lines with parabolic ends. Then it asks whether to build them in joint space or in task space, and what goes wrong in each.

Intuition first

Path = route, trajectory = timetable

A route on a map says “Brisbane, Gympie, Rockhampton”. A timetable says “Gympie at 10:40, moving at 90 km/h”. The robot needs the timetable, because motors are driven by what to do now.

A cubic is a smooth lift ride

A lift starts and stops gently. Four facts fix the ride: where and when it starts and ends, and how fast it moves at each end. Four facts, four coefficients. Rest-to-rest means both end speeds are zero.In the notebook: the cubic is found by solving a 4×4 matrix with np.linalg.solve.

Via points: ride over the hills

A cyclist going up then down stops pedalling at the crest: slope zero. On a steady climb she keeps the average of the slopes either side. That is the lecturer’s simple rule. A smoother ride matches acceleration too, but then the speeds are forced by the whole route.

Parabolic blends: a car on a motorway

Floor the pedal at a constant rate, cruise, brake at the same rate. Speed against time is a trapezoid. If the trip is too short for the chosen acceleration, no cruise fits; there is a minimum acceleration for a given distance and time.

Straight in the joints is not straight in space

Two joints turning at steady rates draw an arc, not a line. To go straight in space you must solve IK at many points along the line, and the arm may not be able to follow (hole, singularity, wrong elbow).

1 · Path, trajectory, and where to build it pp. 6–14, pp. 42–45

Picture

A trajectory is a time sequence of position (and velocity and acceleration) for every degree of freedom (p.7). A path is the same set of poses with no timing. The task: given an initial and a final pose of the tool, work out the motion in between (p.10).

Teach mode (p.9): an operator jogs the robot (teach pendant, or by hand) and the joint positions are stored and replayed. No kinematics are needed. It suits a repeated task with few uncertainties, such as pick and place on an assembly line.

Two ways to generate a trajectory. Joint space (p.12): turn the start and end poses into joint angles with IK, then interpolate each joint. There are infinitely many curves between the same two points (p.13). Task space (p.43): describe the tool motion directly, then use IK along it.

Comparison
Joint spaceTask (Cartesian) space
You specify\(\mathbf q(t)\), one curve per jointpose of {T} relative to {S} against time
Costcheap: no IK along the waymore computation: IK at every sample (p.43)
Tool pathwhatever the joint curves produce (not a line)exactly what you asked for (line, circle, ellipse)
Good forfree motion between pointsa specified path; avoiding an obstacle (p.43)
What goes to the motorsalways joint positions, velocities and accelerations (p.68)

Describing the tool {T} relative to the station {S} (p.45) has two benefits: the description is decoupled from the manipulator (the same description works for different robots), and it adapts to a moving {S}, such as a conveyor belt.

A short “explain” item: compare joint and task space on cost, path shape, and obstacle control, and finish with “either way the motors receive joint commands”. A common wrong claim is that Cartesian commands go straight to the motors; they do not.

2 · Cubic polynomial trajectories pp. 15–20

Picture

A polynomial of order \(N\) has \(N+1\) coefficients, so it needs \(N+1\) conditions: \(q(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}a_kt^k\) (p.15). Starting at rest, moving, and stopping at rest at the goal gives four conditions (start and end position, start and end velocity). The simplest polynomial with four coefficients is a cubic. In the widget above, the default curve is exactly the slide example.

Derivation

Let \(t_0=0\) (shift the clock otherwise, see below). With \(T=t_F\) and \(\Delta q=q_F-q_0\):

\[ q=a_0+a_1t+a_2t^2+a_3t^3,\qquad \dot q=a_1+2a_2t+3a_3t^2,\qquad \ddot q=2a_2+6a_3t \]

Four conditions: \(q(0)=q_0\), \(q(T)=q_F\), \(\dot q(0)=\dot q_0\), \(\dot q(T)=\dot q_F\) (zero for rest-to-rest). In matrix form (notebook cell 16 does this for the example):

\[ \begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&0\\1&T&T^2&T^3\\0&1&0&0\\0&1&2T&3T^2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}a_0\\a_1\\a_2\\a_3\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}q_0\\q_F\\\dot q_0\\\dot q_F\end{bmatrix} \]

Solve by hand. The first and third rows give \(a_0=q_0\) and \(a_1=\dot q_0\). Let \(r=\Delta q-\dot q_0T\) and \(s=\dot q_F-\dot q_0\). The other two rows are \(a_2T^2+a_3T^3=r\) and \(2a_2T+3a_3T^2=s\). Multiply the first by \(2/T\) and subtract it from the second: \(a_3T^2=s-2r/T\). Back-substitute:

\[ a_2=\frac{3\Delta q-(2\dot q_0+\dot q_F)T}{T^2},\qquad a_3=\frac{-2\Delta q+(\dot q_0+\dot q_F)T}{T^3} \]

checked symbolically and numerically For rest to rest (\(\dot q_0=\dot q_F=0\)): \(a_2=3\Delta q/T^2\), \(a_3=-2\Delta q/T^3\). Consequences: the velocity is a parabola with peak \(1.5\,\Delta q/T\) at \(t=T/2\); the acceleration is a straight line, \(+6\Delta q/T^2\) at the start and \(-6\Delta q/T^2\) at the end, zero at \(T/2\) (p.19). Note that the acceleration is not zero at the ends, so it jumps there (that is what a quintic fixes, section 4).

If \(t_0\ne0\): use \(\tau=t-t_0\) and \(T=t_F-t_0\) in the formulas above. Each segment of a multi-point trajectory gets its own clock \(\tau\in[0,T_k]\), which is how the sample solutions (Q8) write it.

More than one joint (p.20, notebook Q1): each joint gets its own cubic, solved independently with its own \(q_0,q_F\). Give all joints the same \(T\) so they start and stop together. A program then evaluates every joint at the controller’s path-update rate.

Exam recipe
  1. State the assumptions: \(t_0=0\), start and end at rest unless told otherwise.
  2. Write \(q,\dot q\) and the four conditions as a list.
  3. \(a_0=q_0\), \(a_1=\dot q_0\) straight away.
  4. Rest to rest: \(a_2=3\Delta q/T^2\), \(a_3=-2\Delta q/T^3\). Otherwise use the general pair above.
  5. Write \(q(t)\) with units: \(a_k\) is in °/s\(^k\) or m/s\(^k\).
  6. Check \(q(T)=q_F\) and \(\dot q(T)=\dot q_F\).
Worked example (slides pp. 17–19, notebook cell 15)

One revolute joint, rest at 10°, to 50° in 2 s, stop. \(\Delta q=40^\circ\), \(T=2\) s. \(a_0=10^\circ\), \(a_1=0\), \(a_2=3(40)/4=30^\circ/\mathrm{s}^2\), \(a_3=-2(40)/8=-10^\circ/\mathrm{s}^3\). So \(q(t)=10+30t^2-10t^3\). Peak speed \(1.5(40)/2=30\) °/s at \(t=1\) s; acceleration \(\ddot q=60-60t\), from +60 to −60 °/s² (the three plots on p.19). checked

Non-zero end velocity. 20° to 80° in 3 s, \(\dot q_0=0\), \(\dot q_F=10\) °/s: \(a_2=[3(60)-(0+10)(3)]/9=16.667\), \(a_3=[-2(60)+(0+10)(3)]/27=-3.333\). Check: \(q(3)=20+16.667(9)-3.333(27)=80\) and \(\dot q(3)=2(16.667)(3)-3(3.333)(9)=10\). checked

Calculation, usually with new numbers (sample Q9 is a rest-to-rest cubic for a prismatic joint). The trap is the denominators: \(t_F^2\) for \(a_2\) and \(t_F^3\) for \(a_3\). Units follow: m/s² and m/s³ for a prismatic joint.

3 · Via points: velocities, the simple rule, continuous acceleration pp. 21–28

Picture

To pass through intermediate (via) points, use a cubic between each adjacent pair (p.21). If every cubic starts and ends at rest, the robot stops at every via point (p.25: “Is this desirable?” Usually not). Each cubic needs a velocity at each end, so the real question is what velocity to give at the via points. There are three options (p.26):

  1. the user specifies them;
  2. the system computes them with a simple formula;
  3. the system computes them so that the acceleration is continuous.
Option 2: the simple rule (p.27)

Slope of each straight chord: \(s_k=\Delta q_k/\Delta t_k\). At a via point, if the slopes either side change sign, the velocity is zero (the curve peaks or dips there). Otherwise it is the average of the two slopes. The first and last velocities are zero (the figure on p.27 shows flat tangents at \(q_0\) and \(q_F\); the slide does not say so in words, so state it as an assumption). Each segment is then the closed-form cubic of section 2 with those end velocities.

Worked example (notebook Q2, “Question 2” data)

Points 10, 50, 20, 40, 45° at 0, 5, 10, 15, 20 s. Slopes: \(+8,\ -6,\ +4,\ +1\) °/s. At qA: +8 then −6, sign change, so 0. At qB: −6 then +4, sign change, 0. At qC: +4 and +1, same sign, average \(2.5\) °/s. At q0, qF: 0. checked

Position and velocity are continuous at every via point, but the acceleration jumps: end of one segment minus start of the next is −2.4, +3.4 and −2.0 °/s² at qA, qB, qC. checked

Option 3: continuous acceleration (p.26, homework p.28, notebook Q3)

The homework asks for two cubics through \(q_0,q_{via},q_F\) with \(\dot q_0=\dot q_F=0\) and “constant acceleration at the via point”. The unknown is the via velocity \(w\). Read “constant” as continuous: the acceleration at the end of segment 1 equals that at the start of segment 2. (If \(a_{via}\) were also prescribed, with \(w\) free, you would have too many conditions; this is the reading the lecturer’s sample answer uses, Q8.)

Count: two cubics have 8 unknowns. Conditions: 4 positions (start, via twice, end), 2 end velocities, velocity continuity, acceleration continuity: 8. Solvable.

Short route. Let \(d_1=q_{via}-q_0\), \(d_2=q_F-q_{via}\), durations \(T_1,T_2\). Use the closed-form cubic with end velocities \((0,w)\) for segment 1 and \((w,0)\) for segment 2. Then \(\ddot q_1(T_1)=2a_2+6a_3T_1=\dfrac{-6d_1+4wT_1}{T_1^2}\) and \(\ddot q_2(0)=2a_2'=\dfrac{6d_2-4wT_2}{T_2^2}\). Setting them equal and solving for \(w\):

\[ \dot q_{via}=\frac{3\,\big[T_1^2\,(q_F-q_{via})+T_2^2\,(q_{via}-q_0)\big]}{2\,T_1T_2\,(T_1+T_2)},\qquad T_1=T_2=T:\ \ \dot q_{via}=\frac{3(q_F-q_0)}{4T} \]

checked symbolically and with the 8×8 system The equal-time result agrees with the lecturer’s coefficient \(a_{21}=3(\theta_g-\theta_0)/(4t_f)\) in Q8. The via velocity does not depend on \(q_{via}\) when the times are equal. The shared acceleration at the via point is \(3(q_0+q_F-2q_{via})/T^2\) (equal times).

Many via points. Continuity of acceleration at each interior point gives one linear equation per interior velocity (a tridiagonal system), where \(h\) are the segment durations and \(s\) the chord slopes:

\[ \frac{w_{i-1}}{h_{i-1}}+2\Big(\frac1{h_{i-1}}+\frac1{h_i}\Big)w_i+\frac{w_{i+1}}{h_i}=3\Big(\frac{s_{i-1}}{h_{i-1}}+\frac{s_i}{h_i}\Big) \]

This is a spline. The slides do not give it; it is for understanding only.

For the Q2 data with zero end velocities it gives via velocities \(0,\ 2.304,\ -3.214,\ 4.554,\ 0\) °/s, a very different answer from the simple rule. checked

Worked example (sample Q8 numbers, and an unequal-time variant)

\(q_0=10^\circ,\ q_{via}=40^\circ,\ q_F=20^\circ\), \(T_1=T_2=1\) s: \(\dot q_{via}=3(10)/4=7.5\) °/s; segment 1: \(10+0t+82.5t^2-52.5t^3\); segment 2: \(40+7.5t-75t^2+47.5t^3\). Shared acceleration \(3(10+20-80)/1=-150\) °/s². checked

Now \(T_1=1\) s, \(T_2=2\) s (same angles): \(\dot q_{via}=3[1(-20)+4(30)]/(2\cdot1\cdot2\cdot3)=300/12=25\) °/s. Segment 1: \(10+65t^2-35t^3\); segment 2: \(40+25t-40t^2+11.25t^3\); shared acceleration −80 °/s². checked

Exam recipe
  1. Write unknowns and conditions (8 for two cubics; velocity and acceleration continuity at the via point).
  2. Give each segment its own clock \(\tau\) and duration.
  3. Use the closed-form cubic with end velocities \((0,w)\) and \((w,0)\) so only \(w\) is unknown.
  4. Equate end-of-1 and start-of-2 accelerations; solve for \(w\).
  5. Substitute back, then check \(q_1(T_1)=q_{via}\), \(q_2(T_2)=q_F\) and the accelerations match.
Two question types. (a) “Apply the simple rule to these points”: compute the slopes, decide sign change, write the velocities (the Q2 numbers above). (b) “Two cubics with continuous acceleration”: sample Q8. State the 8 conditions first; most marks are there. A trap is calling a trajectory that stops at every via point “smooth”; it is smooth in position but wasteful.

4 · Quintic polynomials, and why polynomials are inefficient pp. 29–30

Picture

A quintic has six coefficients, so it takes six conditions: position, velocity and acceleration at both ends (p.29). The extra two conditions let you set the end accelerations (usually zero), so the acceleration no longer jumps at the start and stop.

p.30 (hand sketch): the velocity of any polynomial trajectory is a hump that touches its peak briefly, while a trapezoid holds the peak speed for most of the motion. With a generous blend acceleration a trapezoid covers the same distance in the same time at a peak speed much closer to the mean, so it asks less of the motor. The notebook (cell 28) makes the same point and leads into linear trajectories.

Derivation \[ q=a_0+a_1t+\dots+a_5t^5,\quad \dot q=a_1+2a_2t+3a_3t^2+4a_4t^3+5a_5t^4,\quad \ddot q=2a_2+6a_3t+12a_4t^2+20a_5t^3 \]

Conditions: \(q,\dot q,\ddot q\) at \(t=0\) and \(t=T\). With zero end accelerations: \(a_0=q_0\), \(a_1=\dot q_0\), \(a_2=0\), and

\[ a_3=\frac{20\Delta q-(8\dot q_F+12\dot q_0)T}{2T^3},\quad a_4=\frac{-30\Delta q+(14\dot q_F+16\dot q_0)T}{2T^4},\quad a_5=\frac{12\Delta q-6(\dot q_F+\dot q_0)T}{2T^5} \]

checked symbolically Rest to rest: \(a_3=10\Delta q/T^3\), \(a_4=-15\Delta q/T^4\), \(a_5=6\Delta q/T^5\). These closed forms are mine (the slides only list the conditions); use the 6×6 matrix in an exam. Peak speed is \(1.875\,\Delta q/T\) at \(T/2\), and peak acceleration magnitude \(10\Delta q/(\sqrt3\,T^2)\approx5.77\,\Delta q/T^2\).

Worked example

0° to 15° in 1 s, rest to rest. Quintic: \(a_3=150\), \(a_4=-225\), \(a_5=90\) (units °/s³, °/s⁴, °/s⁵). Peak speed \(1.875(15)=28.125\) °/s; end accelerations 0. Cubic for the same move: peak speed \(1.5(15)=22.5\) °/s, end accelerations \(\pm6(15)=\pm90\) °/s². The mean speed is 15 °/s, so mean/peak is 66.7 % for the cubic and 53.3 % for the quintic. A trapezoid’s peak speed lies between \(\Delta q/T\) (ratio near 100 %, large \(a\)) and \(2\Delta q/T\) (the triangle, smallest \(a\)). checked

Notebook cell 29 prints qd.mean()/qd.max()*100 for traj_B (quintic 0→15 in 1 s, \(\dot q_0=10\), \(\dot q_F=5\), 50 samples). The notebook has no saved output. From the closed form above I compute 68.5 % (and 52.3 % for the zero-end-velocity case traj_A, which differs from 53.3 % because the 50-sample mean is not the exact mean). computed here

Counting questions: linear 2 coefficients, cubic 4, quintic 6; each extra order buys one more boundary condition per end. “Why use a quintic?” Acceleration continuity at the ends (smaller jerk, less vibration). “Why not use a polynomial everywhere?” Peak speed is high for the time taken, so a trapezoid is more efficient.

5 · Linear segments with parabolic blends (LSPB) pp. 31–37

Picture

A straight line \(q=a_0+a_1t\) has constant velocity, which a real joint cannot reach instantly from rest (p.31). So add a blend at each end: constant acceleration \(+a\) for a time \(t_B\), a coast at constant speed \(v\), then \(-a\) for \(t_B\) (pp.32–34). Velocity against time is a trapezoid. Position is parabola, line, parabola.

Derivation (slides pp. 33–36)

Take \(t_0=0\). With \(a\) the blend acceleration magnitude, \(v\) the coast speed, \(t_B\) the blend duration:

\[ q_{B1}=q_0+\tfrac12at_B^2,\qquad q_{B2}=q_{B1}+v\,(t_F-2t_B),\qquad q_F=q_{B2}+v\,t_B-\tfrac12at_B^2,\qquad v=a\,t_B \]

\(q_{B1}\) is the position at the end of blend 1, \(q_{B2}\) the position at the start of blend 2 (time \(t_F-t_B\)). The slide labels equations 1 and 3 “second order polynomial in time” and equation 2 “linear in time”. Eliminate \(q_{B1},q_{B2},v\):

\[ a\,t_B^2-a\,t_F\,t_B+(q_F-q_0)=0\ \Rightarrow\ t_B=\frac{t_F}{2}\pm\frac{\sqrt{a^2t_F^2-4a(q_F-q_0)}}{2a} \]

Take the root that is positive and smaller than \(t_F\) (in fact smaller than \(t_F/2\), the minus sign, because two blends must fit in the time). A real root exists only if the discriminant is non-negative:

\[ a^2t_F^2-4a\,\Delta q\ \ge 0\iff a\ \ge\ \frac{4\,\Delta q}{t_F^2} \]

The slide uses “≥” (non-strict). At equality \(t_B=t_F/2\), the coast phase has length zero and the profile is a triangle with peak \(2\Delta q/t_F\). Below the minimum there is no real root: that motion cannot be done in time \(t_F\). A motor sets a maximum \(a\). The coast speed is \(v=at_B=\tfrac12\big[a t_F-\sqrt{a^2t_F^2-4a\Delta q}\big]\), always in \((\Delta q/t_F,\,2\Delta q/t_F]\). Bigger \(a\) gives a shorter blend and a lower coast speed. checked

If you choose \(v\) instead of \(a\) (the toolbox does this, V=, notebook cell 40): \(\Delta q=v(t_F-t_B)\) gives \(t_B=t_F-\Delta q/v\) and \(a=v/t_B=v^2/(vt_F-\Delta q)\). This needs \(\Delta q/t_F\lt v\le2\Delta q/t_F\). For 120° in 2 s and \(V=70\): \(t_B=2-120/70=0.2857\) s, \(a=245\) °/s². checked

Slides vs notebook (the t₀ caveat)

The slides (pp.33–34) and notebook (cell 35) write a general-\(t_0\) form: \(q_{B1}=q_0+a(t_B-t_0)^2/2\) and \(a t_B^2-a(t_F-t_0)t_B+(q_F-q_0-at_0^2/2)=0\), then say “if \(t_0=0\)”. The general form is inconsistent: \(q_{B2}\) still uses \(t_F-2t_B\) and \(v=at_B\), which only hold for \(t_0=0\). Test: the p.37 motion shifted to start at \(t_0=1\) s and end at \(t_F=3\) s is the same motion and must give the same \(t_B=0.2254\) s. The slide’s quadratic gives roots 2.049 and −0.049 instead. Correct general form: replace \(t_F\) by the duration \(T=t_F-t_0\) everywhere: \(a t_B^2-aTt_B+\Delta q=0\), with \(t_B\) a duration. Every example on the slides has \(t_0=0\), so no slide answer changes. checked

Exam recipe (p.37)
  1. List \(\Delta q\), \(t_0=0\), \(t_F\), \(a\).
  2. Existence: compute \(4\Delta q/t_F^2\) and test \(a\ge\) it. If not, say “no solution; needs a larger \(a\) or a longer \(t_F\)”.
  3. \(t_B=\tfrac{t_F}{2}-\tfrac{\sqrt{a^2t_F^2-4a\Delta q}}{2a}\) (the smaller root).
  4. \(v=at_B\).
  5. Check: \(2\cdot\tfrac12at_B^2+v(t_F-2t_B)=\Delta q\).
Worked example (slide p.37; the slide gives no final answer)

120° in 2 s, \(a=300\) °/s². Step 1: \(4(120)/2^2=120\), and \(300\ge120\) ✓. Step 2: \(a^2t_F^2-4a\Delta q=360000-144000=216000\), \(\sqrt{\cdot}=464.76\), \(t_B=1-464.76/600=0.2254\) s (the + root, 1.7746 s, exceeds \(t_F/2\): rejected). Step 3: \(v=300(0.2254)=67.62\) °/s. Check: blends cover \(2\times7.62=15.24^\circ\), coast covers \(67.62\times1.5492=104.76^\circ\), total 120.00° ✓. checked

Edge cases: \(a=120\): \(t_B=1\) s, \(v=120\) °/s, no coast. \(a=100\): discriminant \(4\,(10^4)-4(100)(120)=-8000\lt 0\), no real root. checked

The standard calculation, and a classic spot-the-error item (strict “>” versus “≥”, wrong root, \(t_F^2\) forgotten in the minimum). Also explain in words why a minimum exists: the joint must accelerate up to \(v\) and brake again within \(t_F\). The gentlest way to cover \(\Delta q\) in \(t_F\) is the triangle: accelerate for half the time, brake for half, which needs exactly \(a=4\Delta q/t_F^2\).

6 · Several segments with blends pp. 38–41

Picture

With via points \(q_i,q_j,q_k,\dots\) the joint moves in straight segments and blends through each corner (p.38). Starting and ending at rest and passing exactly through every via point without stopping is over-constrained (notebook cell 42), so the path passes near the via points, not through them. The blend rounds each corner.

Symbols (p.39): \(t_{djk}\) total duration between \(q_j\) and \(q_k\); \(t_{jk}\) duration of the linear part; \(t_j\) duration of the blend at \(q_j\); \(v_{jk}\) slope of the linear part; \(a_j\) blend acceleration at \(q_j\). Given (p.40): the via points, the segment durations \(t_{djk}\), the acceleration magnitudes \(a_j\). Find: the blend times. The slides refer to Craig §7.3 for the solution (and to Corke §3.3.3 and mstraj for quintic blends, p.41).

Craig’s solution (not on the slides; for understanding)

Each via time is the centre of its blend. For \(n\) points, with \(\mathrm{sgn}\) giving the sign of the acceleration:

First segment: \(\ddot\theta_1=\mathrm{sgn}(\theta_2-\theta_1)|\ddot\theta_1|\), \(t_1=t_{d12}-\sqrt{t_{d12}^2-2(\theta_2-\theta_1)/\ddot\theta_1}\), \(v_{12}=\dfrac{\theta_2-\theta_1}{t_{d12}-\tfrac12t_1}\).

Interior segments: \(v_{jk}=\dfrac{\theta_k-\theta_j}{t_{djk}}\); at a via point \(j\): \(\ddot\theta_j=\mathrm{sgn}(v_{jk}-v_{ij})|\ddot\theta_j|\), \(t_j=\dfrac{v_{jk}-v_{ij}}{\ddot\theta_j}\).

Last segment: \(\ddot\theta_n=\mathrm{sgn}(\theta_{n-1}-\theta_n)|\ddot\theta_n|\), \(t_n=t_{d(n-1)n}-\sqrt{t_{d(n-1)n}^2+2(\theta_n-\theta_{n-1})/\ddot\theta_n}\), \(v_{(n-1)n}=\dfrac{\theta_n-\theta_{n-1}}{t_{d(n-1)n}-\tfrac12t_n}\).

Then the linear duration is \(t_{jk}=t_{djk}-\tfrac12t_j-\tfrac12t_k\) (full \(t_1\) or \(t_n\) at the ends). The first-segment formula comes from the slide’s single-segment algebra, with the end of segment 1 placed at the middle of the next blend: \(\theta_2-\theta_1=a t_1t_{d12}-\tfrac12at_1^2\). Because the path is a rounded corner, it misses the via point by \(|\Delta v|\,t_j/8=a_jt_j^2/8\).

Worked example (mine, built from the formulas above)

Points 0°, 40°, 10°; \(t_{d12}=t_{d23}=2\) s; every blend acceleration magnitude 50 °/s². Blend 1: \(t_1=2-\sqrt{4-2(40)/50}=0.4508\) s; \(v_{12}=40/(2-0.2254)=22.54\) °/s. Last blend: \(\ddot\theta_3=+50\), \(t_3=2-\sqrt{4+2(-30)/50}=0.3267\) s; \(v_{23}=-30/(2-0.1633)=-16.33\) °/s. Via blend: \(\ddot\theta_2=-50\), \(t_2=(-16.33-22.54)/(-50)=0.7775\) s. Linear durations 1.160 s and 1.285 s. The joint misses \(\theta_2=40^\circ\) by \(50(0.7775)^2/8=3.78^\circ\) (it reaches 36.22° at the via time). Checked by integrating the whole profile: it ends at 10° with zero velocity. checked

This is an explain-level topic in the slides (symbols and set-up only). Be ready to say: what is given and what is found; why via points are only approached; that each blend’s acceleration limit sets its duration. Do not expect the full Craig derivation unless the paper supplies it.

7 · Task-space straight lines and their three problems pp. 44–47

Picture

Frames (p.44): base {B}, wrist {W}, tool {T}, station {S}, goal {G}, camera {C}. The trajectory is {T} relative to {S}. Linear interpolation in joint space does not give a straight tool path; a straight tool path needs IK at every sample (p.46).

Three problems with Cartesian straight lines (p.47)
  1. Intermediate points may lie outside the workspace. Both ends can be reachable while the line crosses the hole near the base. Example: \(L_1=1\), \(L_2=0.7\) m (inner radius \(|L_1-L_2|=0.3\) m). The line \(y=0.2\) from \((-1,0.2)\) to \((1,0.2)\) has reachable ends (distance 1.02 m) but its middle \((0,0.2)\) is only 0.2 m from the base: unreachable. checked
  2. The path may pass through a singularity. The Jacobian determinant of a planar 2R is \(L_1L_2\sin q_2\), zero when the arm is fully stretched or folded. To keep a given tool speed the joint rates grow like \(1/\sin q_2\). Example with \(L_1=L_2=1\) m and the tool moving along x at 1 m/s through \((x,0)\): at \(x=1.0\) the largest joint rate is 1.16 rad/s, at 1.9 it is 3.20, at 1.99 it is 10.0 and at 1.999 it is 31.6 rad/s. A real joint cannot do that. checked
  3. Start and goal are reachable only in different configurations. If A needs the elbow on one side and B on the other, a straight line between them would need the arm to switch branch part-way (a joint jump, or a pass through a singularity). See Q4 of the notebook (section 8).
A scenario question: “a robot must weld a straight seam from A to B, list what can go wrong”. Answer all three with one sentence of cause each: hole or limit, singularity (joint rates blow up), different IK branch. Add the fix: replan in joint space around the problem, or change the base position or the path.

8 · The Cartesian recipe and the planar RR example pp. 48–65, p.68

Recipe (p.48)
  1. Given a tool trajectory in Cartesian coordinates, sample it at a few points.
  2. Use IK at each sample to get joint positions.
  3. Fit a curve to each joint’s samples (a cubic in the example; quintic if needed, p.60).
  4. Resample the fitted curves at the controller rate; optionally use FK to draw the resulting tool path (pp.62–64).

Why not run IK at every fine sample? Numerical IK output is not smooth (notebook cells 67–69, Puma 560 with ikine_GN), so fit first, then resample.

Worked example (pp. 49–64, notebook cells 51–59)

Planar RR, \(L_1=4\) m, \(L_2=3\) m. Start \((0,\ L_1+0.25L_2)=(0,4.75)\), end \((4.75,0)\), constant 3 m/s along the straight line.

  1. Path. \(y=-x+4.75\). Length \(\sqrt{4.75^2+4.75^2}=6.7175\) m, time \(6.7175/3=2.2392\) s (the slide rounds to 2.24 s).
  2. Sample 11 points (10 sections), \(t_i=i\,t_F/10\) (the slide writes \(0.224\,i\)): \(x(t)=3t/\sqrt2\), \(y(t)=4.75-3t/\sqrt2\) (p.53).
  3. IK (p.56): \(\cos q_2=\dfrac{x^2+y^2-L_1^2-L_2^2}{2L_1L_2}\), \(q_2=\operatorname{atan2}(+\sqrt{1-\cos^2q_2},\cos q_2)\), \(q_1=\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)-\operatorname{atan2}(L_2\sin q_2,\ L_1+L_2\cos q_2)\). At the start: \(q_1=0.8914\) rad (51.07°), \(q_2=1.6725\) rad (95.83°); at the end \(q_1=-0.6794\) rad (−38.93°), same \(q_2\). The line stays between radius 3.36 m and 4.75 m, inside the annulus \(1\le r\le7\) m, so there is no workspace problem. checked
  4. Cubic fit (p.59): \(q_1(t)\approx0.1346t^3-0.3375t^2-0.6182t+0.8858\), \(q_2(t)\approx0.0000t^3-0.4032t^2+0.9029t+1.6674\) rad. The fit misses the samples by up to 0.008 rad (0.46°). checked
  5. Resample to 50 points and apply FK: the fitted-curve tool path wanders up to 37 mm from the true line (6.7 m long). A quintic fit is more than 5 times closer; the slide’s remark (p.60) that one cubic may not suffice is right. checked

Four observations (cell 60): Cartesian generation costs more but is more intuitive; motors always get joint commands; accuracy depends on the fit and the number of samples; any shape (circle, square, 3D) works the same way.

Slides vs recomputation (p.59)

The slide gives \(q_1=0.1347t^3-0.3376t^2-0.6181t+0.8858\) and \(q_2=0.0004t^3-0.4043t^2+0.9036t+1.6674\). Recomputing with the notebook’s own code (11 equal samples over 2.2392 s) gives the \(q_1\) above (agrees to 0.0001) but \(q_2\) with a cubic term of 0.0000, \(-0.4032\), \(0.9029\). That is expected: \(q_2\) is symmetric (equal at both ends, 1.6725 rad), so a cubic fit must have a zero cubic term. The slide’s \(q_2\) is reproduced only if the samples span about 2.247 s, so it was probably made with a slightly different sampling. Treat the slide’s \(q_2\) digits beyond 0.01 as not exact; the shape and the method are what matter. checked

Two more points from the slides

Poses cannot be interpolated like numbers (p.65): positions and angles can be, but not translation matrices or rotation matrices directly. The toolbox’s ctraj interpolates a pose properly (notebook cells 63–66, from \(\mathrm{Trans}(0.4,-0.2,0)R_x(3)\) to \(\mathrm{Trans}(0.4,0.2,0)R_x(1)\), 2 s, step 0.02 s).

Summary (p.68): start and goal are usually given in Cartesian space as the pose of {T} with respect to {S}; the path between can be made in joint or task space; either way, the motors receive joint positions, velocities and accelerations.

Typical questions: write the line equation, its length and the travel time; give \(x(t_i),y(t_i)\) for one sample; do the IK for one sample (both branches); explain why we fit and resample; say what “higher-order fit” buys.

P5.14 · Write the letter R, and a legged robot (notebook Questions 6 and 7)explainderive

(a) Generate a trajectory for the letter “R” from the toolbox font and compute the joint angles for a six-DoF arm. (b) A legged robot (ANYmal) leg is a 3-DoF manipulator. Generate a circular foot trajectory in Cartesian space and find the joint angles. Give the procedure and the pitfalls.

Model answer (procedure, not run)

Procedure (the p.48 recipe). (1) Get or write the Cartesian points: for the letter, the font gives 2D stroke coordinates; scale and place them on a plane in front of the arm, with the pen orientation fixed. For the leg, sample the circle \(x=x_c+r\cos\phi\), \(y=y_c+r\sin\phi\) in the plane of motion with \(\phi=2\pi t/T\) (constant speed, or a speed profile). (2) Do IK at each sample: a 6-DoF arm with spherical wrist has the closed-form split into position (3 joints) and orientation (3 joints); a 3-DoF leg is an RRR chain (hip, thigh, shank). (3) Fit a curve to each joint, or just use the dense samples. (4) Resample at the controller rate; FK to check the drawn shape.

Mini-quiz

Q1concept

How many boundary conditions does a quintic need, and which ones? How many does a cubic need?

Answer

Quintic: six, position, velocity and acceleration at both ends. Cubic: four, position and velocity at both ends.

Q2calculate

A joint must move 90° in 1.5 s with a trapezoidal velocity profile. What is the smallest blend acceleration that works, and what is the coast phase then?

Answer

\(a_{min}=4(90)/1.5^2=160\) °/s². At that value \(t_B=0.75\) s, so there is no coast phase (a triangle with peak \(2\Delta q/t_F=120\) °/s).

Q3spot the error

“If each joint changes linearly with time, the tool moves in a straight line.” True?

Answer

False. Joint-linear motion gives a curved tool path in general (p.46). In the RR example the tool moves on an arc of radius 4.75 m, 1.39 m off the straight line at its midpoint.
