Q7.1 · Cart and pendulum, full derivation (W13 style)derivecalculate

A cart of mass \(M\) slides on a horizontal rail (position \(x\), driven by a force \(F\)). A point mass \(m\) hangs from the cart on a massless rod of length \(l\), driven by a motor torque \(\tau_\varphi\) at the pivot. The angle \(\varphi\) is measured from the downward vertical. Gravity acts along \(-y\). Derive the two equations of motion with the Lagrangian method. Then evaluate \(F\) and \(\tau_\varphi\) for \(M=2\) kg, \(m=1\) kg, \(l=0.5\) m, \(g=9.81\) m/s², \(\varphi=30^\circ\), \(\dot\varphi=2\) rad/s, \(\ddot\varphi=3\) rad/s², \(\ddot x=1.5\) m/s².

Hint 1

Positions of the mass: \(x_m=x+l\sin\varphi\), \(y_m=-l\cos\varphi\). Differentiate, then square and add to get \(v_m^2\).

Hint 2

\(v_m^2=\dot x^2+2l\dot x\dot\varphi\cos\varphi+l^2\dot\varphi^2\). \(K=\tfrac12M\dot x^2+\tfrac12 m v_m^2\), \(P=-mgl\cos\varphi\), \(\mathcal L=K-P\).

Hint 3

Two generalised coordinates, so two Euler–Lagrange lines: \(F=\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot x}-\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial x}\) and the same for \(\varphi\).

Full solution

Kinematics. \(x_m=x+l\sin\varphi\), \(y_m=-l\cos\varphi\). So \(\dot x_m=\dot x+l\cos\varphi\,\dot\varphi\) and \(\dot y_m=l\sin\varphi\,\dot\varphi\). Then \[ v_m^2=\dot x^2+2l\dot x\dot\varphi\cos\varphi+l^2\dot\varphi^2\cos^2\varphi+l^2\dot\varphi^2\sin^2\varphi=\dot x^2+2l\dot x\dot\varphi\cos\varphi+l^2\dot\varphi^2 . \] Energies. \(K=\tfrac12(M+m)\dot x^2+ml\dot x\dot\varphi\cos\varphi+\tfrac12ml^2\dot\varphi^2\). \(P=-mgl\cos\varphi\) (the mass is below the pivot). \(\mathcal L=K-P=K+mgl\cos\varphi\).

Cart coordinate. \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot x=(M+m)\dot x+ml\dot\varphi\cos\varphi\). Its time derivative is \((M+m)\ddot x+ml\ddot\varphi\cos\varphi-ml\dot\varphi^2\sin\varphi\). \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial x=0\) (nothing depends on \(x\)). So \[ F=(M+m)\ddot x+ml\ddot\varphi\cos\varphi-ml\dot\varphi^2\sin\varphi . \] Pendulum coordinate. \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot\varphi=ml\dot x\cos\varphi+ml^2\dot\varphi\). Its time derivative is \(ml\ddot x\cos\varphi-ml\dot x\dot\varphi\sin\varphi+ml^2\ddot\varphi\). \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\varphi=-ml\dot x\dot\varphi\sin\varphi-mgl\sin\varphi\). Subtracting, the \(ml\dot x\dot\varphi\sin\varphi\) terms cancel: \[ \tau_\varphi=ml^2\ddot\varphi+ml\ddot x\cos\varphi+mgl\sin\varphi . \] Both equations were also obtained symbolically. checked

Numbers. \(F=3(1.5)+0.5(3)\cos30^\circ-0.5(4)\sin30^\circ=4.5+1.299-1.0=4.799\) N. \(\tau_\varphi=0.25(3)+0.5(1.5)\cos30^\circ+9.81(0.5)\sin30^\circ=0.75+0.6495+2.4525=3.852\) N·m. checked

Sanity checks. The coupling term \(ml\cos\varphi\) appears in both equations, so the inertia matrix is symmetric. At rest (\(\dot\varphi=0\), accelerations zero) only \(mgl\sin\varphi\) is left: gravity pulls the pendulum back to \(\varphi=0\).

Common wrong answer. Writing \(+ml\dot\varphi^2\sin\varphi\) in the cart equation gives \(F=6.799\) N. The centripetal term pulls the cart against the swing, so it carries a minus sign.

Common wrong answer. Using the centroid \(I_{xx}=0.09667\) (forgetting that the frame moved), or \(I_{yz}=+0.1\) (dropping the minus sign in the definition of the product of inertia).

Q7.5 · Lagrange versus Newton–Eulerexplain

(a) Explain why the cost of Newton–Euler grows linearly in the number of joints \(n\) while a closed-form Lagrangian grows as \(n^4\), and say when you would use each. (b) Using the slide’s operation counts, find the multiplications for \(n=5\): Newton–Euler \(104n-77\), Lagrange \(32n^4+86n^3+171n^2+53n-128\).

Hint 1

Newton–Euler visits each link twice, outward then inward, doing a fixed amount of work per link.

Hint 2

The Lagrangian writes one energy expression for the whole arm; every torque equation then contains terms from all links beyond it, and differentiating products of many sines and cosines multiplies the term count.

Full solution

(a) Newton–Euler works link by link. The outward pass propagates velocity and acceleration from the base; the inward pass propagates forces from the tool. Each link needs a constant number of operations, so the total is proportional to \(n\) and the internal reaction forces come out for free. The Lagrangian treats the arm as a whole: the energy depends on every joint, and each joint’s equation couples to all others through \(\mathbf M\), \(\mathbf C\), \(\mathbf g\). Building the full closed form scales as \(n^4\). Use Lagrange for analysis and control design (you get the structure \(\mathbf M\), \(\mathbf C\), \(\mathbf g\) and can reason about it). Use Newton–Euler for real-time inverse dynamics on a computer.

(b) \(n=5\): NE \(=104(5)-77=443\). Lagrange \(=32(625)+86(125)+171(25)+53(5)-128=20000+10750+4275+265-128=35162\). checked The ratio is \(35162/443=79.4\). (At \(n=6\) the slides give 547 and 66 394, ratio about 121.)

Common wrong answer. “Lagrange is approximate and Newton–Euler is exact.” Both give identical torques; the difference is the route and the cost.

Q8.5 · Why PD leaves an offset, and how bigexplaincalculate

A partitioned joint with \(m=4\) kg is pushed by a constant \(f_{dist}=12\) N and uses \(k_p=75\) s⁻², critically damped. (a) Explain why a PD servo law cannot remove the resulting error and what integral action changes. (b) Give \(E_{ss}\). (c) What \(k_p\) is needed for \(E_{ss}\le2\) mm, and what is the largest \(k_i\) the PID loop with \(k_p=75\) and critical \(k_v\) can take before it goes unstable?

Hint 1

Steady state: set \(\dot E=\ddot E=0\) in \(\ddot E+k_v\dot E+k_pE=f_{dist}/m\).

Hint 2

PID characteristic polynomial \(s^3+k_vs^2+k_ps+k_i\). Stable only if \(k_vk_p\gt k_i\).

Full solution

(a) With a constant disturbance the error settles where the spring term \(k_pE\) balances the push \(f_{dist}/m\); the derivative terms vanish and so \(k_v\) plays no part. A pure PD law therefore leaves \(E_{ss}\ne0\) unless \(k_p\to\infty\). The integral term keeps growing while any error remains, so it can only stop when \(E=0\): it learns the disturbance. The error equation becomes third order and \(E_{ss}=0\) for a constant \(f_{dist}\).
