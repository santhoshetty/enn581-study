Week 7 · Dynamics of manipulators

What torques make the arm move like that?

Kinematics says where the arm goes; dynamics says what it costs. Every serial arm obeys \(\mathbf Q=\mathbf M(\mathbf q)\ddot{\mathbf q}+\mathbf C(\mathbf q,\dot{\mathbf q})+\mathbf g(\mathbf q)\). This week gives two ways to find M, C and g: Lagrange (energy, the exam’s method) and Newton–Euler (forces, the computer’s method).

How this is examined

Sample question 4 (W13 p.8): “Derive the equations of motion. Mass, moments of inertia and positions of centre of mass are given.” The lecturer’s hand-written plan: FK frames → velocities → KE, positions → PE, then \(Q_i=\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot q_i}-\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial q_i}\) for each joint. The 3-joint version is solved on the Sample Qs tab. This tab builds the method on the slides’ 2-link arm.

Where the marks are (indicative): COM positions from FK; velocities by differentiating; KE and PE written in full; \(\mathcal L=K-P\); one Euler–Lagrange line per joint; M, C, g identified; a physical check (M symmetric, g only from gravity, C vanishes at rest).

Where marks are lost: \(\mathcal L=K+P\); forgetting the cross term \(2L_1L_2\cos q_2\) in the second mass’s speed; differentiating with respect to t before \(\dot q\); a sign error in PE (gravity direction); calling a \(\dot q_i\dot q_j\) term centripetal.

Practical (the W8 sheet is about dynamics, solved here)

The sheet has three questions: a single link’s accelerations, the 3D RRR equations of motion, and torques along a cubic trajectory for a 3-link planar arm. All three are solved below as P7.1–P7.3. The W7–8 scripts (rp_lagrangian_dynamics.py, rp_newton_euler.py) derive the RP arm both ways and check that they agree. Their results are reproduced in section 7.

Intuition first

Four kinds of effort

Holding a heavy bag at arm’s length (gravity, g), swinging it faster (inertia, M), the outward pull when you spin around with it (centripetal), and the sideways push when you move it while turning (Coriolis). Q adds them up.

Lagrange = energy bookkeeping

Write down how much kinetic and potential energy the arm has, as a function of its joints. One recipe (differentiate, differentiate, subtract) turns that single scalar into all the joint torques.

Newton–Euler = pass the bucket

Walk outwards computing each link’s motion, then walk back inwards handing the forces down the chain, link by link, like a bucket brigade. Same answer, much less algebra for a computer.

Far mass is heavy mass

A mass far from the joint is harder to spin: inertia grows with distance squared (the parallel-axis theorem). That is why M11 grows when the arm stretches out.
Two-link arm: Lagrange equations, simulatedpoint masses 2 kg and 1 kg at the link tips · L1 = 1.0 m, L2 = 0.8 m · g = 9.81 m/s² · RK4
Try: start from θ = (30°, 0°) with τ = 0. The arm falls and whips around chaotically, yet the total energy line stays flat: the equations of motion are right. Switch to τ = g(q) (gravity compensation): the arm stays exactly where you put it. With a small push it would drift, because C and M are not cancelled. Watch M11 change with q2.

2 · The Lagrangian method on the slides’ two-link arm pp. 15–24

Derivation

\(\mathcal L=K-P\) (p.16), and for each joint (p.17):

\[ Q_i=\frac{d}{dt}\!\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot q_i}\right)-\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial q_i} \]

Qi is a torque (N·m) for a revolute joint and a force (N) for a prismatic joint. Arm: point masses m1 at the tip of L1 and m2 at the tip of L2; q1 from the horizontal, q2 relative to link 1; gravity along −y (p.18).

Positions (FK): \(x_1=L_1c_1,\;y_1=L_1s_1\); \(x_2=L_1c_1+L_2c_{12},\;y_2=L_1s_1+L_2s_{12}\).

Velocities (differentiate; p.20): \(\dot x_2=-L_1s_1\dot q_1-L_2s_{12}(\dot q_1+\dot q_2)\), \(\dot y_2=L_1c_1\dot q_1+L_2c_{12}(\dot q_1+\dot q_2)\).

Kinetic energy. Squaring and adding, the cross terms combine through \(c_1c_{12}+s_1s_{12}=c_2\):

\[ K_1=\tfrac12m_1L_1^2\dot q_1^2,\qquad K_2=\tfrac12m_2\Big[(L_1^2+2L_1L_2c_2+L_2^2)\dot q_1^2+2(L_2^2+L_1L_2c_2)\dot q_1\dot q_2+L_2^2\dot q_2^2\Big] \]

Potential energy (p.21): \(P_1=m_1gL_1s_1\), \(P_2=m_2g(L_1s_1+L_2s_{12})\).

Apply Euler–Lagrange to \(\mathcal L=K_1+K_2-P_1-P_2\) and collect terms (pp. 22–25):

\[ \mathbf M=\begin{bmatrix} m_1L_1^2+m_2(L_1^2+2L_1L_2c_2+L_2^2) & m_2(L_1L_2c_2+L_2^2)\\ m_2(L_1L_2c_2+L_2^2) & m_2L_2^2\end{bmatrix} \] \[ \mathbf C=\begin{bmatrix}-m_2L_1L_2s_2\,(2\dot q_1\dot q_2+\dot q_2^2)\\ m_2L_1L_2s_2\,\dot q_1^2\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf g=\begin{bmatrix}(m_1+m_2)gL_1c_1+m_2gL_2c_{12}\\ m_2gL_2c_{12}\end{bmatrix} \]

K2, M, C, g all reproduced symbolically from the Lagrangian

Exam recipe (any arm)
  1. Choose generalised coordinates q (the joint variables). Draw gravity’s direction.
  2. Write each COM position from FK. Differentiate for the velocities.
  3. \(K=\sum\tfrac12m_i\mathbf v_{C_i}^\top\mathbf v_{C_i}+\tfrac12\boldsymbol\omega_i^\top\mathbf I_i\boldsymbol\omega_i\) (drop the second term for point masses). \(P=\sum m_ig\,h_i\).
  4. For each joint: \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q_i\) → d/dt (chain rule; product rule on \(\cos q_2\cdot\dot q_1\)) → subtract \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial q_i\).
  5. Collect into M (coefficients of \(\ddot q\)), C (velocity products), g (the rest).
  6. Check: M symmetric, M22 > 0, g independent of \(\dot q\), C = 0 at rest. A unit check per term.
Worked numbers

m = (2, 1) kg, L = (1.0, 0.8) m, g = 9.81 m/s², at q = (30°, 45°), \(\dot{\mathbf q}\) = (1, −0.5) rad/s, \(\ddot{\mathbf q}\) = (0.5, 2) rad/s²:

\(\mathbf M=\begin{bmatrix}4.7714&1.2057\\1.2057&0.6400\end{bmatrix}\) kg·m², \(\mathbf C=(0.4243,\;0.5657)\), \(\mathbf g=(27.5183,\;2.0312)\) N·m, so \(\mathbf Q=(32.7397,\;4.4797)\) N·m. checked; Newton–Euler gives the same Gravity dominates, as the slides say (p.27).

A frequent short question: “which terms remain when the arm is held still?” Only g. “At constant joint speed?” C and g. “Which entry of M changes as the elbow bends, and why?” M11 and M12, through \(\cos q_2\): the second mass moves closer to joint 1.

3 · The general form and what each term means pp. 25–33

Structure \[ \mathbf Q=\mathbf M(\mathbf q)\ddot{\mathbf q}+\mathbf C(\mathbf q,\dot{\mathbf q})+\mathbf g(\mathbf q)+\mathbf f(\dot{\mathbf q})+\mathbf J^\top(\mathbf q)\,\mathbf w \]

Sizes: n×1 = (n×n)(n×1) + n×1 + n×1 (p.25). f is friction; w is an external wrench (contact), mapped to the joints by the Jacobian transpose (p.26).

M, the joint-space inertia matrix (p.28): symmetric and positive definite (so always invertible). Diagonal Mjj is the inertia joint j feels; off-diagonal Mij couples joint j’s acceleration into joint i’s torque. PUMA 560 example on the slide: M11 = 3.659, M12 = −0.4044.

C splits into centripetal terms (squares \(\dot q_i^2\)) and Coriolis terms (products \(\dot q_i\dot q_j\), i ≠ j) (p.30). For the 2R arm, with \(h=m_2L_1L_2s_2\):

\[ \mathbf C=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}0&-h\\h&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\dot q_1^2\\\dot q_2^2\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{centripetal}}+\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}-2h\\0\end{bmatrix}\dot q_1\dot q_2}_{\text{Coriolis}} \]

checked our split; the slide’s handwriting here is not legible

g is present even at rest and usually dominates (p.27). For the PUMA 560 the slide lists [0, 31.64, 6.035, 0, 0.02825, 0] N·m at rest, [0, 46.01, 8.772, 0, 0.02825, 0] with the arm stretched out, and [0, −0.7752, 0.2489, 0, 0, 0] pointing up. Shoulder and elbow carry almost everything, and stretching out raises the shoulder torque (longer lever).

Forward dynamics (p.48): \(\ddot{\mathbf q}=\mathbf M^{-1}\big(\mathbf Q-\mathbf C-\mathbf g-\mathbf f-\mathbf J^\top\mathbf w\big)\), then integrate. This is what the widget above does. Task space (p.32): \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal F}=\mathbf M_x\ddot{\mathbf x}+\mathbf C_x+\mathbf g_x\) and \(\mathbf Q=\mathbf J^\top\boldsymbol{\mathcal F}\).

Two properties used in control proofs added: \(\dot{\mathbf M}-2\mathbf C_{mat}\) is skew-symmetric (with C written as a matrix times \(\dot{\mathbf q}\) via Christoffel symbols); and total energy is conserved when Q = 0 and there is no friction. Both are checked for the Sample Q4 arm.

Spot-the-error favourites: “g depends on \(\dot q\)” (no); “M is diagonal” (only for special arms, e.g. the RP arm below); “\(\dot q_1\dot q_2\) is centripetal” (no, Coriolis); “forward dynamics is for control” (no, for simulation).

4 · Rigid bodies: Newton, Euler and the inertia tensor pp. 34–39

Derivation

For a link with mass m, COM acceleration \(\dot{\mathbf v}_C\), angular velocity ω and acceleration \(\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}\) (p.34):

\[ \mathbf F=m\,\dot{\mathbf v}_C\quad(\text{Newton}),\qquad \mathbf N={}^C\mathbf I\,\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}+\boldsymbol\omega\times{}^C\mathbf I\,\boldsymbol\omega\quad(\text{Euler}) \]

The inertia tensor about frame {A} (p.36), uniform density ρ:

\[ {}^A\mathbf I=\begin{bmatrix}I_{xx}&I_{xy}&I_{xz}\\I_{xy}&I_{yy}&I_{yz}\\I_{xz}&I_{yz}&I_{zz}\end{bmatrix},\quad I_{xx}=\iiint(y^2+z^2)\rho\,dv,\;\dots,\quad I_{xy}=-\iiint xy\,\rho\,dv,\;\dots \]

Cuboid W × L × H (x, y, z), frame at a corner (p.38) and at the centroid (p.39):

\[ {}^{corner}\mathbf I=m\begin{bmatrix}\frac{L^2+H^2}{3}&-\frac{WL}{4}&-\frac{HW}{4}\\-\frac{WL}{4}&\frac{W^2+H^2}{3}&-\frac{HL}{4}\\-\frac{HW}{4}&-\frac{HL}{4}&\frac{L^2+W^2}{3}\end{bmatrix},\qquad {}^{C}\mathbf I=\frac m{12}\operatorname{diag}\big(L^2+H^2,\;W^2+H^2,\;L^2+W^2\big) \]

“The frame of reference is important”: the same body has different tensors in different frames. Parallel-axis theorem added: with p the COM in {A}, \({}^A\mathbf I={}^C\mathbf I+m\big(\mathbf p^\top\mathbf p\,\mathbf 1_3-\mathbf p\mathbf p^\top\big)\). With p = (W/2, L/2, H/2) it turns the centroid tensor into the corner tensor exactly. both tensors and the theorem checked by integration

Worked numbers

m = 6 kg, W = 0.2, L = 0.3, H = 0.1 m. Centroid: diag(0.050, 0.025, 0.065) kg·m². Corner: \(\begin{bmatrix}0.200&-0.090&-0.030\\-0.090&0.100&-0.045\\-0.030&-0.045&0.260\end{bmatrix}\) kg·m². checked

Slides vs slides

Slide 37 writes \(I_{xy}=\iiint xy\rho\,dv=m(WL)/4\) without a minus sign, while the matrix on slide 38 has −m(WL)/4. They agree only if the minus belongs to the definition of the off-diagonal entry, which is how slide 36 defines it. Put the minus in.

5 · Iterative Newton–Euler pp. 40–46

Algorithm

Outward pass (i = 1 … n), joint axis \(\hat Z\) of each frame, \(R={}^{i-1}_{\;\;i}\mathbf R\), \(\mathbf P_i\) = origin of {i} in {i−1} recursions from the unit’s NE script and Craig ch. 6:

\[ \boldsymbol\omega_i=R^\top\boldsymbol\omega_{i-1}+\dot q_i\hat Z,\qquad \dot{\boldsymbol\omega}_i=R^\top\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}_{i-1}+R^\top\boldsymbol\omega_{i-1}\times\dot q_i\hat Z+\ddot q_i\hat Z \] \[ \dot{\mathbf v}_i=R^\top\big(\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}_{i-1}\times\mathbf P_i+\boldsymbol\omega_{i-1}\times(\boldsymbol\omega_{i-1}\times\mathbf P_i)+\dot{\mathbf v}_{i-1}\big),\qquad \dot{\mathbf v}_{C_i}=\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}_i\times\mathbf P_{C_i}+\boldsymbol\omega_i\times(\boldsymbol\omega_i\times\mathbf P_{C_i})+\dot{\mathbf v}_i \]

(for revolute joints; prismatic joints add \(2\boldsymbol\omega_i\times\dot d_i\hat Z+\ddot d_i\hat Z\) to \(\dot{\mathbf v}_i\) and leave ω unchanged). Then \(\mathbf F_i=m_i\dot{\mathbf v}_{C_i}\), \(\mathbf N_i=\mathbf I_i\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}_i+\boldsymbol\omega_i\times\mathbf I_i\boldsymbol\omega_i\).

Inward pass (i = n … 1, with \(\mathbf f_{n+1}=\mathbf n_{n+1}=0\)), \(R'={}^{i}_{\;i+1}\mathbf R\):

\[ \mathbf f_i=R'\mathbf f_{i+1}+\mathbf F_i,\qquad \mathbf n_i=\mathbf N_i+R'\mathbf n_{i+1}+\mathbf P_{C_i}\times\mathbf F_i+\mathbf P_{i+1}\times R'\mathbf f_{i+1},\qquad \tau_i=\mathbf n_i\cdot\hat Z \]

Gravity trick: start with \(\dot{\mathbf v}_0=(0,g,0)\), i.e. pretend the base accelerates upwards, instead of adding gravity to every link. our implementation equals the Lagrangian 2R result at 50 random states (the slide 46 homework)

Slides vs textbook

Slide 45 writes \({}^iT_{i+1}\) where a rotation \({}^i\mathbf R_{i+1}\) is meant, and its moment balance omits rotating \(\mathbf n_{i+1}\) into frame {i}. Craig’s equations, used above, include the rotation.

NE is rarely derived by hand in an exam. Expect “explain the two passes and why NE is O(n)” or “compute \(\dot{\mathbf v}_C\) for one link” (P7.1 below).

Result

\[ \tau_1=(m_1l_1^2+I_{zz1}+I_{zz2}+m_2d_2^2)\ddot\theta_1+2m_2d_2\dot\theta_1\dot d_2+(m_1l_1+m_2d_2)g\cos\theta_1 \] \[ f_2=m_2\ddot d_2-m_2d_2\dot\theta_1^2+m_2g\sin\theta_1 \]

P7.1 · Single link: accelerations (practical sheet Q1)derive

A 1-DoF link (revolute about z1) has m = 1 kg, COM at \({}^1\mathbf P_C=[3,0,0]^\top\) m, \({}^C\mathbf I=\operatorname{diag}(1,2,3)\) kg·m², starts at rest and moves as \(q(t)=bt+ct^2\). Give the link’s angular acceleration and the COM’s linear acceleration in {1}. Then the joint torque (no gravity).

Hint 1

\(\boldsymbol\omega=\dot q\,\hat z\), \(\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}=\ddot q\,\hat z\).

Hint 2

\(\dot{\mathbf v}_C=\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}\times\mathbf P_C+\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf P_C)\): a tangential part and a centripetal part.

Full solution

\(\dot q=b+2ct\), \(\ddot q=2c\). So \({}^1\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}=[0,\,0,\,2c]^\top\) rad/s².

\(\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}\times\mathbf P_C=2c\,\hat z\times3\hat x=[0,\,6c,\,0]\) (tangential). \(\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf P_C)=-\dot q^2\,[3,0,0]\) (centripetal, pointing back to the axis). So \({}^1\dot{\mathbf v}_C=[-3(b+2ct)^2,\;6c,\;0]^\top\) m/s².

Torque: \(\mathbf N={}^C\mathbf I\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf I\boldsymbol\omega=[0,0,6c]\) (the second term is zero because ω lies along a principal axis). Then \(\tau=N_z+(\mathbf P_C\times\mathbf F)_z=6c+3\cdot6c=24c\). Check by the parallel-axis theorem: \(I_{zz}+m\cdot3^2=3+9=12\), and \(12\ddot q=24c\) ✓. checked

P7.6 · Spot the errorsspot the error

A student’s 2R result: “\(\mathcal L=K+P\); \(g_2=m_2gL_2\sin(q_1+q_2)\); the \(-2m_2L_1L_2s_2\dot q_1\dot q_2\) term is centripetal; M12 = m2L1L2c2.”

Answer

(1) \(\mathcal L=K-P\). (2) With q measured from the horizontal and gravity along −y, the lever arm is horizontal: \(g_2=m_2gL_2\cos(q_1+q_2)\). (3) A product of two different joint rates is Coriolis. (4) M12 = m2(L1L2c2 + L2²): the L2² part was dropped.

Mini-quiz

Q1concept

Why is M always invertible?

Answer

It is symmetric positive definite: kinetic energy \(\tfrac12\dot{\mathbf q}^\top\mathbf M\dot{\mathbf q}\) is positive for any non-zero motion.

Q2concept

Forward or inverse dynamics for (a) a simulator, (b) a computed-torque controller?

Answer

(a) Forward: torques in, motion out. (b) Inverse: desired motion in, torques out.

Q3calculate

How many multiplications does Newton–Euler need for n = 7?

Answer

104(7) − 77 = 651.
