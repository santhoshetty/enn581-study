Week 4 · Parallel manipulators

Many legs, one platform: count the freedom, close the loops

A parallel robot holds its end effector with several chains at once. That makes it stiff, light and precise, but every loop adds constraint equations. This week has three exam skills: count mobility (plain and extended Grübler–Kutzbach), write loop-closure equations, and solve IK by splitting the robot into its legs.

Intuition first

A table vs a crane

A serial arm is a crane: each joint carries everything after it, so errors and weight add up. A parallel robot is a table on several legs: each leg carries a share, so it is stiff and precise, but it cannot reach far.

Counting freedom like a budget

Each free body would have D freedoms (3 in a plane, 6 in space). Each joint takes some away and leaves Fi. Grübler–Kutzbach is the balance: what is left over is the mobility.

Spinning a pencil

A rod held by a ball joint at each end can spin about its own axis without moving anything else. That idle spin is a local mobility RM. The counting formula sees it as a real freedom, so it over-counts.

Cut the loop, then glue it

To analyse a closed loop, cut it at one link. You get two simple serial arms. The “glue” is the condition that both halves end at the same point with the same orientation: the loop-closure equations.

2 · Workspace of parallel robots pp. 20–23

Four definitions (p.21): reachable (some orientation), dexterous (all orientations), constant-orientation (points reachable with one given orientation), orientation workspace (all orientations available at one given point). Two ways to compute it: discretisation, i.e. step each actuator by a small amount and take the boundary of the reached nodes (slow; accuracy limited by step size, p.22); or geometry, i.e. intersect the regions allowed by each actuator’s limits (p.23). For a two-leg planar mechanism with prismatic legs, each leg confines the point to an annulus between its minimum and maximum length about its base pivot. The workspace is the overlap of the annuli.

Sketch question: draw each leg’s annulus (rmin, rmax about its pivot), shade the intersection, and label it “reachable”.

3 · Mobility: Grübler–Kutzbach pp. 24–34

Derivation

Start with N bodies, each free with D DoF, then fix one (the ground): D(N − 1) freedoms. Each joint i removes D − Fi of them. So \(M=D(N-1)-\sum_i(D-F_i)\), which rearranges to the slide’s form (p.25):

\[ M=D\,(N-J-1)+\sum_{i=1}^{J}F_i,\qquad D=\begin{cases}3 & \text{planar}\\6 & \text{spatial}\end{cases},\quad N\text{ includes the ground},\quad F_R=F_P=1,\;F_U=2,\;F_S=3 \]
Exam recipe
  1. Decide planar or spatial (D = 3 or 6). Motion out of the drawing’s plane means spatial (“still 3D despite this depiction”, W13 p.14).
  2. Number every rigid body on the figure, ground = 1. A prismatic pair is two bodies.
  3. List every joint and its Fi. A joint connects exactly two bodies; if three bodies meet at one pin, that is two joints.
  4. Substitute. If M disagrees with the obvious motion, look for idle spins (RM) or redundant constraints (RC): section 4.

4 · Extended Grübler–Kutzbach: over-constraints and local mobility pp. 35–43

Equation (slide 35) \[ M=D(N-J-1)+\sum_{i=1}^{J}F_i+R_C-R_M,\qquad R_C=\sum_{i=1}^{J}F_i-\sum_{k=1}^{L}u_k \]

RM: local mobility, i.e. motions of internal parts that do not move the output (a rod spinning about its own axis between two ball joints). The plain formula counts them as DoF, so subtract them.

RC: over-constraints. Some joints repeat a constraint that another joint already imposes, so the plain formula “removes” freedoms twice. uk are the distinct platform DoFs that loop k allows; the sum counts each distinct DoF once across loops.

Common delta (slides 33–36)

Plain count 9, but the platform only translates (3 DoF). Each of the 6 parallelogram rods can spin about its own axis between its two spherical joints: RM = 6. No redundant constraints: RC = 0. So M = 9 + 0 − 6 = 3. checked

4RRR (slides 37–43)

Two square plates joined by four RR legs: N = 10 (base, platform, 8 leg links), J = 12, ΣF = 12. Plain: 6(10 − 12 − 1) + 12 = −6, which is impossible for a mechanism that moves.

Loop 1 (one pair of legs) lets the platform do {Ty, Tz, Rx}; loop 2 lets it do {Tx, Tz, Ry} (pp. 39–41). The distinct DoFs are {Tx, Ty, Tz, Rx, Ry}, so Σuk = 5 (Tz counted once). RC = 12 − 5 = 7, RM = 0. M = −6 + 7 − 0 = 1: the only motion common to both loops is Tz, so the platform can only move up and down (pp. 42–43). checked

“Explain” marks: the plain count assumes every constraint is independent and every DoF moves the output. RC fixes the first assumption, RM the second. Say which one applies, and why, for the mechanism in front of you.

5 · Active and passive joints, and loop-closure equations pp. 45–58

Derivation (four-bar)

Configuration \(\mathbf q=(\boldsymbol\theta,\boldsymbol\varphi)\): n active joints θ, m passive joints φ (p.45). Recipe (p.49): break the loop into serial chains, write the pose of the break point from each side, and equate. Choose the break so that the fewest equations are needed.

Break link 2 at K (p.54), with a and b the distances from K to link 2’s ends (L2 = a + b) and {D} at (L0, 0). Position and orientation of K must agree from both sides: three equations, one active (θ1) and three passive (φ1, φ2, φ3) unknowns.

\[ \begin{aligned} L_1\cos\theta_1+a\cos(\theta_1+\varphi_2)&=L_0+L_3\cos\varphi_1+b\cos(\varphi_1+\varphi_3)\\ L_1\sin\theta_1+a\sin(\theta_1+\varphi_2)&=L_3\sin\varphi_1+b\sin(\varphi_1+\varphi_3)\\ \theta_1+\varphi_2&=\varphi_1+\varphi_3+\pi \end{aligned} \]

Break link 2 at both ends (p.57): only its length must be preserved, giving one equation in θ1, φ1 (the slide calls it the Freudenstein equation):

\[ \eta_1(\theta_1,\varphi_1)=(L_1\cos\theta_1-L_0-L_3\cos\varphi_1)^2+(L_1\sin\theta_1-L_3\sin\varphi_1)^2-L_2^2=0 \]

both forms checked at two assembly modes A Gough–Stewart platform has 6 active (prismatic) and 30 passive joint variables in many loops; choosing which equations to use is not trivial (p.58).

6 · Forward kinematics: the hard direction pp. 59–67

Derivation

FK of a parallel robot means: given the active joints, solve the loop-closure equations for the passive joints, then compute the output pose. There is no general method as there is for serial arms (p.60). Solutions can be multiple, some imaginary, so numerical methods are often used (p.67).

Four-bar, by geometry added: with θ1 known, A = L1(cos θ1, sin θ1) and D = (L0, 0) are fixed. B lies on the circle of radius L2 about A and the circle of radius L3 about D. Two circles meet in two points, the two assembly modes, one point (a limit), or not at all (the linkage cannot be assembled at that θ1).

The slides’ route (pp. 62–66), given K = (x, y) and seeking δ = θ1+φ2: squaring and adding each chain’s equations gives two equations linear in cos δ and sin δ:

\[ A_1\cos\delta+B_1\sin\delta+C_1=0,\quad A_1=x-L_0,\;B_1=y,\;C_1=\frac{(x-L_0)^2+y^2+b^2-L_3^2}{2b} \] \[ A_2\cos\delta+B_2\sin\delta+C_2=0,\quad A_2=x,\;B_2=y,\;C_2=\frac{L_1^2-a^2-x^2-y^2}{2a} \]

By Cramer’s rule, \(\cos\delta=\dfrac{B_1C_2-B_2C_1}{A_1B_2-A_2B_1}\) and \(\sin\delta=\dfrac{A_2C_1-A_1C_2}{A_1B_2-A_2B_1}\), so \(\delta=\operatorname{atan2}(\sin\delta,\cos\delta)\). Consistency of the two requires \((A_1B_2-A_2B_1)^2=(A_1C_2-A_2C_1)^2+(B_1C_2-B_2C_1)^2\), which is slide 65’s condition on (x, y). constants C1, C2 and the identity checked

Slides vs maths: slide 65

The slide prints \(\delta=\tan^{-1}\!\big(\tfrac{A_1C_2-A_2C_1}{(B_1C_2-B_2C_1)+(A_1B_2-A_2B_1)}\big)\). With the tangent-half-angle substitution the correct result is \(\delta=2\tan^{-1}\!\big(\tfrac{A_2C_1-A_1C_2}{(B_1C_2-B_2C_1)+(A_1B_2-A_2B_1)}\big)\): opposite sign in the numerator and a factor of 2. In our test the slide form gives 0.300° where the true δ is −0.600° checked. Safest in the exam: use the atan2 of the Cramer’s-rule sine and cosine above.

7 · Inverse kinematics: the easy direction pp. 68–73

Derivation

Given the pose of the output link, each leg is a serial chain whose end pose is known, so solve each leg separately. No loop-closure equations are needed (p.69). Each leg must not be redundant, or its solution is not unique.

Four-bar (pp. 70–72; Sample Q5): the coupler’s ends are A = K − a(cos φ, sin φ) and B = K + b(cos φ, sin φ). So θ1 = atan2(y − a sin φ, x − a cos φ), φ2 = φ − θ1, φ1 = atan2(y + b sin φ, x + b cos φ − L0), φ3 = φ + π − φ1 (mod 2π). The full worked answer, and a note on the sign printed on W13 slide 11, are on the Sample Qs tab.

Gough–Stewart leg (homework, p.73): leg i joins base point \(\mathbf b_i\) (in {B}) to platform point \(\mathbf p_i\) (in {P}). Given \({}^B\mathbf T_P=[\mathbf R\;|\;\mathbf t]\):

\[ \mathbf l_i=\mathbf t+\mathbf R\,\mathbf p_i-\mathbf b_i,\qquad d_i=\lVert\mathbf l_i\rVert\ (\text{active prismatic}),\qquad \hat{\mathbf n}_i=\mathbf l_i/d_i \]

The passive joint angles follow from the leg direction \(\hat{\mathbf n}_i\): the spherical joint at the base takes the leg’s azimuth and elevation, and the universal joint at the platform takes the angle between \(\hat{\mathbf n}_i\) and the platform axes \(\mathbf R\). One unique answer per leg: this is why parallel IK is “simple”.

P4.1 · Mobility of the 4RRRcalculateexplain

Two square plates are joined by four identical RR legs (slides 37–43). Compute the plain G–K mobility, explain why it is wrong, and find the true mobility with the extended equation.

Hint 1

N = 10, J = 12, all revolute, D = 6.

Hint 2

Write the platform DoFs each pair of legs allows; count the union, not the sum.

Full solution

Plain: 6(10 − 12 − 1) + 12 = −6. That is impossible for a moving mechanism, so constraints are being double-counted. Loop DoFs: {Ty, Tz, Rx} and {Tx, Tz, Ry}; union = 5. RC = 12 − 5 = 7, RM = 0, so M = −6 + 7 = 1 (pure Tz).

P4.4 · Why is the delta’s platform always level?explainsketch

Explain, with a sketch of one leg, why a delta platform translates but never rotates, and connect it to the mobility count.

Model answer

Each forearm is a parallelogram: two equal, parallel rods with spherical joints at the four corners. A parallelogram keeps its top and bottom edges parallel, so each leg keeps one platform edge parallel to its upper-arm axis. Three legs at 120° lock all three rotations, leaving 3 translations. Plain G–K counts each rod’s idle spin as a DoF (9), so subtract RM = 6 to get 3.

P4.6 · Spot the errorspot the error

“The slider-crank (ground, crank, connecting rod, slider; 3 revolute joints and 1 prismatic) has M = 6(4 − 4 − 1) + 4 = −2, so it is a structure.” What is wrong?

Answer

It is planar, so D = 3: M = 3(4 − 4 − 1) + 4 = 1. A slider-crank has one DoF (crank angle ↔ slider position).

Mini-quiz

Q1concept

Why is FK hard and IK easy for parallel robots, the opposite of serial ones?

Answer

FK needs the passive joints, which are found only by solving the coupled nonlinear loop-closure equations (multiple, possibly imaginary solutions). IK gives each leg’s end pose directly, so each leg is a small independent serial IK.

Q2calculate

A spatial mechanism has N = 6, J = 7, joints: 5 R, 1 U, 1 S. Plain G–K mobility?

Answer

ΣF = 5 + 2 + 3 = 10; M = 6(6 − 7 − 1) + 10 = −2. Report it, then look for over-constraints (RC) before concluding it is a structure.

Q3concept

Name the two meanings of RM and RC in one line each.

Answer

RM: internal motions that do not move the output (subtract). RC: constraints counted twice because they repeat each other (add back).
