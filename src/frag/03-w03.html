Week 3 · Inverse kinematics

Which joint values put the tool where I want it?

IK runs FK backwards: from a desired pose to joint values. Unlike FK, the answer may not exist, may not be unique, and may need a numerical search. The exam skill is the closed-form derivation: project, apply the cosine rule, use atan2, list every branch.

Practical (optional)

The W3 practical folder holds the PUMA 560 FK scripts (proximal DH and distal ETS), which pair with W2. rrp_ik.py in the lecture folder is, despite its name, a planar 2R arm (L1 = L2 = 1). It has the analytic IK (both elbows), its 2×2 Jacobian, and a Newton–Raphson solver. Its cos q2 is clipped to [−1, 1], so an unreachable target silently returns the nearest boundary pose.

Intuition first

Touch your nose two ways

Keep your fingertip on your nose and move your elbow: the hand stays put while the arm changes. A robot arm has the same freedom at its “elbow”, so most reachable points have an elbow-up and an elbow-down answer.

A triangle does the work

For two links, shoulder, elbow and tip form a triangle with known sides L1, L2 and the distance to the target. The cosine rule gives the elbow angle; the rest is pointing.

Split position from orientation

With a spherical wrist, the first three joints carry the wrist centre (like your shoulder and elbow placing your wrist). The last three only turn the hand. Two small problems instead of one big one.

Numerical IK = walking downhill

Measure how far the tool is from the goal, use the Jacobian as a local map of “which joint moves the tool where”, step, and repeat. Like adjusting a desk lamp by small nudges until the light lands on the page.

1 · The IK problem and its spaces pp. 7–11

Picture

Configuration (joint) space 𝒞 holds the joint vector q; for two revolute joints \(\mathcal C=\mathbb S^1\times\mathbb S^1\) (each angle in [0, 2π)). Task space 𝒯 holds the tool pose ξ, written as \({}^0\mathbf T_E\), as (x, y, z, roll, pitch, yaw), or as position + quaternion (p.9). FK is \(\xi=\mathcal K(\mathbf q)\); IK is \(\mathbf q=\mathcal K^{-1}(\xi)\) (p.11). 𝒦 is a many-to-one, nonlinear map, which is why its inverse is hard.

Definitions are cheap marks: be able to say what 𝒞 and 𝒯 are for a given robot, and why IK can have 0, 1, several or infinitely many solutions.

2 · Does a solution exist? Workspace pp. 12–16

Picture

Reachable workspace: every point the tool can reach in at least one orientation. Dexterous workspace: every point it can reach in every orientation. For a full 3D pose you need the dexterous workspace (notebook Q1).

“Sketch the workspace” (W13 Q2) is this idea in 3D. Always give the radii and say why (cosine rule: distance ranges from |L1−L2| to L1+L2).

3 · Multiple solutions and how to choose pp. 17–20

Picture

A reachable point usually has several joint solutions (p.17). To pick one (p.18): the closest to the current configuration (smallest absolute or weighted joint change; prefer moving small distal joints over large proximal ones), or the one that avoids obstacles (p.19). Solution methods (p.20): closed form (algebraic, geometric, polynomial; non-iterative; all solutions known) or numerical (iterative; slower; handles long chains and returns a close-enough answer when no exact one exists, p.54).

A 6-DoF arm has no continuous redundancy for a 6-number pose (p.33), but it still has several discrete solutions. The PUMA 560 has up to 8: shoulder left/right × elbow up/down × wrist flip. added: standard result, Craig ch. 4

Slides vs slides

Slide 54 lists “unique solution to all positions” for closed form. Read it as “a definite, complete set of solutions”: slides 17 and 20 show that one point can have several solutions.

4 · Planar RP arm pp. 23–24

Derivation

A revolute joint q1 followed by a prismatic extension S along the link: tip \((x,y)=(S\cos q_1,\;S\sin q_1)\). Polar coordinates invert it directly:

\[ S=\sqrt{x^2+y^2},\qquad q_1=\operatorname{atan2}(y,x) \]

The slide writes \(\tan^{-1}(y/x)\) (p.24); use atan2 for the quadrant. If S may be negative, (−S, q1+π) is a second solution.

Worked example

(x, y) = (0.3, 0.4) m → S = 0.5 m, q1 = 53.130°. checked

5 · Planar RR arm, algebraic solution, and atan2 pp. 25–30

Derivation

FK (p.26): \(x=L_1c_1+L_2c_{12}\), \(y=L_1s_1+L_2s_{12}\). Square and add; cross terms collapse with \(c_1c_{12}+s_1s_{12}=\cos q_2\):

\[ x^2+y^2=L_1^2+L_2^2+2L_1L_2\cos q_2\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;\cos q_2=\frac{x^2+y^2-L_1^2-L_2^2}{2L_1L_2} \] \[ \sin q_2=\pm\sqrt{1-\cos^2q_2},\qquad q_2=\operatorname{atan2}(\sin q_2,\;\cos q_2) \]

The ± is the elbow: two solutions (p.28). If \(|\cos q_2|>1\) the point is out of reach.

For q1, expand the compound angles (p.30): \(x=(L_1+L_2c_2)c_1-(L_2s_2)s_1\), \(y=(L_2s_2)c_1+(L_1+L_2c_2)s_1\). This is the point (x, y) seen as the vector \((L_1+L_2c_2,\;L_2s_2)\) rotated by q1, so

\[ q_1=\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)-\operatorname{atan2}\big(L_2\sin q_2,\;L_1+L_2\cos q_2\big) \]

One q1 for each q2. The slide’s general tool for \(A\cos\varphi+B\sin\varphi=C\) gives the same result: \(\varphi=\operatorname{atan2}(C,\pm\sqrt{A^2+B^2-C^2})-\operatorname{atan2}(A,B)\).

atan2 (p.29) returns the angle of the point (x, y) in (−π, π], using both signs. \(\tan^{-1}(y/x)\) cannot tell (1, 1) from (−1, −1): both give 45°, but the second is −135°. checked

Exam recipe (any 2-link planar sub-problem)
  1. Write FK. Identify the shoulder point and the target in the plane (subtract offsets first).
  2. \(\cos q_2\) by square-and-add (or the cosine rule). Check \(|\cos q_2|\le1\).
  3. \(q_2=\operatorname{atan2}(\pm\sqrt{1-c_2^2},\,c_2)\): two branches.
  4. \(q_1=\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)-\operatorname{atan2}(L_2s_2,\,L_1+L_2c_2)\) for each branch.
  5. Check one branch by FK.
Worked example

L1 = 1.0 m, L2 = 0.7 m, target (1.2, 0.8) m. \(\cos q_2=(1.44+0.64-1-0.49)/1.4 = 0.421429\), so q2 = ±65.075°. atan2(0.8, 1.2) = 33.690°.

Elbow with q2 = +65.075°: q1 = 33.690° − 26.114° = 7.576°. With q2 = −65.075°: q1 = 33.690° + 26.114° = 59.804°. Both reproduce (1.2, 0.8). checked

6 · Planar RR arm, geometric solution p. 31

Derivation (triangle OAB)

O is the base, A the elbow, B the tip at distance \(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\). The interior angle at A is \(\pi-q_2\) (q2 is measured from link 1’s extension). Cosine rule:

\[ x^2+y^2=L_1^2+L_2^2-2L_1L_2\cos(\pi-q_2)=L_1^2+L_2^2+2L_1L_2\cos q_2 \]

the same as the algebra. Sine rule for the angle μ at O between OA and OB: \(\dfrac{\sin\mu}{L_2}=\dfrac{\sin(\pi-q_2)}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}=\dfrac{\sin q_2}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}\). With \(\sigma=\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)\), the slide’s answer is \(q_1=\sigma-\mu\).

Signs: μ takes the sign of q2. For the elbow drawn on the slide (q2 > 0) the tip is anticlockwise of link 1, so link 1 lies below the line OB. For the other branch μ < 0 and q1 = σ + |μ|. Computing μ = atan2(L2s2, L1+L2c2) handles both automatically. checked for both branches

Geometric answers earn full marks if the triangle is drawn and labelled. Write “interior angle at the elbow = π − q2” explicitly; that one line prevents the sign error below.

7 · Six-DoF arm with a spherical wrist: decouple position and orientation pp. 32–38

Picture

A spherical wrist has its last three axes meeting at one point, the wrist centre W (p.34). Rotating joints 4–6 cannot move W, so W’s position depends only on q1–q3. Solve q1–q3 from W, then q4–q6 from the remaining rotation.

Derivation

Step 0, wrist centre added: if the tool point lies a distance d6 beyond W along the approach axis \(\hat z_E\), then \(\mathbf p_W=\mathbf p_E-d_6\,{}^0\mathbf R_E\,[0\;0\;1]^\top\) (the third column of R scaled by d6).

Step 1 (top view, p.35): \(q_1=\operatorname{atan2}(y_W,x_W)\), second branch q1+π.

Step 2 (the arm’s vertical plane, p.36): shoulder height H, upper arm G, forearm F, \(r_W^2=x_W^2+y_W^2\). With q3 the relative elbow angle (anticlockwise positive, same as q2 in the 2R arm):

\[ \cos q_3=\frac{r_W^2+(z_W-H)^2-F^2-G^2}{2FG},\qquad q_3=\operatorname{atan2}\big(\pm\sqrt{1-\cos^2q_3},\cos q_3\big) \]

Step 3 (p.37): \(\sigma=\operatorname{atan2}(z_W-H,\;r_W)\), \(\mu=\operatorname{atan2}(F\sin q_3,\;G+F\cos q_3)\), and with signed angles \(q_2=\sigma-\mu\).

Step 4, wrist (p.38): compute \({}^0\mathbf R_3\) from q1–q3, then \({}^3\mathbf R_6=({}^0\mathbf R_3)^\top\,{}^0\mathbf R_6\), and read q4–q6 as Euler angles. For a Z–Y–Z wrist \({}^3\mathbf R_6=R_z(q_4)R_y(q_5)R_z(q_6)\) added:

\[ q_5=\operatorname{atan2}\!\big(\sqrt{r_{13}^2+r_{23}^2},\,r_{33}\big),\quad q_4=\operatorname{atan2}(r_{23},\,r_{13}),\quad q_6=\operatorname{atan2}(r_{32},\,-r_{31}) \]

The other sign of q5 is the wrist flip (q4, q6 each change by π). If q5 = 0 the wrist is singular: only q4+q6 is determined. checked: extraction and flip both reproduce R

Slides vs maths: elbow sign on pp. 36–37

Slide 36 writes \(\cos(\pi-q_3)\) on the left. By the cosine rule (the interior angle at the elbow is π − q3), the right-hand side equals \(+\cos q_3\), not \(\cos(\pi-q_3)\) checked. Slide 37’s \(q_2=\sigma+\mu\) is correct when q3 is read as a clockwise bend (the drawn elbow-up pose) and μ as a positive angle checked. In the exam, define your positive direction on the sketch and stay with it. Signed anticlockwise angles give the formulas above.

Worked example (position part)

H = 0.5, G = 0.4, F = 0.3 m; wrist centre W = (0.4813, 0.4039, 0.5974) m. Then q1 = 40°, rW = 0.6283 m, zW − H = 0.0974 m, cos q3 = 0.6428, q3 = ±50°.

q3 = −50° (elbow up) gives q2 = 30°; q3 = +50° (elbow down) gives q2 = −12.378°. With the second q1 branch (220°) that is 4 position solutions; times 2 wrist flips makes 8. checked

8 · Numerical IK: goal potential, Newton–Raphson, pseudo-inverse pp. 40–52

Derivation

IK as optimisation (p.41): \(\mathbf q^*=\arg\min_{\mathbf q}|\mathcal K(\mathbf q)\ominus\xi^*|\). The quantity being minimised is the goal potential \(G(\mathbf q)\). For position only (p.42), \(G=(x^*-x)^2+(y^*-y)^2\).

Newton–Raphson (pp. 45–47): linearise \(G(q)\approx G(q^k)+G'(q^k)(q-q^k)\), set it to 0: \(q^{k+1}=q^k-G'(q^k)^{-1}G(q^k)\). Stop when the relative change is below ε.

For IK (pp. 48–52) the derivative is the Jacobian \(\mathbf J=\partial\mathcal F/\partial\mathbf q\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}\). With error \(\mathbf e=\mathbf x^*-\mathcal F(\mathbf q^i)\), the update is

\[ \mathbf q^{i+1}=\mathbf q^i+\mathbf J^{\dagger}(\mathbf q^i)\,\mathbf e,\qquad \mathbf J^\dagger=\begin{cases}\mathbf J^{-1} & n=m\\ \mathbf J^\top(\mathbf J\mathbf J^\top)^{-1} & n>m\ (\text{“fat”: infinitely many }\Delta\mathbf q\text{, picks the smallest})\\ (\mathbf J^\top\mathbf J)^{-1}\mathbf J^\top & n<m\ (\text{“tall”: no exact }\Delta\mathbf q\text{, least squares})\end{cases} \]

pseudo-inverse forms checked against numpy Caveats (notebook Q4–Q6): G = 0 may be impossible (target outside the workspace); the method may stall in a local minimum or diverge near a singularity; the answer found depends on the initial guess.

Worked example: one Newton step

2R arm (1.0, 0.7) m, target (1.2, 0.8), start q0 = (0.5, 0.5) rad. FK gives (1.2558, 1.0685), so e0 = (−0.0558, −0.2685).

\[ \mathbf J(\mathbf q^0)=\begin{bmatrix}-L_1s_1-L_2s_{12} & -L_2s_{12}\\ L_1c_1+L_2c_{12} & L_2c_{12}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-1.0685&-0.5890\\1.2558&0.3782\end{bmatrix} \]

\(\Delta\mathbf q=\mathbf J^{-1}\mathbf e^0=(-0.5341,\;1.0635)\), so q1 = (−0.0341, 1.5635) rad and |e1| = 0.2181 (down from 0.2742). It converges to the elbow branch (7.576°, 65.075°) in 5 iterations. checked

Calculation risk: J is 2×2 here, so invert it by hand: \(\mathbf J^{-1}=\frac{1}{ad-bc}\begin{bmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{bmatrix}\). The explain-type question asks why numerical IK may fail. Name three reasons (unreachable, local minimum or singular J, initial guess).

9 · When does a closed form exist? pp. 55–56

Any 6-DoF serial chain of R and P joints is solvable, but not always analytically. A sufficient condition for a closed form: three neighbouring joint axes intersect at a point (p.56), e.g. a spherical wrist. This is Pieper’s result; three consecutive parallel axes also work added: Craig ch. 4.

P3.6 · Spot the errorspot the error

A student solving a 2R arm writes \(x^2+y^2=L_1^2+L_2^2-2L_1L_2\cos q_2\) and gets q2 = ±114.9° for the target of P3.1. What went wrong?

Answer

They used the cosine rule with the joint angle q2 instead of the interior angle π − q2. This flips the sign of cos q2: they found the supplement (180° − 65.075° = 114.925°). The same slip is printed on slide 36.

P3.7 · Numerical IK caveats (notebook Q4–Q6)explain

Is it always possible to reach G(q) = 0? Will the solver always find a solution that exists? Is it unique?

Model answer

No: outside the reachable workspace the minimum of G is positive. No: Newton steps can stall in a local minimum or blow up where J is singular, and the result depends on the start. No: a redundant arm has infinitely many solutions, and even a 6-DoF arm has several; the initial guess decides which one is found.

Mini-quiz

Q1concept

Why do we need the dexterous rather than the reachable workspace when a full pose is specified?

Answer

The reachable workspace only guarantees some orientation; a full pose fixes the orientation too, so the point must be reachable in that orientation.

Q2calculate

For L1 = 1, L2 = 0.7, is (0, 0.2) reachable?

Answer

No. Its distance 0.2 m is below |L1 − L2| = 0.3 m, so cos q2 = (0.04 − 1.49)/1.4 = −1.036 < −1.

Q3spot the error

“A tall Jacobian (n < m) uses J† = Jᵀ(JJᵀ)⁻¹.” True?

Answer

False. For tall J, JJᵀ (m×m) is singular; use (JᵀJ)⁻¹Jᵀ (least squares). Jᵀ(JJᵀ)⁻¹ is for fat J (n > m).
