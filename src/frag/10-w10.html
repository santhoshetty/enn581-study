Week 10 · Wheeled robots: kinematics

How fast must each wheel spin to move the robot like this?

A wheeled robot’s chassis moves with velocity (u, v, θ̇) in its own frame. Each wheel only cares about the velocity of its contact point along its driving direction. One formula, the generalised wheel model, turns that sentence into a row of a matrix. Stack one row per wheel and you have the robot’s kinematics.

Intuition first

A wheel is a one-way street

A normal wheel rolls forwards and backwards but refuses to slide sideways. That refusal is a constraint on the chassis: it is why a car cannot parallel-park by moving sideways.

An omni wheel is a wheel made of wheels

Small rollers round the rim let it slide freely along one direction while it drives along another. So it constrains nothing: three of them can push the chassis in any direction.

Every point on a turning body circles one point

At each instant a planar rigid body rotates about its instantaneous centre of rotation (ICR). Every wheel axle that cannot slip must point at it, like spokes at a hub.

Holonomic = you can get anywhere, any way

A car can reach any parking spot, but not along any path: its velocity is restricted, not its position. That is a non-holonomic constraint. An omni robot has no such restriction.

1 · Frames and the chassis velocity pp. 11–17

Derivation

Inertial frame {O}; chassis frame {C} at angle θ (anticlockwise from xO). The chassis velocity in its own axes is ζ = (u, v, θ̇): u forwards along xC, v sideways along yC (p.13). In {O} it is ν = (ẋ, ẏ, θ̇):

\[ \boldsymbol\nu=\begin{bmatrix}\dot x\\\dot y\\\dot\theta\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf J(\theta)\,\boldsymbol\zeta=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta&0\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\\\dot\theta\end{bmatrix},\qquad \boldsymbol\zeta=\mathbf J^{-1}(\theta)\boldsymbol\nu=\mathbf J^\top(\theta)\boldsymbol\nu \]

We command wheel speeds, not (u, v, θ̇), so the real relation is \(\boldsymbol\zeta=\mathbf W\dot{\boldsymbol\varphi}\) with W set by the wheel layout (p.17). Forward kinematics (wheels → motion) is for simulation; inverse (motion → wheels) is for control.

2 · Wheel types and pure rolling pp. 18–25

Picture

Five types (p.18): fixed standard (axle fixed to the chassis), steerable standard (axle turns about a vertical axis), castor (offset steering, passive), Swedish/omni (rollers on the rim), spherical (a ball). Only the two standard wheels impose a constraint on the chassis, because they cannot slide sideways. Castor, Swedish and spherical wheels impose none. Assumption throughout: pure rolling, no slip at the contact point (p.20).

Omni wheel in its own frame {W}: xW is the driving direction, yW the wheel-plane normal; the rollers let the contact slide freely along a direction at angle γ from yW (pp. 22–25). The contact velocity splits into a driven part and a free part:

\[ \begin{bmatrix}v_x\\v_y\end{bmatrix}=v_{drive}\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}+v_{free}\begin{bmatrix}-\sin\gamma\\\cos\gamma\end{bmatrix}\;\Rightarrow\;v_{drive}=v_x+v_y\tan\gamma,\qquad \dot\varphi=\frac{v_{drive}}{r}=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac1r&\tfrac{\tan\gamma}{r}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}v_x\\v_y\end{bmatrix} \]

With rollers perpendicular to the drive direction, γ = 0 and \(\dot\varphi=v_x/r\): the wheel ignores the sideways component.

3 · The generalised wheel model pp. 26–36

Derivation (three steps)

Step 1 (p.28): the wheel frame is turned by βi from the chassis (β = angle from xC to the drive direction), so \({}^C\mathbf v_{W_i}=R(\beta_i)\,{}^{W_i}\mathbf v\).

Step 2 (p.30): the wheel sits at \(({}^Cx_{W_i},{}^Cy_{W_i})\) on a rigid chassis turning at θ̇, so its velocity is the chassis velocity plus \(\dot\theta\hat z\times\mathbf p\):

\[ {}^C\mathbf v_{W_i}=\begin{bmatrix}u-\dot\theta\,{}^Cy_{W_i}\\ v+\dot\theta\,{}^Cx_{W_i}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&-{}^Cy_{W_i}\\0&1&{}^Cx_{W_i}\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol\zeta \]

Combine and pick out the drive component (p.36), adding R⁻¹(θ) to start from inertial velocities:

\[ \dot\varphi_i=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac1{r_i}&\tfrac{\tan\gamma_i}{r_i}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\cos\beta_i&\sin\beta_i\\-\sin\beta_i&\cos\beta_i\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&0&-{}^Cy_{W_i}\\0&1&{}^Cx_{W_i}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta&0\\-\sin\theta&\cos\theta&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\dot x\\\dot y\\\dot\theta\end{bmatrix} \]

Read right to left: inertial → chassis; chassis twist → contact-point velocity; chassis axes → wheel axes; keep the driven component, divide by r. equals the contact velocity projected on the drive direction, checked

Standard wheels: two equations (slides 37–42, 68)

Fixed standard wheel: γ = 0, β constant. Steerable standard: γ = 0, β = β(t). Each gives a rolling equation (the model above) and a no-sliding equation (the axle component must vanish):

\[ r_i\dot\varphi_i=\big[\cos\beta_i\;\;\sin\beta_i\;\;-{}^Cy\cos\beta_i+{}^Cx\sin\beta_i\big]R^{-1}(\theta)\boldsymbol\nu,\qquad 0=\big[-\sin\beta_i\;\;\cos\beta_i\;\;{}^Cy\sin\beta_i+{}^Cx\cos\beta_i\big]R^{-1}(\theta)\boldsymbol\nu \]

both rows checked against the model and the rigid-body contact velocity The no-sliding rows are the non-holonomic constraints (p.68).

Exam recipe (any wheeled robot)
  1. Draw {C}. For each wheel write Cx, Cy, β (angle of the drive direction from xC), γ, r. Say which way positive φ̇ drives.
  2. Write one row per wheel: \(r\dot\varphi_i=\cos\beta_i(u-\dot\theta\,{}^Cy)+\sin\beta_i(v+\dot\theta\,{}^Cx)\) (γ = 0).
  3. For standard wheels add the no-slide row. It usually forces v = 0.
  4. Stack the rows: \(\dot{\boldsymbol\varphi}=\mathbf W^{-1}\boldsymbol\zeta\). Invert (or pseudo-invert) for \(\boldsymbol\zeta=\mathbf W\dot{\boldsymbol\varphi}\).
  5. Multiply by J(θ) for inertial velocities. Check a simple motion (straight ahead; spin on the spot).

4 · Differential drive and skid-steer pp. 43–52

Derivation

Two fixed standard wheels at (0, ±a), radius r, β = 0, γ = 0 (p.44). Rolling rows: \(\dot\varphi_1=(u-a\dot\theta)/r\) (left, +a) and \(\dot\varphi_2=(u+a\dot\theta)/r\) (right, −a). No-slide: v = 0. Inverting (p.47):

\[ \begin{bmatrix}u\\v\\\dot\theta\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac r2&\tfrac r2\\0&0\\-\tfrac{r}{2a}&\tfrac{r}{2a}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\dot\varphi_1\\\dot\varphi_2\end{bmatrix} \]

Skid-steer, 4 standard wheels at (±b, ±a) (pp. 49–52): the rows are (1/r)[1, 0, −a] for the left pair and (1/r)[1, 0, +a] for the right pair. b drops out. The slide’s 3×4 inverse \(u=\tfrac r4\sum\dot\varphi_i\), \(\dot\theta=\tfrac{r}{4a}(-\dot\varphi_1-\dot\varphi_2+\dot\varphi_3+\dot\varphi_4)\) is the least-squares (pseudo-) inverse checked. Note that a real skid-steer must slip to turn at all, which the pure-rolling model ignores.

Worked example

r = 0.05 m, a = 0.15 m, \(\dot\varphi\) = (4, 6) rad/s: u = 0.05(10)/2 = 0.25 m/s, θ̇ = 0.05(2)/0.3 = 0.3333 rad/s. ICR at y = u/θ̇ = 0.75 m to the left. checked

5 · Omnidirectional robots pp. 53–55

Three Swedish wheels at the corners of an equilateral triangle (centre-to-wheel d), drive directions tangential, rollers perpendicular (γ = 0). With the drive directions taken clockwise (β = 0°, −120°, +120°) the rows give

\[ \dot{\boldsymbol\varphi}=\frac1r\begin{bmatrix}1&0&-d\\-\tfrac12&-\tfrac{\sqrt3}2&-d\\-\tfrac12&\tfrac{\sqrt3}2&-d\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol\zeta,\qquad \boldsymbol\zeta=\frac r3\begin{bmatrix}2&-1&-1\\0&-\sqrt3&\sqrt3\\-\tfrac1d&-\tfrac1d&-\tfrac1d\end{bmatrix}\dot{\boldsymbol\varphi} \]

The matrix is invertible (det = 3√3d/2), so any (u, v, θ̇) is possible: holonomic. Full derivation: Sample Q10. The four-wheel Swedish layout (p.55) works the same way with 45° rollers (tan γ ≠ 0). checked

6 · Tricycle, car, Ackermann, bicycle pp. 56–61, 69

Derivation

Tricycle (pp. 56–58): front wheel W1 at (b, 0), driven and steered (β1(t)); rear wheels fixed and passive. If the rear axle passes through C (the slide assumes this without saying it), the rear no-slide rows force v = 0. Step 2 gives the front contact velocity (u, bθ̇), and step 1 says it equals \(r\dot\varphi_1(\cos\beta_1,\sin\beta_1)\):

\[ \dot\varphi_1=\frac1r\sqrt{u^2+b^2\dot\theta^2},\qquad \beta_1=\operatorname{atan2}(b\dot\theta,\;u) \]

Ackermann steering (p.61): the two front wheels are steered by different amounts so that every axle line passes through one ICR on the rear axle’s line, so no wheel scrubs. Geometry added: \(\cot\beta_{out}-\cot\beta_{in}=\text{track}/\text{wheelbase}\). Example: wheelbase 2.5 m, track 1.5 m, turn radius 6 m about the rear-axle midpoint: inner 25.463°, outer 20.323°. checked

Bicycle (practical Q4, slide 69): rear wheel fixed at C, driven; front wheel at (L, 0), steered by β, not driven. Rear no-slide: v = 0. Front no-slide: \(-u\sin\beta+L\dot\theta\cos\beta=0\), so

\[ \dot\theta=\frac{u\tan\beta}{L},\qquad \text{turning radius } R=\frac{L}{\tan\beta} \]

checked Steering jammed straight (β = 0): θ̇ = 0 and v = 0, so it can only roll forwards or backwards.

7 · Manoeuvrability; holonomic vs non-holonomic pp. 62–68

Definitions

Degree of manoeuvrability δM = δm + δs (p.63). The slide’s informal wording: δm (mobility) ≈ how many directions the chassis can be driven in directly; δs (steerability) ≈ how many independent steering angles. A precise version added: Siegwart et al. ch. 3: stack the no-slide rows of all standard wheels into C1; then \(\delta_m=3-\operatorname{rank}C_1\) and \(\delta_s=\operatorname{rank}C_{1s}\) (the steered wheels’ rows). It reproduces all four slide examples (p.64):

RobotδmδsδM
2 fixed wheels on one axle + castor (differential drive)202
2 fixed + 1 steered standard (tricycle / car-like)112
2 steered standard + 1 Swedish123
3 Swedish (omni triangle)303

rank computation checked for each row

Holonomic constraint (p.66): an equation in positions only, f(x, y, θ, …) = 0. Non-holonomic (p.67): involves velocities and cannot be integrated into a position-only equation. The no-sliding condition of a standard wheel, \(-\dot x\sin\theta+\dot y\cos\theta=0\) for a differential drive, is the classic example. It forbids sideways velocity at every instant but not any final position: parallel parking is possible, just not directly. Holonomic robot: no non-holonomic constraints, so δM = δm = 3 (the omni triangle).

Explain-type marks: “is the bicycle holonomic?” No. Its rear no-slide constraint restricts velocity, not position, and cannot be integrated, yet the bicycle can still reach any (x, y, θ) by manoeuvring. Name the constraint equation, say “velocity, not integrable”, and give the consequence.

P10.2 · Where does it go? (practical Q2)calculatesketch

d = 0.10 m, r = 0.05 m, constant \(\dot{\boldsymbol\varphi}\) = (4, −1, −1) rad/s, starting at the origin with θ = 0. Find (u, v, θ̇), describe the path, and give the pose after 2 s.

Hint 1

Use the inverse matrix of section 5.

Hint 2

Constant (u, v, θ̇) means a circle about the ICR, which is at \((-v/\dot\theta,\;u/\dot\theta)\) in the chassis frame.

Full solution

u = (0.05/3)(8 + 1 + 1) = 0.1667 m/s; v = (0.05/√3)(−1 + 1) = 0; θ̇ = −(0.05/0.3)(4 − 1 − 1) = −0.3333 rad/s. ICR at (0, u/θ̇) = (0, −0.5) m: a clockwise circle of radius 0.5 m. After 2 s, θ = −0.6667 rad, x = R sin|θ| = 0.5 sin 0.6667 = 0.3092 m, y = −0.5(1 − cos 0.6667) = −0.1071 m. Euler integration with dt = 0.01 s lands within 0.55 mm of this. checked

P10.3 · ICR of a differential drive (practical Q3)derivecalculate

Derive the position of the ICR for wheel speeds \(\dot\varphi_1\) (left, at +a) and \(\dot\varphi_2\) (right, at −a). Evaluate for a = 0.3 m, \(\dot\varphi\) = (2, 5) rad/s.

Hint

The ICR is the chassis point with zero velocity: \(u-\dot\theta y=0\), v = 0 → on the axle at y = u/θ̇.

Full solution

\(y_{ICR}=\dfrac{u}{\dot\theta}=\dfrac{r(\dot\varphi_1+\dot\varphi_2)/2}{r(\dot\varphi_2-\dot\varphi_1)/(2a)}=a\dfrac{\dot\varphi_1+\dot\varphi_2}{\dot\varphi_2-\dot\varphi_1}\), on the axle (x = 0). Equal speeds: at infinity (straight). Opposite speeds: at the centre (spin). Here 0.3 × 7/3 = 0.7 m. r cancels. checked

P10.4 · Bicycle (practical Q4)deriveexplain

Rear wheel fixed and driven, front wheel steered and not driven, wheelbase L. Derive the kinematics. Is it holonomic? Evaluate θ̇ for u = 2 m/s, β = 20°, L = 1.2 m.

Full solution

Rear rolling: \(r\dot\varphi_r=u\). Rear no-slide: v = 0. Front no-slide (β, at (L, 0)): \(-u\sin\beta+L\dot\theta\cos\beta=0\Rightarrow\dot\theta=u\tan\beta/L\). In {O}: \(\dot x=u\cos\theta\), \(\dot y=u\sin\theta\), \(\dot\theta=u\tan\beta/L\). Non-holonomic: \(-\dot x\sin\theta+\dot y\cos\theta=0\) restricts velocity only and cannot be integrated. Numbers: 2 tan 20°/1.2 = 0.6066 rad/s, turning radius 3.297 m. checked

A tricycle with the front wheel at b = 0.6 m (r = 0.1 m) must move at u = 0.4 m/s while turning at θ̇ = 0.5 rad/s. Find \(\dot\varphi_1\) and β1.

P10.6 · Spot the errorspot the error

“For a differential drive, \(\dot\varphi_1=(u+a\dot\theta)/r\) for the left wheel at y = +a; and since both wheels are standard, v can be any value.”

Answer

The left wheel’s contact velocity is \(u-\dot\theta\,y=u-a\dot\theta\): turning left (θ̇ > 0) slows the inner (left) wheel. And the no-slide constraint of the fixed wheels forces v = 0: a standard wheel cannot slide sideways.

Mini-quiz

Q1concept

Which wheel types constrain the chassis motion?

Answer

Only standard wheels (fixed and steerable), which cannot slide sideways. Castor, Swedish and spherical wheels impose no constraint.

Q2calculate

Differential drive, r = 0.1 m, a = 0.2 m, \(\dot\varphi\) = (−5, 5) rad/s. u and θ̇?

Answer

u = 0; θ̇ = 0.1(10)/0.4 = 2.5 rad/s: a spin on the spot about C.

Q3concept

What does δM = 3 mean for the omni triangle?

Answer

All three chassis velocities (u, v, θ̇) can be set directly and independently: the robot is holonomic and omnidirectional.
