### Kinematics and dynamics

**Kinematics** is the study of motion of objects without considering the forces that cause it. In other words, we are interested only in the geometry of motion. In the context of robotics, this can be the motion of a robot arm, a mobile robot, a drone, a humanoid robot, etc. 

**Dynamics**, on the other hand, is the study of the forces and torques required to cause motion. This is the study of why an object moves or does not move. The field of dynamics is in general divided into two branches: **Statics** - in which the object does not experience any acceleration, and **Kinetics** - in which the object does experience acceleration.

These terms can often get very confusing, especially becasue they dont originate in English and these words are sometimes used interchangeably in other languages. We will stick to the above distinctions in this course.

### Mechanism

A mechanism is a mechanical system that transfers/modifies motion and/or force from one or more sources to one or more outputs. 

Let us begin our study with robot arms and revisit these concepts later in the course when we study mobile robots.

A typical robot manipulator arm is a mechanism that transfers motion and torque from the motors to the end effector. It consists of a series of links (rigid mechanical structures) connected by joints (elements that allow relative motion between the links). Such a system of links and joints is called a kinematic chain. A mechanism can have one or more kinematics chains between a fixed base and the end effector.

### Degrees of freedom

A mechanism like a robot arm moves around in space. Its configuration is constantly changing. We would like to be able to describe this configuration in a way that is easy to understand and analyze. The number of independent parameters required to describe the configuration of a mechanism is called its degrees of freedom (DOF). Let us consider a few examples to understand this concept better.


<!-- cell 5 md -->
**A point in 2D space**  
In how many direction can a point move in 2D space? It can move in two directions - along the two axes that define the plane. The configuration of such a point is its position on the plane with respect to a chosen reference frame (for example, its x and y coordinates). We therefore need two independent parameters to describe its configuration. Hence, a point in 2D space has 2 degrees of freedom. A point has no orientation, so we do not need to consider any rotational parameters.

**A pendulum in 2D space**  
A pendulum is a mechanism with a single link that can rotate around a fixed point. The configuration of the pendulum is the position of the mass in the 2D plane. Let us assume that this is the x-y plane. Although the mass can move in both x and y directions, its motion is constrained by the length of the link and the point of suspension. In other words, the motion in the x direction is related to the motion in the y direction, and knowing one will tell us about the other. This relation is captured by the angle that the swinging link makes with a reference axis. We therefore need only one independent parameter to describe the configuration of the pendulum. Hence, a pendulum in 2D space has 1 degree of freedom.


In this example, the length of the link is assumed to be known. Similarly, when we talk about degrees of freedom of mechanisms, we will always assume that the geometry of the links are given. Although the overall movement of the links might change when the geometry changes, the degrees of freedom are independent of the geometry.

**A rigid body in 3D space**  
A rigid body in 3D spcae has six possible motions - three translations along the x, y, and z axes, and three rotations around the x, y, and z axes. The configuration of a rigid body is its position and orientation in 3D space. We therefore need six independent parameters to describe its configuration. Hence, a rigid body in 3D space has 6 degrees of freedom.

### Joints add constraints to the degrees of freedom
We saw that in the case of the pendulum, a revolute (a.k.a hinge, pin) type joint reduced the possible degrees of freedom from 2 to 1. Links, which are rigid bodies, start with 6 (or 3) DOF in 3D (or 2D) space. Introducing a joint in between two links, reduces the degrees of freedom of the mechanism. Joints restrict the motion of links in one or more axes by limiting translation or rotation. The type of joint and the number of joints in a mechanism determine the number of degrees of freedom of the mechanism.

Consider the example of two planar links in 2D x-y space as shown in the figure. Each of the links is free to move in the x-y plane and can have an arbitrary orientation. When not connected by a joint, each link has 3 DOF and we need 6 parameters to describe the configuration of the two links. When connected by a revolute joint, knowing the position of one link, we can determine the position of the other link. Only the orientation of the second link is independent of the first link. The revolute joint reduces the DOF of the mechanism from 6 to 4.

### Controllable DOF
If a mechanism has an actuator for every DOF, then all the DOF are controllable. In the case of a robot arm, if a joint is actuated by a motor, then it is controllable. Joints without an actuator are not controllable. 

A robot is said to be **fully actuated** if the controllable DOF are equal to the total DOF of the mechanism.  
A robot is said to be **underactuated** if the controllable DOF are less than the total DOF of the mechanism.  
A robot is said to be **redundant** if the controllable DOF are more than the total DOF of the mechanism. An example of this is a 7DOF robot arm in 3D spcae.

### Mobility of a mechanism

The term mobility is also commonly used to describe the number of degrees of freedom of a mechanism. Particularly, it referss to the degrees of freedom remaining, after all the constraints have been applied.

In simple serial kinematic chains one can use the Grübler-Kutzbach equation to determine the mobility of a mechanism. Note here that both the adjectives "simple" and "serial" are important.

The Grübler-Kutzbach equation is given as follows.  
in 3D space: $M = 6(𝑁−1) − 5𝑃_1 − 4𝑃_2 − 3𝑃_3 − 2𝑃_4 − 𝑃_5$ and  
in 2D space: $M = 3(𝑁−1) − 2𝑃_1 − 𝑃_2$  
where,
- $M$ is the mobility of the mechanism,
- $N$ is the number of links in the mechanism (one of which is grounded), and
- $P_i$ is the number of link pairs having $i$ degrees of freedom.

<!-- cell 10 md -->
Here is another form of the Grübler-Kutzbach equation. 

$M = D(N - J - 1) + \sum_{i=1}^{J}{F_i} 
= D(N - 1) - DJ + \sum_{i=1}^{J}{F_i}$  
where,
- $M$ is the mobility of the mechanism,
- $D$ is 6 of 3D mechanisms and 3 for 2D mechanisms,
- $N$ is the number of links in the mechanism (one of which is grounded),
- $J$ is the number of joints in the mechanism,
- $F_i$ is the degrees of freedom of joint $i$.

To understand this equation, recall that joints constrain degrees of freedom of links. The first term $D(N - 1)$ is the total number of degrees of freedom of the links if there were no joints. From this, first subtract all degrees of freedom for each joint, i.e. $DJ$. Then give back the number of degrees of freedom allowed for each joint, i.e. $\sum_{i=1}^{J}{F_i}$.

Let us apply the first form of the Grübler-Kutzbach equation.  
It is a 2D case, so we use $M = 3(𝑁−1) − 2𝑃_1 − 𝑃_2$.  
$N = 4$ Link 0 is grounded and there are 3 other links.   
$P_1 = 3$ All three joints have 1 degree of freedom.  
$P_2 = 0$ There are no pairs of links with 2 degrees of freedom.  
Therefore, $M = 3(4-1) - 2*3 - 0 = 3$.

<!-- cell 13 md -->
The same result can be obtained using the second form of the Grübler-Kutzbach equation.  
$M= D(N - 1) - DJ + \sum_{i=1}^{J}{F_i}$.  
$D = 3$ for 2D mechanisms.  
$N = 4$  There are four links.  
$J = 3$  There are three joints.  
$F_1 = 1$  The first joint is revolute. It has 1 degree of freedom.  
$F_2 = 1$  The second joint is prismatic. It has 1 degree of freedom.  
$F_3 = 1$  The third joint is revolute. It has 1 degree of freedom.  
Therefore, $M = 3(4 - 1) - 3*3 + (1 + 1 + 1) = 3$.

### Workspace of a mechanism (a robot arm in this case)

The workspace of a manipulator robot arm is the set of all positions that the end effector can reach. The workspace is a function of the geometry of the robot arm (such as the lengths of the links) and the joint limits. The workspace can be visualized as a volume in 3D space or an area in 2D space. The workspace is important because it determines the range of tasks that the robot can perform.

### Reachable vs Dexterous workspace

The **reachable workspace** of a robot arm is the region of space that the end effector can reach in one orientation.  
The **dexterous workspace** of a robot arm is the region of space that the end effector can reach in all possible orientations.
