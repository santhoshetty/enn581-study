In this lesson, we will discuss the dynamics of robot manipulators. The study of manipulator dynamics builds on the dynamics of rigid bodies. Study of dynamics helps determine and relate the force that the tool is applying, the torque required at the motors, and accelerations of joints. 

There are two common problems in manipulator dynamics. In the first problem, we are given a trajectory $\textbf{q}, \dot{\textbf{q}}, \ddot{\textbf{q}}$ and we wish to determine the required vector of joint torques (or forces) $\textbf{Q}$. This is the inverse dynamics problem and is useful for controllng the manipulator. The second problem is to determine the motion of the manipulator $\textbf{q}, \dot{\textbf{q}}, \ddot{\textbf{q}}$ given the joint torques $\textbf{Q}$. This is the forward dynamics problem and is useful for simulation.

<!-- cell 4 md -->
### Joint forces and torques

<!-- cell 5 md -->
Actuators in a robot manipulator have to balance torques (or forces) from multiple sources. These include:
- Dynamic torques - those that arise due to motion
    - Inertial (proportional to acceleration)
    - Centripetal (those that constrain a body to rotate about a point, directed towards the center of rotation)
    - Coriolis (those that arise as a result of interaction between two rotating links)
- Static torques - those that arise from an external load
- Gravity torques - those that arise due to a link's mass
- Friction torques - those that arise due to friction in the joints
- Other external torques - those that arise from the task


<!-- cell 6 md -->
### Invere Dynamics

<!-- cell 7 md -->
We will first consider the inverse dynamics problem. There are two broad approaches to solving this problem. The first is the Lagrangian formulation and the second is the Newton-Euler formulation.

<!-- cell 8 md -->
#### Lagrangian Formulation

<!-- cell 9 md -->
The Lagrangian formualtion is an energy based approach in which the entire manipulator is considered as a single system. The key quantity in this method ia a scalar function called the Lagrangian ($\mathcal{L}$) which is defined as the difference between the kinetic energy ($K$) and the potential energy ($P$) of the system. 

\begin{align*}
\mathcal{L}\left(\textbf{q}, \dot{\textbf{q}}\right) = K\left(\textbf{q}, \dot{\textbf{q}}\right) - P\left(\textbf{q}\right)
\end{align*}

<!-- cell 10 md -->
The vector of joint torqes and forces can be expressed in terms of the Lagrangian as

\begin{align*}
\textbf{Q} = \frac{\mathrm{d} }{\mathrm{d} t } \left( \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}\left(\textbf{q}, \dot{\textbf{q} }\right)}{\partial \dot{\textbf{q} }} \right) - \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}\left(\textbf{q}, \dot{\textbf{q} }\right)}{\partial \textbf{q} }
\end{align*}

Consider the two link manipulator shown in the figure above. Let the mass of the llinks be concentrated at the distal end of each link as $m_1$ and $m_2$. Gravity is in the negative y direction.  
Given $\left(q_1, \dot{q}_1, \ddot{q}_1\right)$ and $\left(q_2, \dot{q}_2, \ddot{q}_2\right)$, our ai is to find $Q_1$ and $Q_2$. We begin by writing the kinetic and potential energy of the point masses in the current configuration.

<!-- cell 14 md -->
The position of mass $m_1$ is

\begin{align*}
\mathrm{x_1} &= \mathrm{L_1} cos(q_1) \\
\mathrm{y_1} &= \mathrm{L_1} sin(q_1) \\
\end{align*}

<!-- cell 15 md -->
Its velocity is then

\begin{align*}
\dot{\mathrm{x}}_1 &= -\mathrm{L_1} sin(q_1)\dot{q}_1 \\
\dot{\mathrm{y}}_1 &= \mathrm{L_1} cos(q_1)\dot{q}_1 \\
\end{align*}

<!-- cell 16 md -->
Therefore its kinetic energy can be written as

\begin{align*}
K_1 &= \frac{1}{2} m_1 (\dot{\mathrm{x}}_1^2 + \dot{\mathrm{y}}_1^2) = \frac{1}{2}m_1\mathrm{L}_1^2\dot{q}_1^2
\end{align*}

<!-- cell 17 md -->
Similarly, the position of $m_2$ is 

\begin{align*}
\mathrm{x_1} &= \mathrm{L_1} cos(q_1) + \mathrm{L_2} cos(q_1+q_2) \\
\mathrm{y_1} &= \mathrm{L_1} sin(q_1) + \mathrm{L_2} sin(q_1+q_2)\\
\end{align*}

<!-- cell 18 md -->
Its velocity is

\begin{align*}
\dot{\mathrm{x}}_2 &= -\mathrm{L_1} sin(q_1)\dot{q}_1 - \mathrm{L_2} sin(q_1+q_2)(\dot{q}_1 + \dot{q}_2)\\
\dot{\mathrm{y}}_2 &= \mathrm{L_1} cos(q_1)\dot{q}_1 + \mathrm{L_2} cos(q_1+q_2)(\dot{q}_1 + \dot{q}_2)\\
\end{align*}

<!-- cell 19 md -->
Therefore its kinetic energy is

\begin{align*}
K_2 &= \frac{1}{2} m_1 (\dot{\mathrm{x}}_2^2 + \dot{\mathrm{y}}_2^2) \\
&= \frac{1}{2}m_2 \left( \left( L_1^2 +2L_1L_2cos(q_2) + L_2^2 \right)\dot{q}_1^2 + 2\left(L_2^2 + L_1L_2cos(q_2)\right)\dot{q}_1\dot{q}_2 + m_2\mathrm{L}_1^2\dot{q}_2^2 \right)    
\end{align*}

<!-- cell 20 md -->
Now let us write the potential energies of the two masses

\begin{align*}
P_1 &= m_1 g \mathrm{L_1} sin(q_1) \\
P_2 &= m_2 g \left(\mathrm{L_1} sin(q_1) + \mathrm{L_2} sin(q_1+q_2)\right)\\
\end{align*}

<!-- cell 21 md -->
We can now write the Lagrangian as

\begin{align*}
\mathcal{L} = K_1 - P_1 + K_2 - P_2
\end{align*}

<!-- cell 22 md -->
The equations of motion for the two joints can be computed as

\begin{align*}
Q_1 = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} \left( \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}\left(\textbf{q}, \dot{\textbf{q}}\right)}{\partial \dot{q}_1} \right) - \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}\left(\textbf{q}, \dot{\textbf{q}}\right)}{\partial q_1} \\
Q_2 = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} \left( \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}\left(\textbf{q}, \dot{\textbf{q}}\right)}{\partial \dot{q}_2} \right) - \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}\left(\textbf{q}, \dot{\textbf{q}}\right)}{\partial q_2}
\end{align*}


<!-- cell 23 md -->
### General equations of motion

<!-- cell 24 md -->
The equations of motion in the two link example above, can be rewritten in matrix form as

\begin{align*}
\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{q})
\end{align*}

<!-- cell 25 md -->
where 

\begin{align*}
\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q}) = 
\begin{bmatrix}
m_1 \mathrm{L_1}^2 + m_2 (\mathrm{L_1}^2 + 2\mathrm{L_1} \mathrm{L_2} cos⁡(q_2 )+ \mathrm{L_2}^2 ) & m_2 (\mathrm{L_1} \mathrm{L_2}  cos⁡(q_2)+\mathrm{L_2}^2 ) \\
m_2 (\mathrm{L_1} \mathrm{L_2}  cos⁡(q_2)+ \mathrm{L_2}^2 ) & m_2 \mathrm{L_2}^2
\end{bmatrix}
\end{align*}

\begin{align*}
\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) = 
\begin{bmatrix}
−m_2 \mathrm{L_1} \mathrm{L_2}  sin⁡(q_2) (2\dot{q}_1 \dot{q}_2+ \dot{q}_2^2 ) \\
m_2 \mathrm{L_1} \mathrm{L_2} \dot{q}_1^2 sin(q_2)
\end{bmatrix}
\end{align*}

\begin{align*}
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{q}) = 
\begin{bmatrix}
(m_1 + m_2) \mathrm{L_1} g cos(q_1) + m_2 g \mathrm{L_2} cos(q_1 + q_2) \\
m_2 g \mathrm{L_2} cos(q_1 + q_2)
\end{bmatrix}
\end{align*}

<!-- cell 26 md -->
In a more general case, the equations of motion include other terms like friction, and external forces from contact. These can all be captured as 

\begin{align*}
\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{q}) + \mathbf{f}(\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \textbf{J}^\top(\mathbf{q})\mathbf{w}
\end{align*}

Here $\mathbf{M}$ is the mass matrix or the Joint-space inertia matrix.  
$\mathbf{C}$ captures the centripetal and coriolis terms that arise from coupling of velocities.  
$\mathbf{g}$ accounts for the potential energy (gravity, spring force, etc).  
$\mathbf{f}$ accounts for frictional forces.  
$\mathbf{w}$ is the external force.

## Newton-Euler formulation

The Newton-Euler formulation is one of force and moment balance. An in-depth explanation of the method can be found in Craig's Intoduction to Robotics textbook. Here we summarise a numerical approach of iteratively solving for joint torques using the robotics toolbox. Also refer to chapter 9 of Peter Corke's Robotics Vision and Control textbook.

<!-- cell 28 code -->
```python
# consider the PUMA 560 robot arm
robot = rtb.models.DH.Puma560()

# we start with the robot in a specific configuration, with known joint angles, velocity and acceleration
zeros = np.zeros((6,)) # zero vector

# we can use the rne method on the robot to joint torques to achieve the desired joint motion
Q = robot.rne(robot.qn, zeros, zeros) # joint torques to hold the robot in the default configuration with zero velocity and acceleration
print("The joint torques in nominal configuration with zero velocity and acceleration are: \n", Q)
# This is the torque required to overcome the effects of gravity

# we can override the gravity vector and set it to zero
print("Gravity vector is", robot.gravity)
Q_nogravity = robot.rne(robot.qn, zeros, zeros, gravity=[0, 0, 0])
print("The joint torques in nominal configuration with zero velocity and acceleration and no gravity are:\n" ,Q_nogravity)

# we can specify a velocity to the first joint, say 1 rad/s
Q_velocity = robot.rne(robot.qn, [1, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0], zeros, gravity=[0, 0, 0])
print("The joint torques in nominal configuration with first joint having a velocity of 1 rad/s and no gravity:\n", Q_velocity)

```

<!-- cell 29 code -->
```python
# To observe the torques required to overcome gravity, we can also use the more convenient method, gravload
Q_gravity = robot.gravload(robot.qn) # same as Q = robot.rne(robot.qn, zeros, zeros)
print("The joint torques in nominal configuration with zero velocity and acceleration are: \n", Q_gravity)

# The mass matrix of the robot can be computed using the method, inertia
M = robot.inertia(robot.qn)
print("The mass matrix of the robot in the nominal configuration is: \n", M)

# The Coriolis matrix of the robot can be computed using the method, coriolis
C = robot.coriolis(robot.qn, qd=[0, 0, 1, 0, 0, 0]) # setting the velocity of the elbow joint to 1 rad/s
print("The Coriolis matrix of the robot in the nominal configuration is: \n", C)

# The friction torques can be computed using the method, friction
f = robot.friction([1, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0]) # setting the velocity of the first joint to 1 rad/s
print("The friction torques of the robot in the nominal configuration is: \n", f)

```

## Forward Dynamics
The forward dynamics problem is one in which we are interested in determining the motion of the robot, when given the torques and forces on the joints. From the general equation of motion that we discussed above, we can write

\begin{align*}
\ddot{\mathbf{q}} = \mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})^{-1} \left( \mathbf{Q} - \mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) - \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{q}) - \mathbf{f}(\dot{\mathbf{q}}) - \textbf{J}^\top(\mathbf{q})\mathbf{w} \right) 
\end{align*}



<!-- cell 31 md -->
The mass matrix, being square and positive definite, is always invertible. The above function can be computed using the robotics toolbox

<!-- cell 32 code -->
```python
qdd =  robot.accel(robot.qn, [1, 1, 0, 0, 0, 0], [10,20,30,40,50,60]) # arguments are joint angles, joint velocities and joint torques
print("The joint accelerations are \n", qdd)
```
