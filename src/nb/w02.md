<!-- cell 4 md -->
### What is forward kinematics?

Forward kinematics gives the pose of the end effector of a robot arm when the coordinates of all the joints are provided. The pose of the end effector is given with respect to a known reference frame. The term *joint coordinate* is here used to represent both the angular position (in the case of a rotary joint) and linear position (in the case of a linear joint).

<!-- cell 5 md -->
Let $\textbf{q} = \begin{Bmatrix} q_1 & q_2 & \ldots & q_n \end{Bmatrix}^\top$ be a column vector representing the joint coordinates of a manipulator with $n$ joints.  
Let $\xi_n$ be the pose of the end effector with respect to the base frame of the robot. This relative pose could be represented as a homogenous transformation matrix $^0\textbf{T}_n$, a twist $\textbf{S}$, a vector-quaternion pair ($\textbf{t}, \mathring{q}$) or in some other form.  

Forward kinematics can be thought of as a mapping from joint coordinates to the end effector pose, i.e. $\xi_n =$ 𝒦$(\textbf{q})$.

<!-- cell 6 md -->
### Example: Planar RRR Manipulator
Consider the planar RRR manipulator shown below. The manipulator has three revolute joints, each with a joint angle $q_i$. The lengths of the links are L1, L2 and L3. The end effector E is at the tip of the last link.
<center>
    <figure>
        <img src="2_Figures\planar_RRR.png"
            width="30%">
    </figure>
</center>

The forward kinematics problem is to determine the pose of the end effector E with respect to the base frame O. In this case, we are interested in the x and y coordinates of E with respect to the base frame O, and the angle $q_E$ that the end effector makes with the x-axis. In this simple case, the problem can be solved using trigonometry.  

You can verify that the forward kinematics of this manipulator is given by:

<!-- cell 7 md -->
\begin{align*}
x = L_1 \cos(q_1) + L_2 \cos(q_1 + q_2) + L_3 \cos(q_1 + q_2 + q_3) \\
y = L_1 \sin(q_1) + L_2 \sin(q_1 + q_2) + L_3 \sin(q_1 + q_2 + q_3) \\
q_E = q_1 + q_2 + q_3
\end{align*}

<!-- cell 8 md -->
In this example, the pose of the end effector E, given by the vector $\begin{Bmatrix} x & y & q_E \end{Bmatrix}^\top$ is a function of the joint coordinates $\begin{Bmatrix} q_1 & q_2 & q_3 \end{Bmatrix}^\top$. This function also includes the manipulator's geometry in terms of the link lengths $L_1$, $L_2$ and $L_3$.

### Elementary Transform Sequence
In the case of more complex manipulators like a 7DOF robot arm, the forward kinematics mapping can be obtained using a systematic approach like the [Elementary Transform Sequence](https://petercorke.com/doc/simple_systematic.pdf). As the name suggests, this method makes use of elementary transformations, represented in this text as $\mathbf{E}_i$. Each elementary transform is either a rotation or a translation and is one of the following.

<!-- cell 11 md -->
\begin{align*}
\bf{E}_i =
    \left\{
        \begin{matrix}
            \bf{T}_{t_{x}}(\eta_i) \\
            \bf{T}_{t_{y}}(\eta_i) \\
            \bf{T}_{t_{z}}(\eta_i) \\
            \bf{T}_{R_{x}}(\eta_i) \\
            \bf{T}_{R_{y}}(\eta_i) \\
            \bf{T}_{R_{z}}(\eta_i)
        \end{matrix}
    \right.
\end{align*}

<!-- cell 12 md -->
where the parameter $\eta_i$ is either a constant $c_i$ (translational offset or fixed rotation) or a joint variable $q_j(t)$ that changes with time.  

<!-- cell 13 md -->
\begin{align*}
\eta_i =
    \left\{
        \begin{matrix}
            c_i \\
            q_j(t)
        \end{matrix}
    \right.
\end{align*}

<!-- cell 14 md -->
Like mentioned before, the joint variable represents either a joint angle or a joint translation depending on the type of the joint it refers to.

<!-- cell 15 md -->
\begin{align*}
q_j(t) =
    \left\{
        \begin{matrix}
            \theta_j(t) \quad \textrm{for a revolute joint}\\
            d_j(t) \quad \textrm{for a prismatic joint}
        \end{matrix}
    \right.
\end{align*}

<!-- cell 16 md -->
The key idea in this method is to walk along the manipulator from the base to the end effector and keep track of all the elementary transforms encountered. The pose of the end effector with respect to the base frame is then obtained by multiplying all the elementary transforms in the order they were encountered.

<!-- cell 20 md -->
The sequence of elementary transforms to reach the end effector from the base frame is

<!-- cell 21 md -->
\begin{align*}
^\mathrm{B}\bf{T}_\mathrm{E} = \bf{T}_{t_{z}}(\mathrm{L_0}) 
\bf{T}_{R_{z}}(\mathrm{\theta_1}) 
\bf{T}_{t_{x}}(\mathrm{L_1}) 
\bf{T}_{R_{x}}(\mathrm{\theta_2}) 
\bf{T}_{t_{z}}(\mathrm{-L_2}) 
\bf{T}_{R_{y}}(\mathrm{\pi})
\end{align*}

<!-- cell 22 md -->
This sequence is visually represented in the following animation.

<center>
    <figure>`
        <img src="2_Figures\furuta_pendulum_ETS_3.gif"
            width="40%">
    </figure>
</center>

<!-- cell 23 md -->
This is the pose of the end effector with respect to the base frame and gives the non-linear mapping from the joint angles $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ to the pose of the end effector. Relative poses of other links can also be obtained as subsets of the above sequece of transforms. Assigning reference frames to each link (at the side closer to the base), these relative poses are

<!-- cell 24 md -->
\begin{align*}
^\mathrm{B}\bf{T}_\mathrm{1} = \bf{T}_{t_{z}}(\mathrm{L_0}) \bf{T}_{R_{z}}(\mathrm{\theta_1}) \\
^\mathrm{1}\bf{T}_\mathrm{2} = \bf{T}_{t_{x}}(\mathrm{L_1}) \bf{T}_{R_{x}}(\mathrm{\theta_2}) \\
^\mathrm{2}\bf{T}_\mathrm{E} = \bf{T}_{t_{z}}(\mathrm{-L_2}) \bf{T}_{R_{y}}(\mathrm{\pi})
\end{align*}

# Take the case of the Furuta Pendulum
# Say we are given the link lengths as L0 = 50 cm, L1 = 40 cm, L2 = 30 cm

E1 = rtb.ET.tz(0.50)
E2 = rtb.ET.Rz()
E3 = rtb.ET.tx(0.40)

# We can compose the ET to obtain an ETS in a number of ways

# Option 1: Use the * operator between ETs
ets1 = E1 * E2 * E3

# Option 2 use the ETS constructor and pass in a list of ETs
ets2 = rtb.ETS([E1, E2, E3])

# Option 3: a combiation of options 1 and 2
ets3 = ets2 * E4
ets4 = ets2 * rtb.ETS([E4, E5])

print(ets2)
print(ets3)
print(ets4)

We now have all the components to compute the forward kinematics.

<!-- cell 39 code -->
```python
# first, let us assign values for the joint coordinates at which we wish to compute the pose of the end effector.
q = [np.pi/3, np.pi/4]

# create a numpy array to store the transformation matrix representing the forward kinematics
fk = np.eye(4)

# loop through the ETs in the ETS
for et in ets_furuta:
    if et.isjoint: # if the ET is a joint (has a variable joint parameter)
        # use the q array to assign a value to the joint cooridinate
        fk = fk @ et.A(q[et.jindex]) # the @ operator is used to multiply the transformation matrices
    else:
        # if the ET is not a joint, use the .A() method to compute the transformation
        fk = fk @ et.A()

# create a spatialmath SE3 object from the resulting transformation matrix
sm_fk = sm.SE3(fk)
print(sm_fk)
```

### Alternative ways to obtain forward kinematics

Over the years, many approaches to obtaining the forwad kinematics of serial link manipulators have been proposed. One of the more popular methods is the Denavit-Hartenberg system. While in the ETS method, there is flexibility in assigning frames (the joint axis can be either the local x, y or z axis), in the D-H method, this flexibility is sacrificed in favour of a more systematic approach.

There are many variations of the D-H method. This method will not be discussed here. Refer to [Chapter 3: Manipulator Kinematics from Craig's book](https://qut.primo.exlibrisgroup.com/permalink/61QUT_INST/18s5r5e/alma991010126086304001) for one flavour of the D-H method. def ets_general(ets_arr):
    """
    This function takes an array of elementary transformations and returns the corresponding elementary transformation sequence (ETS).
    
    Parameters:
    ets_arr (list): A list of elementary transformations (ETs).
    
    Returns:
    rtb.ETS: The resulting elementary transformation sequence (ETS).
    """
    ets = rtb.ETS(ets_arr)
    return ets

robot = rtb.models.DH.Puma560()

num_samples = 5000

workspace_points = np.zeros((num_samples, 3))

    # Sample random joint angles between min and max limits for each joint
    q_random = np.random.uniform(robot.qlim[0], robot.qlim[1])

    T = robot.fkine(q_random)
